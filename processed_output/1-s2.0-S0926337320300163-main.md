In this study, a nanostructured ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ material was synthesized using a simple co-precipitation technique and investigated as a catalyst for phenol degradation via peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation. The structural and morphological properties of the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst were characterized using XRD, XPS, FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, TGA, SEM, and TEM. The results indicated that the prepared catalyst exhibited a pure crystalline phase with a rod-like structure. The ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst exhibited high activity towards PMS activation, resulting in complete degradation of phenol within the first $8 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ at room temperature. The degradation reaction was found to follow pseudo-first-order kinetics with a low activation energy of $2 3 . 7 4 \mathrm { ~ k J / m o l }$ . The degradation followed a catalytic surface-mediated electron transfer route as confirmed using ESR measurements. The effects of several experimental parameters on phenol degradation were investigated, including the catalyst loading, initial phenol concentration, PMS dosage, reaction temperature, and $\mathrm { p H } .$ . The regeneration and reusability of the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst were also examined for 5 consecutive cycles. This study puts forward ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ as a heterogeneous catalyst for the complete removal of persistent organic pollutants via PMS activation, indicating a favorable application in wastewater treatment.

# 1. Introduction

The wastewater produced during industrial processes typically contains organic and inorganic pollutants that are environmentally persistent and cannot be removed by conventional methods [1,2]. Some of these pollutants are dyes, pesticides and organic solvents which may contain aromatic compounds, cyanides, ammonia, sulfides, and phenols [3–5]. Phenols are often used in agriculture and in general disinfection. Phenol is a persistent organic pollutant and is listed as a class 2 water hazard [6]. The environmental protection agency (EPA) restricted its concentration to less than $1 \ \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ in the discharged inland water [7] and to $2 \ \mathrm { m g / L }$ in drinking water [8]. Phenol’s poor biodegradability demands a tertiary treatment as the conventional primary and secondary processes are not efficient [9–11]. Techniques like membrane technology, electrochemical process and chemical oxidation are often adopted in wastewater treatment [12–14]. Recent studies have indicated an increase in the prospect of advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) focused on peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation owing to the pH flexibility, their redox potential and great oxidation ability against common environmental contaminants such as pharmaceuticals, dyes, phenol, and perfluorinated compounds. The use of peroxomonosulfate (PMS) as an oxidant has attracted a lot of attention due to the presence of different reactive oxygen species produced during its activation process [15,16].

Various physicochemical activation strategies of PMS were carried out using ultraviolet, metal oxides, ultrasound, transition metals or heat [17]. Among these approaches, the activation via metal oxides as heterogeneous catalysts has drawn much attention. [15,17,18]. Even though transition metals, as homogeneous catalysts, showed high activation of PMS, their high solubility and toxicity made them less favorable. Conversely, heterogeneous catalysts are reusable, easy to recover and reduce the need for chemical reagents [9,17–19]. The reaction pathway is in coherence with those of transition metals homogenous catalysts activation strategies wherein the conduction band (generated) electrons selectively cleave the peroxide bond to generate $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ .

Persulfate activation via non radical mechanism, employing transition metal-based mediators, mainly proceed through two hypotheses which are singlet oxygenation and metal-mediated electron transfer [20–22]. Despite that, literature reports indicate that the activity of the hydroxyl and sulphate radicals could be hindered by the presence of background interferences caused by the organic compounds and anions $( \mathbf { C l } ^ { - }$ , ${ \mathrm { H C O } } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ , natural organic matter… etc.) presented in the system [23], owing to the radical trapping effect, hence limiting their application to real water treatment [24]. On the other hand, the non-radical mechanism has been explored widely due to the high resistance of the reactive oxygen species (single oxygen, $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } )$ to some of the background interferences [25]. Besides, $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ has demonstrated an efficient degradation of pharmaceutical contaminants in real wastewater [26].

$\mathrm { M } { \cdot } \mathrm { V O } _ { 4 }$ materials are environmentally abundant oxides with remarkable physical and chemical properties which enabled their applications in batteries, semiconductors, and catalysis [27]. Combining vanadate with rare earth metals such as Ce, La and $\mathrm { P r }$ showed a great enhancement in their electrochemical properties, thermal stability, surface area, and magnetic properties [28]. The naturally occurring cerium vanadate $\mathrm { ( C e V O _ { 4 , } ) }$ ) mineral wakefieldite exhibits a tetragonal zircon-type structure (space group 141/amd) [29]. Due to it’s optical, electrical, magnetic and catalytic properties [30–32], ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ often used as a catalyst, superhydrophobic material, gas sensor, counter electrode, and supercapacitor [33]. There are several reports about its efficiency for degrading organic pollutants such as organic dyes [34] and for the oxidative dehydrogenation of propane [35].

In the present work, ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ was prepared using a simple one-pot coprecipitation method. The prepared ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles were characterized using XRD, FTIR, Raman, TGA, XPS, SEM-EDX and TEM. ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst was used to activate PMS for phenol degradation. The reaction operating conditions were optimized by investigating the effect of the catalyst loading, phenol initial concentration, PMS dosage, reaction temperature, and pH. The mechanism of the reaction was proposed by identifying the intermediate species in the reaction.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Catalysts preparation

Nanostructured ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ particles were prepared by using a simple one-pot co-precipitation method employing high purity $( 9 9 . 9 ~ \ \% )$ cerium nitrate $[ \mathsf { C e } ( \mathsf { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 } . 6 \mathsf { H } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } ]$ and ammonium metavanadate $\mathrm { ( N H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { V O } _ { 3 } \mathrm { ) }$ as obtained from Sigma Aldrich. For a typical synthesis procedure, $2 0 \ \mathrm { m m o l }$ of ammonium metavanadate and $2 0 \ \mathrm { m m o l }$ of Ce $( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } 0$ were dissolved separately in $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of de-ionized (DI) water. The ammonium metavanadate solution was warmed tilled $\mathrm { N H _ { 4 } V O _ { 3 } }$ was completely dissolved. Then the cerium nitrate solution was added to the $\mathrm { N H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { V O } _ { 3 }$ solution under continuous stirring. Ammonia solution was used to maintain the pH of the solution at 9, till a brown cloudy precipitate was formed. The reaction was continued at ${ 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } }$ with stirring for two hours. The obtained brown precipitate was separated by centrifuge and washed with DI water and ethanol. The product, ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst, was then dried at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in an oven for two hours.

# 2.3. The degradation of phenol

In a typical reaction, a predetermined amount of PMS was dissolved in a $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ solution of a $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { p p m }$ phenol solution in a $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ beaker. Then, the catalyst was added to the reaction mixture with continuous stirring at room temperature. Samples were withdrawn and filtered using $0 . 2 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ nylon membrane filters at specific time intervals. The phenol concentration in the filtrate was determined using a Shimadzu HPLC with a C18 column (Restek, $4 . 6 \times 1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m m } )$ and a UV detector that was set at $2 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ . The mobile phase used in the analysis contained a mixture of methanol $( 3 5 ~ \% )$ , water $( 6 4 ~ \% )$ , and acetic acid $( 1 ~ \% )$ . Phenol degradation was monitored and the intermediates of the degradation process were observed using the same method [39]. The percentage of phenol removal was calculated by the equation below:

$$
{ \mathrm { R e m o v a l } } \% = { \frac { \left( { \bf C } \circ - { \bf \sigma } { \bf C } _ { t } \right) } { { \bf C } \circ } } \times 1 0 0 
$$

where $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and $\mathrm { C _ { t } }$ are the initial and residual concentrations of phenol, respectively, at a specific time t. The degradation reactions of phenol were modeled using the following first-order kinetic equation:

$$
l n \frac { C _ { t } } { C _ { o } } = - k t
$$

where $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and $\mathrm { C _ { t } }$ are the initial and residual concentrations of phenol, respectively, at a specific time t. k is the rate constant of the degradation reaction which can be calculated from the slope of $\ln ( \mathrm { C _ { t } / C _ { o } ) }$ versus time. The effect of different reaction parameters on ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalytic activity was investigated, including catalyst loading, PMS dosage, initial phenol concentration, reaction temperature, and pH.

The activation energy of the degradation reaction was determined using the Arrhenius equation:

$$
k = A e ^ { \frac { - E _ { a } } { R T } }
$$

where $\mathbf { k }$ is the reaction rate constant, A is a pre-exponential factor, $\mathrm { E _ { a } }$ is the reaction activation energy, R is the gas constant and $\mathrm { T }$ is the absolute temperature.

The reaction pathway of the phenol degradation was evaluated using an ESR spectrometer (Bruker EMX X-Band Spectrometer, United States). The active species involved in the reaction were determined by spin trapping using DMPO (5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide) for˙ ${ \cal O } ^ { 2 - }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - - }$ or ˙OH and TEMP (2,2,6,6-Tetramethyl-4-piperidone) $\mathrm { f o r } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ . The reaction intermediates were identified by LCeMS (ACQUITY UPLC HClass System from Waters Company) equipped with Bruker MS, using photodiode arrays and -ESI negative and a SB-C18 column. The stability of the catalyst was further determined by quantifying the metal leaching at the end of the reaction using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrophotometer (ICP-OES, Perkin Elmer).

The efficiency of PMS in AOPs depends on the initial pH of the solution. Therefore, the effect of different pH values on phenol degradation was investigated. The results are presented in Fig. 7(b) which shows that as the pH increased from 3 to 12, the phenol removal decreased indicating that the degradation reaction favors acidic conditions. # 3.4. Degradation efficiency of different pollutants

To investigate the applicability of ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ and PMS system for the catalytic degradation of different organic pollutants, four different pollutants were also investigated. The degradation of each of resorcinol, acrylamide, methyl violet (6B) and methyl orange was tested under the same experimental conditions. Fig. 8 (b) shows that ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ /PMS system is not phenol oriented and it successfully degraded all the above organic pollutants.

The regeneration and reusability are important criteria for practical applications of a heterogeneous catalyst. The performance of the regenerated ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles was tested for 5 consecutive cycles at the same reaction parameters. After each cycle, the catalyst was recovered by filtration followed by washing with DI water. The obtained results are presented in Fig. 9 which shows that, even after 5 consecutive regeneration cycles, the reused catalyst exhibited a high catalytic activity as indicated by complete degradation of phenol within 60 min. These results further specify the good repeatability and robustness of the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst. In addition to that, there was no significant leaching of either Ce or V $_ { \cdot < 5 }$ ppm using ICP-OES) from the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst during the catalytic reaction.

Fig. S2 compares the Raman, XRD, FT-IR and Fig. S3 compares SEM/EDX data for ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ before and after the reaction. The recovered catalyst was washed and dried prior to the analysis. The obtained results suggest no notable changes in the composition and morphology of the catalyst after the reaction. The EDX data agrees with the ICP-OES results which confirm the stability of ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst. On the other hand, the FT-IR showed an increase in the intensity of $\mathrm { O - H }$ peak (3400 $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } .$ ) which could be due to the adsorption of phenol onto the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ surface. The appearance of $\scriptstyle { \mathrm { C } } = { \mathrm { C } }$ bond around $1 6 3 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ in addition to the shift in $\mathrm { O H - }$ bond are both a clear indication of the non-radical pathway via phenol adsorption on the catalyst surface. Similar observations were previously reported in the literature [68].

# 3.5. Identification of reactive species and plausible mechanism

Phenol degradation via PMS activation by heterogeneous catalysts has been reported previously by various research groups [69–71], which followed different mechanistic pathways as (a) radical-mediated $\mathrm { ( } \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \cdot \mathrm { ~ - ~ } }$ and % OH radicals) catalytic PMS decomposition [15,72–74] (b) non-radical mediated pathway where PMS activation is carried out by electrons from the organic substrate via an electron transfer mediator [69]. The quenching experiments using the scavenging agents for these radicals can be employed as a typical test to identify the reactive species in the PMS activation process. Tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) is employed as a characteristic scavenger for OH radicals $( \mathrm { k } = 3 . 8 – 7 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ , whereas methanol (MeOH) is exploited for scavenging both $S 0 _ { 4 } \mathrm { ~ \ddot { ~ } ( k ~ = ~ }$ $3 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ as well as OH radicals $( \mathbf { k _ { \alpha } } = 9 . 7 ~ \times ~ 1 0 ^ { 8 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ~ ^ { - 1 } )$ [70]. ${ \mathrm { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ is reported as an efficient quencher for % OH $( { \bf k } = 1 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 0 }$ $\mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } \textbf { \em s } ^ { - 1 } )$ , $S O _ { 4 } \cdot -$ $( \mathbf { k } \ = \ 2 . 5 1 \ \times \ 1 0 ^ { 9 } \ \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } \ s \ ^ { - 1 } )$ and non-radical oxidation process $^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } \left( \mathrm { k } = 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { ~ s ~ } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ [75].

Referring to Fig. 4, negligible phenol degradation was achieved when the catalyst was removed from the reaction system which shows that the reaction is completely derived by the catalyst. The proposed reaction pathway was further investigated by scavenging the active species and quenching the reaction using ${ \mathrm { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ , MeOH and TBA. The results are depicted in Fig. 10(b) which clearly indicates that TBA and MeOH had almost no inhibition effect on phenol degradation, whereas a considerable decrease in the phenol degradation was observed in the presence of $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ . These results confirm that the reaction proceeds via a non-radical path. Compared to the initial degradation pathway, the oxidation rate of phenol at a given condition decreased by $9 8 ~ \%$ on the addition of $1 0 \mathrm { m M N a N } _ { 3 }$ and nearly by $2 0 \%$ on the addition of $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ MeOH and $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ TBA, respectively. Furthermore, upon the addition of excess $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 } ,$ , a change in degradation level was perceived linear to the concentration of the added scavenger. The degradation efficiency decreased from the original $1 0 0 ~ \%$ at $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ to 81, 54 and $2 8 \%$ on the addition of $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ , 5 mM and $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ of $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ , respectively, as evident from Fig. 10(a). This indicates that the reaction is mainly driven by the non-radical pathway via singlet oxygen.

To further confirm the non-radical reaction pathway of the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 } /$ PMS system, ESR measurements were performed using spin trapping agents DMPO to determine $\mathbf { \partial } ^ { \cdot _ { 0 } 2 ^ { - } }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - - }$ or ˙OH as well as TEMP for $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . The results are presented in Fig. 11 which shows no signal of DMPO−OH and $\mathrm { D M P O - } S 0 _ { 4 }$ adduct observed in the case of DMPO, DMPO/PMS and DMPO/PMS- ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ systems indicating that $\mathbf { \partial } ^ { 2 - }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - - }$ or ˙OH radicals are not generated in the $\mathrm { C e V O _ { 4 } / P M S }$ system. Although, in the case of TEMP/PMS- $\mathrm { C e V O } _ { 4 }$ system, a characteristic triplet signal with a relative intensity of 1:1:1 of TEMPO adduct is observed and can be assigned to $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ mediated oxidation of TEMP [76]. The above results are in concurrence with the radical quenching experiments, which further confirm the presence of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ as the most reactive species in the activation of PMS using ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 } ,$ , proposing a non-radical pathway as a prevailing catalytic reaction for phenol degradation [77].

The detection of the reaction intermediates was performed by HPLC throughout the catalytic process by identifying the intermediate products that were formed during the degradation of phenol. Fig. 11(b), depicts the process of the degradation, where the phenol peak at a retention time (RT) of $4 . 5 \mathrm { m i n }$ decreases with increasing the reaction time and completely disappears within $8 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of the reaction time. A new peak appeared at a RT 2.5 min due to a reaction intermediate within the first $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of catalytic reaction along with a shoulder at $2 . 1 9 \mathrm { m i n }$ and increased in intensity till $3 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of the reaction time, after which the peak intensity decreased indicating the degradation of the reaction intermediate. By increasing the reaction time, three new peaks appeared at RT 1.5, 1.2 and $0 . 6 8 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , indicating the presence of secondary degraded products. The peaks were identified as p-benzoquinone, hydroquinone, maleic acid, malonic acid, and acetic acid, respectively, distinguished by RT values of their standard solutions.

To further confirm the existence of these intermediates in the degradation process, the samples were analyzed by LC–MS and the results are depicted in Fig. S4.

Based on the results obtained from the scavenging studies and the intermediate identification, the degradation mechanism for phenol by PMS activation follows a non-radical pathway via singlet oxygen generation and can be schematically represented in Fig. 12 and Fig. 13. During PMS activation by ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ the first step is the adsorption of PMS on to the surface of ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ nanocatalysts and subsequent charge transfer happens between the catalysts and PMS molecules resulting in the generation of singlet oxygen. The singlet oxygen is then is successively desorbed from the surface of the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles into the aqueous solution containing phenol for degrading phenol. Similar observations are being reported by other groups [78–80]. In the process of PMS activation, ${ \mathsf { C e } } ^ { 3 + }$ in ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ is oxidized to ${ \mathsf { C e } } ^ { 4 + }$ by the defect sites in the crystal lattice of the ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ and $\mathrm { V } ^ { 5 + }$ is reduced to $\mathrm { V } ^ { 4 + }$ by the electrons present simultaneously. Aforementioned, a redox reaction can rapidly occur between $\nabla ^ { 4 + }$ with strong reducing capacity and ${ \mathsf { C e } } ^ { 4 + }$ with strong oxidizing ability, generating the original $\mathsf { V } ^ { 5 + }$ and $\mathsf { C e } ^ { 3 + }$ , respectively. The formation of these types of redox reaction centers in ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ is widely studied in-depth and reported in the literature previously [49,81–83]. The generation of oxygen vacancies also favors the interfacial electron transfer mechanism, as reported earlier by other research groups [80,84,85]. Henceforth the redox centers and oxygen defects present at the catalyst interface play a major role in the generation of singlet oxygen.

# 4. Conclusions

This study demonstrates the high catalytic performance of ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst towards phenol degradation via PMS activation. The ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles, synthesized through a simple one-pot co-precipitation method, exhibited good crystallinity and displayed a rod-like structure. The catalytic degradation process was found to depend on various operating parameters such as the initial concentration of phenol, loading of the catalyst, PMS dosage, pH and reaction temperature. Detailed kinetic studies revealed that the catalytic degradation of phenol fitted with the pseudo-first-order kinetics, with an $\mathrm { { E _ { a } } = 2 3 . 7 4 \ k J / m o l } { }$ . The catalytic degradation mechanism and the species involved were further investigated by quenching the radicals using scavengers like TBA, MeOH, and ${ \mathrm { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ , which showed that the degradation followed a nonradical pathway as confirmed by the ESR results. The regeneration and reusability experiments of the catalyst over 5 consecutive cycles indicated the good reusability of the catalyst. The catalytic system was efficient at removing a wide range of organic pollutants indicating that the catalytic performance of the $\mathrm { C e V O _ { 4 } / P M S }$ system was not phenol dependent. The reaction intermediates were investigated using LC–MS and confirmed the presence of $\mathbf { p }$ -benzoquinone, hydroquinone, maleic acid, malonic acid, and acetic acid during the phenol degradation process. Combining a simple synthesis method with excellent catalytic ${ \mathrm { C e V O } } _ { 4 }$ properties is a suitable cost-effective, environmentally friendly technique that can be employed in water treatment applications.
