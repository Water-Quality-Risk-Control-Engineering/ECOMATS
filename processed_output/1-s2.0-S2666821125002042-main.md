In this study, a novel catalyst was synthesized from hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA), boric acid, and nickel for the efficient removal of bisphenol A (BPA) via peroxydisulfate (PDS) activation. The optimized composite, prepared with a molar ratio of 1:5:0.05 (HMTA: boric acid: nickel), was calcinated under optimized conditions, heated to $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at a ramping rate of $2 . 1 8 ~ \mathrm { ^ { \circ } C / m i n }$ over $^ { 4 } \mathrm { h }$ , and held for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . XRD analysis confirmed the coexistence of crystalline NiO and amorphous $\mathbf { B } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 3 }$ phases within a porous carbon framework. The removal process was optimized using response surface methodology (RSM) and Box-Behnken design (BBD), with maximum BPA removal efficiencies of $9 9 . 9 \%$ in groundwater and $9 8 . 9 \%$ in treated wastewater at an initial BPA concentration of $0 . 5 \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ achieved under optimal conditions $_ { ( 0 . 4 8 \ g / \mathrm { L } }$ of catalyst, $0 . 8 5 \mathrm { m M }$ of PDS, pH of 6.97, and $6 . 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction time). Importantly, a high total organic carbon (TOC) removal of over $9 8 ~ \%$ was obtained, confirming nearly complete mineralization. Quenching experiments demonstrated that singlet oxygen was the dominant reactive species, with GC–MS analysis of intermediates corroborating a non-radical degradation pathway. ICP-OES analysis confirmed negligible metal leaching $( < 0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { m g / L } )$ , highlighting the catalyst’s stability. The catalyst’s performance remained unhindered by common coexisting ions and maintained over $9 5 \%$ efficiency for five consecutive cycles, offering a highly efficient, mineralizing, and reusable solution for BPA removal in aquatic environments.

# 1. Introduction

The increasing discharge of organic pollutants into water resources is a pressing global challenge, threatening both ecological integrity and public health. Among these pollutants, bisphenol A (BPA), an endocrinedisrupting compound found in plastics and epoxy resins, is of particular concern due to its widespread use and potential for negative health effects, even at low concentrations [1,2]. BPA’s persistence in the environment and its ability to interfere with hormonal systems highlight the urgent need for efficient removal strategies [3,4]. Conventional wastewater treatment plants often struggle to effectively remove emerging contaminants, necessitating the development of innovative and sustainable remediation technologies [5,6].

Several methods have been developed for the removal of bisphenol A (BPA), including enzymatic degradation, microbial degradation, fungal degradation, adsorption, membrane filtration, electrochemical processes, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) and photocatalysis.

However, these methods have various limitations. Enzymatic degradation often involves high costs and limited enzyme stability. Microbial and fungal degradation can be slow and may lead to incomplete removal or secondary pollution. Adsorption and membrane filtration are affected by saturation and require frequent regeneration, reducing their longterm efficiency [7]. Electrochemical processes may be less effective in the presence of certain ions, and photocatalysis can produce harmful by-products and needs specific conditions to work properly [8]. Unlike conventional methods, AOPs involve the in-situ generation of highly reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals $( \bullet \mathrm { O H } )$ , sulfate radicals $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet } )$ and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ , which can effectively oxidize and mineralize organic contaminants [9,10]. These processes offer several advantages, including rapid and complete pollutant removal, operating across a wide pH range, and significant reduction in water toxicity through full mineralization [11,12].

Among the various AOPs, peroxydisulfate (PDS) activation has garnered significant attention due to its high oxidation potential and versatility in generating both sulfate and hydroxyl radicals [13,14]. Moreover, non-radical pathways involving singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ have also been recognized as important mechanisms in PDS-based AOPs, offering alternative and more selective oxidation routes [15,16]. PDS can be activated through various methods. Heat is a common method for activating PDS, as it efficiently generates sulfate radicals. However, the continuous energy input required can limit its practical application [17]. UV activation application is limited by high energy consumption, complex reactor design, and the potential formation of toxic by-products [18,19]. Additionally, its efficiency can be reduced in the presence of scavenging substances or complex wastewater matrices [20]. Heterogeneous catalysts are highly effective for PDS activation, offering advantages such as lower energy requirements and the ability to utilize visible light. Compared to heat and UV methods, they demonstrate high stability and reusability, coupled with enhanced oxidation efficiency and selective degradation capabilities [21–23].

Boron oxide $\mathbf { ( B _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } ) }$ plays a distinct and crucial role in the activation of persulfate (PS) for advanced oxidation processes. When incorporated into catalytic materials, such as boron-doped coconut-shell biochar or as amorphous boron, $\mathbf { B } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 3 }$ significantly enhances the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which notably include sulfate radicals $( \mathrm { { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - } ) }$ , hydroxyl radicals $( \bullet \mathrm { O H } )$ , and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ . The catalytic action of $\mathbf { B } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 3 }$ in activating persulfate leads to highly effective degradation of various organic pollutants [24,25].

Transition metals are also effective activators of peroxydisulfate (PDS) due to their ability to operate efficiently across varied pH levels, exhibit good stability, and they can be reused with minimal activity loss [26,27]. Transition metals like manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), cobalt (Co), iron (Fe) and nickel (Ni) are commonly used for PS activation due to their unique properties and benefits [28,29]. Mn-based catalysts are effective for water and soil remediation, while copper-based catalysts, especially when doped with N or cerium, show enhanced activity and stability [30,31]. Cobalt, often used in bimetallic systems, can synergize with other metals to improve PDS activation efficiency [32]. Iron-based catalysts, particularly in ferrite forms, are effective due to their stability and magnetic properties [33]. Nickel stands out as a promising transition metal for activating peroxydisulfate (PDS) due to its notable selectivity and minimal undesirable side reactions [34]. Various nickel-based catalytic materials, including Ni nanoclusters, Ni–NiO nanocomposites, and oxygen-vacancy-rich NiO, have demonstrated efficacy in promoting electron transfer and facilitating the formation of reactive complexes with PDS [35–37]. Compared to more aggressive activators like cobalt or copper, nickel exhibits moderate activity, which is particularly advantageous for controlled, non-radical degradation pathways. This controlled activation primarily leads to the generation of singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ , offering a selective and efficient approach for various oxidative processes [38,39].

Singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ is an excited state of molecular oxygen with high energy and strong electrophilic properties that is generated through various methods, including photochemical production, decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, and AOPs [40,41]. It is used in water treatment, selective oxidation, and environmental remediation for the degradation of contaminants like phenol, sulfamethoxazole, and bisphenol A (BPA) [42,43]. Singlet oxygen offers advantages over reactive oxygen species, including higher selectivity, resistance to environmental interference, and lower toxic byproduct production [44, 45].

This study focuses on the development of a novel NiO doped $\mathrm { { B _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } } }$ –porous carbon catalyst composed of hexamethylenetetramine, boric acid, and nickel for the efficient removal of BPA through PDS activation. The catalyst was synthesized by optimizing the preparation conditions, and its performance was evaluated in terms of BPA removal efficiency, reusability, and applicability to real water and wastewater samples. The role of singlet oxygen in the degradation process was also investigated. The Box-Behnken design (BBD) was employed to optimize the degradation process and evaluate the interactions between key parameters. This research aims to provide a sustainable and effective solution for BPA removal in water and wastewater treatment applications.

# 2.2. Catalyst preparation

The catalyst was synthesized by mixing hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA), boric acid $\mathrm { ( H _ { 3 } B O _ { 3 } ) }$ , and nickel chloride $\mathrm { ( N i C l _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 H _ { 2 } O ) }$ in a molar ratio of 1:5:0.05. The mixture was dissolved in deionized water and stirred magnetically at room temperature for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ to ensure homogeneity, followed by drying in an oven at $9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 12 hours. The dried precursor was ground into fine powder and heated to $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at a ramping rate of $2 . 1 8 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { m i n }$ over $4 \mathrm { h }$ , and then held at $5 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in a muffle furnace for calcination. After natural cooling to room temperature, the resulting solid was collected, ground, and stored for use.

# 2.3. Catalyst characterization

Functional groups on the material’s surface were identified using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR, Thermo, AVATAR, USA), with spectra collected in the $4 0 0 { \ - } 4 0 0 0 { \ } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ range at a resolution of $4 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ . The bonding characteristics and carbon structure were confirmed using Raman spectroscopy (TakRam N1–541, Iran), employing a $5 3 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ excitation laser. The surface morphology and elemental distribution were examined by Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM, MIRA3 TESCAN, Czech Republic) and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX, MIRA3 TESCAN, Czech Republic). # 2.5. Experimental setup

The characteristics of the groundwater and treated wastewater used in the experiments are summarized in Table S1. All experiments, except for the specific wastewater studies, were conducted using groundwater. Batch experiments were conducted using $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ glass beakers containing $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ of BPA solution. The pH was adjusted using 0.1 and 1 mol/L solutions of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrochloric acid (HCl). Precise dosages of the catalyst and peroxydisulfate (PDS) were added simultaneously to the solution to initiate the degradation process. The reaction was allowed to proceed under continuous stirring for predetermined time intervals. All experiments were performed in duplicate, and the reported data represent the mean values.

# 2.6. Process optimization using response surface methodology (RSM)

Response surface methodology (RSM) based on the Box–Behnken design (BBD) was employed using Design-Expert software (version v12.0.3.0, Stat-Ease Inc., USA) to systematically evaluate and optimize the operational parameters. Five independent variables were selected based on preliminary experiments and literature: solution pH (3–11), catalyst dosage $( 0 . 1 { - } 0 . 6 \ \mathbf { g } / \mathrm { L } )$ , PDS dosage $( 0 . 1 8 5 { - } 1 . 4 8 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ ), reaction time $( 2 { - } 3 0 \mathrm { m i n } )$ , and initial BPA concentration $( 0 . 5 \mathrm { - } 3 0 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ ). A total of 46 experimental runs were conducted according to the Box–Behnken design matrix, and BPA removal efficiency $( \% )$ was used as the response variable. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine the significance of the regression model and the contribution of each parameter.

# 2.7. Quenching experiments

Quenching experiments were conducted to identify the reactive species involved in BPA degradation. Methanol (MeOH) was employed as a scavenger for both hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and sulfate radicals $\left( { \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 } ^ { - } \right)$ , tert‑butyl alcohol (TBA) was used as a selective quencher for •OH radicals, and sodium azide $\mathrm { ( N a N _ { 3 } ) }$ was used to quench singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ [46–48]. A specific concentration of the quenching agent (MeOH, TBA, or $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 } .$ ) was added to the reaction mixture containing BPA and the activated catalyst. The concentration of the quenching agent was varied to observe its effect on BPA removal efficiency. Samples were then collected and analyzed to determine the BPA removal efficiency.

# 2.8. Co-existing ions experiments

The influence of commonly found co-existing ions on BPA removal efficiency was systematically investigated to understand their impact on the catalytic process in aqueous environments. The ions investigated were $S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { 2 \cdot }$ , $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ , Cl- , $\mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ , $\mathsf { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ , $\mathrm { M g } ^ { 2 + }$ , and humic acid (HA). For each experimental run, solutions containing an initial BPA concentration and specific ion concentrations were prepared in deionized water.

Subsequently, the predetermined dosages of catalyst and PDS were added to initiate the degradation process. The BPA removal efficiency was then compared to control experiments conducted in deionized water without the addition of co-existing ions, in order to investigate their effects.

# 2.9. Catalyst reusability tests

To assess the catalyst’s reusability and long-term stability, a series of consecutive batch experiments were performed. At the end of each cycle, the catalyst was separated from the solution by centrifugation. The collected catalyst was then washed three times consecutively with absolute ethanol and deionized water to remove any adsorbed species or reaction by-products. After washing, the catalyst was dried under vacuum at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for a specified duration before being re-introduced into a fresh BPA solution with new dosages of catalyst and PDS for the subsequent degradation cycle. The BPA removal efficiency was monitored over multiple cycles to evaluate the catalyst’s sustained performance.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Catalyst synthesis optimization

To determine the optimal thermal treatment conditions, the effect of calcination temperature was first investigated using a fixed molar ratio of 1:1 (HMTA: boric acid) and a ramping time of $4 \mathrm { h }$ , followed by a 30- minute hold at the target temperature. Four temperatures were examined: $3 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , $4 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , and $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . Among them, the catalyst calcined at $5 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ exhibited the highest BPA degradation efficiency. The sample prepared at $3 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ was structurally unstable and completely dissolved in water, while the catalysts calcined at $4 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ showed noticeably lower degradation performance (Fig. 1a). Therefore, $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ was selected as the optimal calcination temperature. Subsequently, the ramping rate to reach $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ was varied (8.75, 4.37, 2.9, and $2 . 1 8 ~ \mathrm { ^ { \circ } C / m i n }$ , from a starting temperature of ${ } ^ { 2 5 } \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ to explore its influence on catalytic performance. Ramping rate can influence the structural development of catalyst precursors by controlling the rate of decomposition, particle growth, and interaction between components during calcination [49,50]. In all cases, the final temperature was held for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . Results showed that a slower ramping rate improved performance, with the $2 . 1 8 ~ \mathrm { \mathrm { ~ } ^ { \circ } C / m i n }$ ramp providing the most effective catalyst (Fig. 1b).

Following the optimization of calcination conditions, the molar ratio of HMTA to boric acid was optimized to enhance the catalytic performance. A range of molar ratios was examined: 1:0 (HMTA with no boric acid), 1:0.1, 1:0.25, 1:0.5, 1:1, 1:2, 1:5, and 1:10. As depicted in Fig. 1c, decreasing the HMTA to boric acid molar ratio generally improved the BPA degradation efficiency, with catalysts prepared with molar ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:5 notably exhibiting significantly superior performance compared to other investigated compositions. Conversely, the catalyst prepared with a 1:10 ratio was highly unstable and exhibited undesired solubility in the reaction medium, thereby rendering it impractical for catalytic application. To better discriminate between top-performing ratios (1:1, 1:2, and 1:5), a follow-up experiment was conducted under more challenging conditions using lower dosages of both catalyst and PDS (Fig. 1d). Under these conditions, the catalyst with a 1:5 ratio demonstrated faster and more complete removal of BPA compared to the others. This result confirmed the superior reactivity and stability of the 1:5 ratio, which was consequently selected as the optimized formulation for subsequent metal doping and performance evaluations.

# 3.2. Metal doping

To further enhance catalytic performance, various transition metals were introduced into the optimized 1:5 matrix, including cobalt, copper, iron, aluminum, and nickel. Each metal was added in the form of its nitrate salt using the following precursor amounts: 20 mmol HMTA, 100 mmol boric acid, and $0 . 5 \mathrm { \ m m o l }$ metal ion. Performance evaluations were initially conducted under lower catalyst and PDS dosages to better distinguish differences between metal types. Among the doped samples, copper, iron, and nickel demonstrated significantly higher BPA removal efficiencies compared to cobalt and aluminum (Fig. 2a). These promising candidates were selected for further comparison. To confirm the superior activity of the most promising dopants, Cu-, Fe-, and Ni-doped catalysts were tested alongside the metal-free sample (control) under conditions involving higher initial BPA concentrations (Fig. 2b). The Nidoped catalyst outperformed all others, achieving more rapid and complete BPA degradation, even at elevated pollutant levels. This affirmed nickel as the optimal metal dopant for this system.

# 3.3. Characterization

The BET surface area of the catalyst was $1 0 . 7 8 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g }$ . The total pore volume at a relative pressure $\left( \mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 } \right)$ of 0.990 was measured as 0.0919 $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } / { \bf g } ;$ , and the mean pore diameter was calculated to be $3 4 . 1 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Table S2). Although the BET surface area is relatively low compared to conventional porous carbons, the catalyst exhibited outstanding BPA removal performance. This indicates that the surface chemistry and active-site functionality of the catalyst play a more decisive role than the total surface area in PDS activation.

The FTIR spectrum (Fig. 3a) reveals distinct absorption bands indicative of the presence of B-O, $\mathrm { O - H }$ and nickel oxide (Ni-O) bonds, which confirm the successful incorporation of boron and nickel species within the catalyst. The broad band observed around $3 2 2 0 . 5 4 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ corresponds to $\mathrm { O - H }$ vibrations, suggesting the presence of surface hydroxyl groups [51,52]. The peaks at $1 4 4 4 . 4 9 \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ and $1 2 0 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ can be attributed to B-O stretching vibrations, while those at $3 3 8 0 . 2 3 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , $1 6 2 5 . 5 5 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , $7 5 6 . 3 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , $6 4 5 . 1 4 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , and $5 4 7 . 9 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ are characteristic of Ni–O bond vibrations, as also observed in the FTIR spectrum of pure NiO (Fig. S2) [53–55].

XRD patterns of the catalyst before and after multiple BPA removal cycles are compared in Fig. 3b While no new diffraction peaks emerged after recycling, the overall intensity of the peaks decreased, indicating a partial reduction in crystallinity [56]. The diffraction peak observed around $_ { 1 5 ^ { \circ } }$ can be attributed to amorphous ${ \bf B } _ { 2 } { \bf O } _ { 3 }$ , while the reflections at $2 8 ^ { \circ }$ , $3 1 ^ { \circ }$ , and $ 4 0 ^ { \circ }$ may also indicate the presence of $\mathbf { { B } } _ { 2 } \mathbf { { O } } _ { 3 }$ -based phases, in good agreement with previous XRD studies that confirm its presence [57]. Furthermore, weak but distinguishable peaks appearing at $4 3 ^ { \circ }$ , $5 4 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ , and $6 2 ^ { \circ }$ are characteristic of nickel oxide (NiO), confirming the partial crystallization of NiO within the composite structure [58]. These reflections are consistent with the peaks of pure NiO (Fig. S3), further verifying that NiO domains are preserved within the catalyst. The relatively low intensity of these NiO peaks can be attributed to the low Ni content in the catalyst. The absence of new peaks suggests that the catalyst maintained its structural framework and phase identity, confirming its compositional stability over reuse.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the elemental composition and successful incorporation of nickel, boron, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen within the catalyst. The EDS spectrum (Fig. S4) showed distinct peaks corresponding to boron $\left( \mathrm { ~ B ~ K ~ } \alpha \right)$ , carbon $( \mathbb { C } \ \mathbb { K } \alpha )$ , nitrogen $( \mathrm { ~ N ~ K ~ } \alpha )$ , oxygen $( \mathrm { O } \mathrm { K } \alpha )$ , and nickel (Ni Kα and Ni Kβ). Quantitative analysis revealed the elemental composition as approximately 15.20 wt $\%$ boron, 31.32 wt $\%$ carbon, 4.03 wt $\%$ nitrogen, 47.72 wt $\%$ oxygen, and 1.73 wt $\%$ nickel, confirming the intended doping and the presence of all precursor elements. In addition, EDS mapping demonstrated the uniform distribution of Ni, C, N, and O across the porous carbon framework (Fig. S5). Boron, however, was not clearly visualized in the mapping images due to the detection limitations of low-atomicnumber elements in EDS mapping. Its presence was nevertheless confirmed by the EDS spectrum and further supported by complementary characterizations (XRD, FTIR), verifying its successful incorporation into the catalyst structure.

The Raman spectrum (Fig. 3c) provides insights into the molecular structure of the catalyst. The peak at $1 3 5 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ is assigned to the d-band, representing defects in the carbon structure, while the $1 5 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ peak corresponds to the G-band of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ -bonded carbon [59]. The D/G intensity ratio reflects the degree of disorder in the carbonaceous phase. The calculated $\mathrm { I _ { D } / I _ { G } }$ ratio is 0.91, indicating a moderate level of structural disorder.

The surface charge properties of the catalyst were evaluated by zeta potential analysis over a wide $\mathtt { p H }$ range (2–12) to understand its interfacial behavior relevant to peroxydisulfate (PDS) activation for bisphenol A (BPA) degradation. Furthermore, this negative surface charge can play a significant role in enhancing the generation of reactive species, including singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ , which is particularly effective and stable in these higher pH ranges, compared to acidic conditions [62,63].

# 3.7. Quenching studies

The addition of MeOH at increasing concentrations $( 1 0 { - } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ ) did not significantly reduce BPA removal efficiency (Fig. 7a). Similarly, the presence of TBA (Fig. 7b) also had minimal impact, suggesting that •OH radicals played a minor role in this system. The high stability of BPA removal in the presence of MeOH and TBA reinforces the non-radical nature of the dominant oxidation pathway. The involvement of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ could be attributed to the interaction of activated PDS with surface functionalities on the Ni-doped catalyst, facilitating a non-radical electron-transfer mechanism or energy transfer process. This is consistent with previously reported systems where singlet oxygen formation was favored in metal-based PDS activation routes and offers additional advantages in terms of selectivity and resistance to radical scavengers often present in complex water matrices [65–67].

# 3.8. Mechanistic insights into electron transfer pathways

The dominance of singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ over •OH and $\mathrm { { \cal S O } } _ { 4 } \bullet \mathrm { { \overline { { { \cal ~ S } } } } }$ radicals, as confirmed by quenching experiments, indicates that BPA degradation in the NiO– ${ \bf \delta } _ { { \bf { B } } _ { 2 } } { \bf { O } } _ { 3 }$ –porous carbon/PDS system primarily follows a nonradical pathway. This route is strongly linked to surface-mediated electron transfer. Zeta potential measurements showed that the catalyst carries a negative charge under neutral $\mathrm { p H }$ , enhancing the adsorption of peroxydisulfate anions $( S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - } )$ onto the catalyst surface [68,69].

This electron-transfer pathway explains the high selectivity of the system, its resistance to radical scavengers, and the strong performance in real water matrices. The proposed mechanism is consistent with previous reports that highlighted the importance of Ni(II)/Ni(III) cycling and boron functionalities in persulfate activation and singlet oxygen generation [70–72].

# 3.9. Effect of co-existing ions

Based on findings reported in the literature, various naturally occurring and anthropogenic ions present in environmental water matrices can influence the performance of advanced oxidation processes and degradation efficiency of bisphenol A [73,74]. Therefore, the effect of common co-existing ions typically found in aqueous sources including sulfate $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } )$ , bicarbonate $\mathrm { ( H C O _ { 3 } ^ { - } ) }$ , chloride (Cl- ), nitrate $\left( \mathrm { N O } \bar { 3 } \right)$ , humic acid (HA), calcium $( \mathsf { C a } ^ { 2 + } )$ , and magnesium $( \mathrm { M g } ^ { 2 + } )$ was systematically investigated [75,76]. To gain a more comprehensive understanding, each ion was tested at different concentrations to assess the extent of its influence on the process (Fig. S11). Sulfate, bicarbonate, chloride, nitrate, and magnesium ions exhibited negligible or no inhibitory effects while sustaining nearly $1 0 0 ~ \%$ BPA removal across all tested concentrations, as illustrated in Fig. S11a. This indicates that the catalytic activity of the material and the activation of peroxydisulfate (PDS) are minimally affected by these ions. $\mathrm { C a ^ { 2 + } }$ significantly impeded BPA elimination; the efficiency decreased from $8 9 \%$ at $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ to $7 3 \%$ at $4 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ . This inhibition is likely due to surface complexation, precipitation, or alterations in the electrostatic environment of the catalyst, thereby reducing access to active sites. At elevated concentrations, humic acid slightly diminished degradation efficiency (Fig. S11b).

A comprehensive study was conducted to examine potential interactions exclusively among $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ , humic acid, and $\mathsf { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ , as humic acid and $\mathrm { C a ^ { 2 + } }$ were the only species exhibiting measurable inhibitory effects. # 3.10. Reusability of the catalyst

The reusability and long-term stability of a catalyst are crucial factors in determining its practical applicability and economic viability for environmental remediation processes. To assess the durability and recyclability of the synthesized catalyst for bisphenol A (BPA) degradation via peroxydisulfate (PDS) activation, a series of reusability tests were conducted over multiple consecutive reaction cycles. As shown in Fig. S13, the removal efficiency remained exceptionally high $( > 9 5 ~ \% )$ ) up to the fifth cycle, demonstrating the catalyst’s robustness and stability. To further verify structural stability at this stage, XRD analysis was performed on the catalyst after the fifth cycle (Fig. 3b). The postreuse pattern exhibited no new diffraction peaks compared with the fresh catalyst, although a decrease in overall peak intensity was observed, suggesting partial loss of crystallinity. The persistence of the characteristic amorphous $\mathbf { { B } } _ { 2 } \mathbf { { O } } _ { 3 }$ band and NiO reflections confirmed that the catalyst maintained its structural framework and phase identity after repeated operation. ICP–OES analysis was conducted to evaluate nickel leaching from the NiO doped $\mathrm { B _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ –porous carbon catalyst during repeated use. Across cycles 1 to 5, $\mathrm { N i ^ { 2 + } }$ concentrations remained below the detection threshold of $0 . 0 1 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ . These values are substantially lower than the U.S. EPA drinking water guideline for nickel $( 0 . 1 0 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ and well below typical industrial discharge limits (several mg/L) [81, 82], confirming that the catalyst exhibits excellent structural stability and negligible metal release under operating conditions.

However, a gradual decline in performance was observed from the sixth cycle. This decrease is most likely associated with partial pore blockage and surface fouling caused by the accumulation of oxidation byproducts, together with a progressive reduction in crystallinity, as evidenced by the decreased XRD peak intensity after reuse [83,84]. Both effects can gradually reduce the accessibility of active sites without significant nickel leaching, which remained negligible throughout the cycles. Despite this gradual decline, the catalyst maintained a reasonable level of activity even after nine cycles, with a removal efficiency of $6 5 \%$ in the final cycle. The catalyst’s strong reusability and moderate long-term stability indicate its potential applicability in practical scenarios. It is therefore a suitable candidate for advanced oxidation processes aimed at the removal of BPA from contaminated waters and for sustainable wastewater treatment.

# 3.11. Possible BPA degradation pathways

To gain further insight into the degradation mechanism of BPA, GC–MS analysis was performed under optimized conditions, and the detected intermediates are shown in Table S7 and Fig. S14. Several compounds were identified, including 2‑sec-butylcyclopentanone $\mathbf { R T } =$ $3 . 8 1 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ ), dibutyl phthalate $( \mathrm { R T } = 1 4 . 4 1 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ , 7-hexadecenal $\left( \mathbf { R } \mathbf { T } \right. =$ $1 4 . 7 8 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ ), cis-vaccenic acid $( \mathrm { R T } = 1 5 . 3 5 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ ), 9,17-octadecadienal $( \mathrm { R T } = 1 7 . 0 9 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ ), Z-9-octadecenal $( \mathrm { R T } = 1 9 . 4 6 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ ), 7-pentadecyne $( \mathrm { R T } = 1 9 . 8 6 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ , diisooctyl phthalate $( \mathrm { R T } = 2 0 . 4 1 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ , and longchain alcohols such as 1-heptatriacotanol $( \mathrm { R T } ~ = ~ 2 2 . 4 9 ~ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ ). The occurrence of phthalate derivatives suggests partial retention and transformation of aromatic structures, while the detection of aldehydes, alcohols, and unsaturated hydrocarbons points to oxidative cleavage and transformation of aliphatic moieties. Together, these results indicate that BPA degradation proceeds through dual routes involving aromatic modification and aliphatic oxidation.

Notably, classical hydroxylated aromatic intermediates such as catechol or hydroquinone, which are widely reported in radicalmediated oxidation pathways [85,86], were not detected in the system. This absence, combined with the strong inhibition observed in sodium azide quenching experiments, supports that a non-radical singlet oxygen pathway was primarily responsible for BPA degradation. Moreover, although small molecular acids such as formic and acetic acids are expected as terminal intermediates, they were not detected in GC–MS spectra, which is consistent with the known limitations of the technique in identifying low molecular weight and highly polar products without derivatization. Nonetheless, the nearly complete TOC removal $( 9 8 . 6 4 ~ \% )$ ) achieved under the same conditions confirms that the majority of BPA and its intermediates were ultimately mineralized into $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ . Based on these findings, a possible degradation pathway is proposed (Fig. 9), highlighting electrophilic attack on BPA’s side chains, subsequent formation of phthalate-type and aliphatic intermediates, and further oxidation toward mineralization.

# 3.12. Comparison with literature

To further evaluate the performance of the NiO– ${ \bf - B _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ –porous carbon catalyst, its catalytic activity was compared with several reported systems for BPA degradation through persulfate activation (Table 3). The results clearly indicate that the present catalyst achieves nearly complete BPA removal within only $6 . 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , which is substantially faster than many other reported catalysts that typically require reaction times ranging from 10 to $5 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ under comparable conditions. In addition, the persulfate dosage applied in this study $( 0 . 8 5 \mathrm { m M } )$ is significantly lower than those used in several previously reported systems, while still maintaining excellent degradation efficiency. This demonstrates the high catalytic activity and effective persulfate utilization of the developed material.

Another important advantage is that the catalyst maintained high removal efficiencies even at elevated BPA concentrations. The catalyst also performed effectively over a broad pH range (3–11), which is critical for real-world applications where water matrices vary widely in acidity and alkalinity. Furthermore, the catalyst retained over $9 5 \%$ BPA removal efficiency for five consecutive reuse cycles, highlighting its stability and reusability. The catalytic process was also effective in real water matrices, including groundwater and treated wastewater, confirming its robustness against environmental interferences. Overall, compared with other persulfate activation systems, the present catalyst demonstrates faster kinetics, lower oxidant demand, strong performance at higher pollutant concentrations, broad pH applicability, and excellent stability and reusability, making it highly attractive for practical water and wastewater treatment applications.

# 4. Conclusions

This study successfully developed and optimized a novel NiO-doped $\mathbf { { B } } _ { 2 } \mathbf { { O } } _ { 3 }$ –porous carbon catalyst for the efficient removal of bisphenol A (BPA) through peroxydisulfate (PDS) activation. Systematic optimization revealed that calcination at $5 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with a $2 . 1 8 ~ \mathrm { ^ { \circ } C / m i n }$ ramping rate and a 1:5:0.05 molar ratio yielded the most effective catalyst, with XRD analysis confirming the coexistence of crystalline NiO domains and $\mathbf { B } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 3 }$ phases within a stable porous carbon framework. Through response surface methodology, optimal conditions were identified that achieved remarkable BPA removal efficiencies of $9 9 . 9 \%$ in groundwater and 98.9 $\%$ in treated wastewater. Beyond just removal, total organic carbon (TOC) analysis confirmed a high mineralization efficiency of over $9 8 \%$ , demonstrating the system’s ability to achieve near-complete degradation of BPA and its intermediates. Quenching experiments, along with GC–MS analysis of degradation intermediates, provided a comprehensive body of evidence that confirmed singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ as the dominant reactive species. This highlights a selective, non-radical pathway that minimizes the formation of undesirable byproducts. The catalyst exhibited exceptional stability and reusability, maintaining over $9 5 ~ \%$ removal efficiency for five consecutive cycles. ICP-OES analysis confirmed that metal leaching was negligible $( < 0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { m g / L } )$ , ensuring the process is both environmentally friendly and sustainable. Furthermore, the catalyst demonstrated robust resilience against matrix interference from co-existing ions. This research provides an efficient, selective, and sustainable approach for BPA removal that effectively bridges laboratory research with practical environmental applications.
