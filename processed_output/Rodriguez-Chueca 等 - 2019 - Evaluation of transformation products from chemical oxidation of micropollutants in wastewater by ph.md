# ABSTRACT

In this research, the degradation of seven different micropollutants (MPs) and the formation of their transformation products (TPs) have been assessed during the application of different advanced oxidation processes: photolytic and photocatalytic activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and persulfate (PS). The results were compared with those obtained from the photolytic experiments using hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ as oxidant. A significant abatement of almost all MPs was achieved, even with very low UV-C contact time (9 and 28 s). The degradation of atenolol (ATN) and caffeine (CFN) ranged from 84 to $1 0 0 \%$ with a dose of $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ of any oxidant. The efficiencies for bisphenol-A (BPA), carbamazepine (CBZ), diclofenac (DCF), ibuprofen (IBP), and sulfamethoxazole (SMX) varied depending on the oxidation system and operating conditions (oxidant dose and UV-C contact time), leading to the photolysis of PMS to higher efficiencies than PS and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ . In all cases, the abatement of MPs ranged from 63 to $8 3 \%$ , even with the lowest PMS dosage. Moreover, the addition of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ as a catalyst enhanced the removal efficiency, reaching almost total removal, especially over CBZ, DCF, and IBP. The Dissolved Organic Carbon (DOC) removal ranged between 44 and $6 2 \%$ , suggesting the transformation of MPs in intermediate compounds. The identification of transformation products was carried out for each micropollutant and each oxidation treatment, being observed some transformation products specific of oxidation by sulfate radicals. For example, m/z 165.0432 only appeared after PMS/Fe(II)/UV-C on the degradation of BFA, $m / z 2 5 1 . 0 8 2 $ appeared after photolytic activation of PMS and PS on CBZ removal, and $m / z \ 1 2 8 . 0 4 5 2$ was observed after any sulfate radical oxidation treatment, but not after photolysis of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ .

Keywords: sulfate radicals; micropollutants; transformation products; mechanisms; UVC radiation.

# 1. Introduction

Reports of micropollutants (MPs) occurrence in water bodies have increased in recent years. MPs pollution is the result of human activities, such as industrial, hospital, veterinary, domestic, etc. The real effects that these MPs could exert on ecosystems and humans are currently undetermined, although some researchers assert that they could negatively affect aquatic ecosystems, biological processes of organisms, and overall human health (Boxall et al., 2012; Schwarzenbach et al., 2006). Due to the chemical stability and low biodegradability of most MPs, conventional wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) are unable to achieve their complete removal (Collado et al., 2014; Casas & Bester, 2015; Gruchlik et al., 2018). For these reasons, although the vast majority of these compounds are not yet legislatively regulated, monitoring them is strongly recommended (i.e., in the European Union through 2013/39/EU). Therefore, wastewater reuse could constitute an important route for the release of MPs to the environment, and if MPs reach water bodies or water supplies (catchment of fresh water, or reuse of treated wastewater), they could present a serious concern due to their unknown consequences (Badia-Fabregat et al., 2015).

Sulfate Radical-based Advanced Oxidation Processes (SR-AOPs) are becoming emerging alternatives to the widely reported hydroxyl radicals-based AOPs (HR-AOPs). Among the main advantages of SR-AOPs are the low cost of persulfate salts, their lack of toxicity, ease of handling and storage, and the low pH dependency of their activity (Wacławek et al., 2017). Sulfate radicals could be generated from the activation of different persulfate salts, such as potassium peroxymonosulfate ${ \mathrm { R S O } } _ { 5 } { \mathrm { \overline { { ~ } } } }$ ; PMS) and sodium or potassium persulfate $\left( \mathbf { S } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - } \right.$ ; PS). Nevertheless, these persulfate anions are not efficient by themselves, and they need to be activated by the following routes: i) heat; ii) UV radiation; iii) elevated $\mathrm { p H }$ ; or iv) addition of transition metals. Their high redox potential $( \mathrm { E } _ { 0 } { = } 2 . 5 { - } 3 . 1 \mathrm { V } )$ and reactivity make sulfate radicals, as a strong oxidant species, able to degrade any recalcitrant organic substance (Ling et al., 2017; Ahmed et al., 2014; Pang et al., 2016; Xia et al., 2017; Oncu et al., 2015), and they have been proven efficient in the inactivation of microorganisms (Rodríguez-Chueca et al., 2017a, b).

Monitoring of the transformation products generated during the process is as important as the removal of MPs present in wastewater. Some authors have investigated the degradation mechanisms of different MPs by sulfate radicals (Pang et al., 2016; Ling et al., 2017; Mercado et al., 2018). However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies currently exist about degradation mechanisms by SR-AOPs of MPs in real wastewater effluents.

This study is focused on the evaluation of the efficiency of different homogeneous AOPs at the laboratory scale $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { U V } { - } \mathrm { C }$ ; PS/UV-C; PS/Fe(II)/UV-C; PMS/UV-C and PMS/Fe(II)/UV-C) towards the degradation of detected MPs in real WWTP effluents, including assessment of their transformation mechanisms and the extent of the mineralization process.

# 2. Material and methods

# 2.1. Wastewater samples

Treatments were applied over real secondary effluents from Estiviel WWTP, located in the city of Toledo (Spain). Samples were collected in an intermediate bulk container (IBC) from tertiary treatment of the WWTP, prior to the UV-C disinfection step but after a coagulation/flocculation/filtration process. The water was shipped to Universidad Rey Juan Carlos facilities (Móstoles, Spain) to be characterized and to be submitted under experimental treatments. Main characteristics are summarized in Table S1 (Supplementary material).

# 2.2. UV-C photoreactor

All of the treatments were performed in continuous mode using flow rates of 1 and 3 $\mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m i n }$ , and at the natural pH of samples without further adjustment $( 7 . 1 7 \pm 0 . 0 8 )$ . These flow rate values were selected to provide a UV-C contact time ranging from 9 to $2 8 \mathrm { ~ s ~ }$ , in the order of the actual values in real wastewater treatment plants (Rodríguez-Chueca et al., 2018a). The feed system consists of a stirred tank with a volume of $1 \mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ (enough to feed the reactor for few hours to allow the analysis of the system’s performance under steady state), a centrifugal pump, and a flow meter. The reactor is a $4 6 \mathrm { { c m } }$ in length and $3 . 6 0 ~ \mathrm { c m }$ in inner-diameter quartz tube illuminated by a UV-C lamp (Philips TUV PL-L 95W/4P HO 1CT/25, $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } { = } 2 5 4 \mathrm { n m }$ ) placed $1 0 \mathrm { c m }$ above the tube in the axis of a parabolic reflector. The total illuminated volume is ca. $4 7 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ . The UV-C dosages from 19 to 57 J/L, calculated from the experimental value of $9 5 . 5 \ \mathrm { W / m } ^ { 2 }$ measured with a UV-C radiometer (Delta OHM, Model HD 2102.2). The assay started when the UV-C radiation system was switched on, also corresponding to the addition of oxidants (PMS, PS, or HP) and the metal activator $\mathrm { [ F e ( I I ) ] }$ . After reaching steady state operation, three replicate samples were collected at 10 minutes of time interval to present results as average data.

# 2.3. Chemicals

Different oxidation treatments (SR-AOPs and HR-AOPs) were applied using two oxidant concentration levels (0.5 and $5 \mathrm { m M }$ ) based on previous studies (Rodríguez-Chueca et al., 2018a, 2018b). Photolytic and catalytic activation of peroxymonosulfate $\mathrm { ( H S O 5 ^ { - } }$ , PMS) and persulfate $\left( \mathbf { S } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - } \right.$ ; PS) were performed. Potassium peroxymonosulphate (2KHSO $5 \cdot$ KHSO4·K2SO4) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, while sodium persulfate was provided by Panreac. For catalytic activation of PMS, iron (II) sulfate heptahydrate $\mathrm { ( F e S O _ { 4 } \bullet 7 H _ { 2 } O ) }$ from Panreac was used, always in the same molar ratio as PMS and PS. Finally, $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ b $3 0 \%$ w/w; HP) was acquired from Panreac, for application of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { U V } { - } \mathrm { C }$ treatments. All of the reagents were used in analytical grade. Deionized water was used throughout the study for analytics.

# 2.4. Analytical methods

Physicochemical parameters (pH, soluble chemical oxygen demand (CODs), biological oxygen demand (BOD), total organic carbon (TOC) and nitrite $\left( \mathbf { N } { \cdot } \mathbf { N } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ ) were determined according to Standard Methods (Eaton et al., 2005). Total, inorganic and organic carbon (TC, IC and TOC) were determined by a Shimadzu analyzer (TOCVSCH). Turbidity was measured employing a turbidimeter (HACH 2100N) and $\mathrm { p H }$ was monitored using a selective electrode pH meter HACH HQ30d. COD and $\mathrm { N O } _ { 2 } \mathrm { ^ - }$ were determined by reagents commercial kits specific for their determination, and using a multiparameter spectrophotometer HACH LANGE DR3900.

# 2.5. Micropollutants analysis

Seven target MPs, namely, ibuprofen (IBU), atenolol (ATN), carbamazepine (CBZ), diclofenac (DCF), bisphenol A (BPA), caffeine (CFN) and sulfamethoxazole (SMX), were detected in the studied wastewater samples by the employed analytical method. The analytical methodology developed for the quantification of MPs was based on conventional solid phase extraction (SPE), using as isotopic internal standards to assess the recovery: diclofenac-(acetophenyl ring- $^ { 1 3 } { \mathrm { C } } _ { 6 }$ ) sodium salt hemi (nonahydrate) (13C6C8H10Cl2NNaO2·4.5H2O; Sigma-Aldrich), caffeine- $^ { 1 3 } { \bf C } _ { 3 }$ solution $^ { 1 3 } { \bf C } _ { 3 } { \bf C } _ { 5 } { \bf H } _ { 1 0 } { \bf N } _ { 4 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ ; Sigma-Aldrich), and carbamazepine- $\mathbf { \delta D } _ { 1 0 }$ $\mathrm { C } _ { 1 5 } \mathrm { D } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ ; Sigma-Aldrich). The internal standard mixture was added to a filtered $1 { , } 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ water sample, and the sample was extracted with a Waters Oasis HLB SPE cartridge that was first conditioned with $4 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of methanol and $2 \mathrm { x } 4 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of water. The cartridge was then washed with $2 \mathrm { x } 4 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of $5 \%$ methanol in water and dried with a vacuum. The extract was eluted first with $1 2 \mathrm { m L }$ ethyl acetate, and then with $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of methanol. The two fractions were mixed and finally concentrated to $2 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ under a stream of nitrogen. Then, eluted samples were analyzed by reverse phase liquid chromatography (UPLC) coupled with an LTQ Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer.

Chromatographic separation, followed by HRMS analysis, was run on a Phenomenex (Castel Maggiore, BO, Italy) Luna C18 column, $1 5 0 \times 2 . 0 \mathrm { m m }$ , $3 \mu \mathrm { m }$ particle size, using an Ultimate 3000 HPLC instrument (Thermo Scientific, Milan, Italy). The injection volume was $2 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ , and the flow rate was $2 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L / m i n }$ . The following gradient mobile phase composition was adopted: 95/5 to 0/95 in 40 min aqueous eluent/acetonitrile. For positive ion analysis, the aqueous eluent was $0 . 0 5 \%$ formic acid, and for negative ion analysis $5 \mathrm { m M }$ ammonium acetate.

An LTQ Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with an ESI ion source was used. The LC column effluent was delivered to the ion source, using nitrogen as both sheath and auxiliary gas. The source voltage was set to $4 . 5 \ \mathrm { k V }$ in positive ion mode and $3 . 5 \ \mathrm { k V }$ in negative ion mode. The heated capillary temperature was maintained at $2 7 0 \mathrm { { } ^ { \circ } C }$ . The main tuning parameters adopted for the ESI source were: positive ion mode: $1 3 . 0 0 \mathrm { V }$ for capillary voltage and $5 5 \mathrm { V }$ for tube lens; and negative ion mode: $1 . 0 0 \mathrm { V }$ for capillary voltage and $4 2 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ for tube lens. Full scan spectra were acquired in the range $1 0 0 { - } 4 5 0 m / z$ . MSn spectra were acquired in the range between ion trap cut-off and precursor ion $m / z$ values. Mass resolution was set to 30000. Mass accuracy of recorded ions (vs. calculated) was $\pm \ : 0 . 0 0 1$ u (without internal calibration). Quantitative determinations were performed on the basis of the validated method described in previous work (Medana et al., 2008).

# 3. Results and Discussion

Micropollutants detected in this research have been previously detected by other authors in treated wastewater samples and water bodies (Collado et al., 2014; Luo et al., 2014; Ben et al., 2014). In general terms, the concentration of the studied MPs are in the same range than those reported in literature, with the exception of CBZ and SMX, with values slightly higher than those reported by Collado et al. (2014) and Ben et al. (2018). However this result may be not surprising taking into account the widespread use and high refractoriness of these molecules to common wastewater treatments.

# 3.1. Photolysis of HP, PMS, and PS

The photolysis efficiency of HP, PMS, and PS was investigated on the removal of seven target MPs detected in real wastewater samples, and the results are shown in Table 1. In general, these treatments showed an efficiency significantly higher than UV-C radiation alone (Rodríguez-Chueca et al., 2018a; Rodríguez-Chueca et al., 2018b), although some exceptions exist, for instance, in the case of IBP for which no degradation was observed under some operating conditions (photolysis of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , and with molar flux lower than 300 mmol/h of PMS and PS). In this case, just the PMS/UV-C system obtained notable abatement when the highest dosage of PMS $\mathbf { \zeta } \mathbf { \mathrm { m M } } ,$ was used $( 6 8 \pm 3 \ \%$ and $1 0 0 \%$ depending on the UV-C contact time).

CFN exhibited the highest abatement levels in all experimental conditions. Three oxidation systems reached total removal of CFN with $5 \ \mathrm { m M }$ as oxidant dose, although efficiency between 84 to $1 0 0 \%$ was obtained already using 10-fold less concentration of oxidants. ATN was also totally abated, but in this case, because of the very low initial concentration $( 2 2 0 \pm 1 5 0 \mathrm { n g / L } )$ , it was easier to reach the limit of quantification (LOQ).

For the remaining MPs (BPA, CBZ, DCF, and SMX), efficiencies varied depending on applied oxidation system and operating conditions (oxidant dose and UV-C contact time). In all of these cases, the combination PMS/UV-C reached higher efficiencies than HP/UV-C and PS/UV-C. These differences were especially evident for DCF, CBZ, and SMX. Moreover, as expected, the highest abatements were obtained by using the highest dose of oxidants $( 5 \ \mathrm { m M } )$ ). However, the use of lower PMS doses $( 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { m M } )$ achieved removal values higher than $9 0 \%$ for DCF and SMX, and in the range from 63 to $8 3 \%$ of SMX with the other two oxidants. Finally, IBP was not adequately removed under all of the applied operating conditions in the different photosystems. Indeed, only the PMS/UVC system was able to achieve partial removal when a dose of $5 \mathrm { m M }$ was used.

Two different mechanisms might be involved in the activation of PS and PMS by UV radiation (Wang & Wang, 2018). The first one is the cleavage of the O-O bond by energy input of UV, as shown in Eqs. (1) and (2):

$$
S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - } \stackrel { h \upsilon } {  } 2 S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }
$$

$$
H S O _ { 5 } ^ { - } \stackrel { h \upsilon } {  } 2 S O _ { 4 } ^ { * - } + H O ^ { \bullet }
$$

The other PS and PMS activation hypothesis is the production of the electron by the interaction of UV radiation with water molecule, as described in Eqs. (3) to (5) (Wang & Wang, 2018):

$$
{ \mathrm { H } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } \longrightarrow { \mathrm { H } } ^ { \bullet } + { \mathrm { H O } } ^ { \bullet }
$$

$$
\mathrm { S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - } + \mathrm { H } ^ { \bullet }  \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - } + \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { 2 - } + \mathrm { H } ^ { + }
$$

$$
\mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - } + \mathrm { H } ^ { \bullet }  \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } + \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }
$$

For the second mechanism, the propagation of radicals was limited due to the extremely short penetration depth of UV-254 nm (Wang & Wang, 2018).

The application of photolytic activation of PMS and PS to remove organic MPs is widely reported in the literature (Gao et al., 2012; Tan et al., 2013; Mahdiahmed & Chiron, 2014; Ao & Liu, 2017; Yang et al., 2017). Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, no references have yet applied sulfate radicals to different MPs at the same time, with just seconds of UV-C contact time and with very low dosages of PMS and PS. For instance, Sharma et al. (2015) reported almost total abatement of BPA $( 9 6 . 7 \% )$ with an optimum dose of PMS of $0 . 6 6 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ , which is very close to the dose $( 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { m M } )$ ) reported here, but with a higher UV-C contact time $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } } ,$ ) because of the different reactor configuration (batch). On the other hand, Khan et al. (2017) reached total removal of lindane after 100 min of treatment with $1 \mathrm { m M }$ of PMS/UV-C. Dhaka et al. (2018) compared the efficiency of three photolytic systems (HP/UV-C, PS/UV-C, and PMS/UV-C) on the removal of ethyl paraben. Dhaka et al.’s (2018) results showed the highest efficiency of PS/UV-C, followed by HP/UV-C and PMS/UV-C, with the following operating conditions: batch reactor, clean water samples, and high UV-C contact time ${ \mathrm { 9 0 ~ m i n } } , $ ). On the other hand, Tan et al. (2013) reported a higher efficiency of the removal of antipyrine from HP/UVC than from the combination PS/UV-C, with an oxidant dose of $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ (equal to that used in this research) and $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ of reaction time. Furthermore, Ao and Liu (2017) found the degradation of SMX in Milli-Q water samples spiked with $2 3 . 6 9 ~ \mu \mathrm { M }$ of this antibiotic, using photolysis of HP, PS, and PMS. With an oxidant dosage of $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ and UV-C dosages of $2 0 0 \ \mathrm { m J } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ , the highest SMX removal rates are for the PMS/UV-C system followed by PS/UV-C and HP/UV-C. Overall, all of these references point out the importance of the results of this study, demonstrating the efficiency of photolytic activation of PMS and PS on the removal, at the same time, of different MPs in a very complex water matrix (real secondary effluent from WWTP) with low oxidant dosages and very low UV-C contact time (9 and 28 seconds). However, it is also demonstrated that the concentration of PS or PMS required for the removal of organic compounds changed with the types and initial concentration of organic compounds. This constitutes a great challenge for the treatment of real wastewater, and the optimal concentration of PS or PMS should be investigated when dealing with real wastewater (Wang & Wang, 2018).

# 3.2. Photocatalytic activation of PMS and PS with Fe(II)

Table 2 shows the results of MPs removal after the use of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ as an activator of PMS and PS in combination with UV-C radiation. In general, it can be observed that the addition of Fe(II) enhanced the MPs removal. However, because of the high abatement obtained in photolytic treatments, improvement by $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ was clearly observed over CBZ, DCF, and IBP only. For instance, in the case of CBZ, the PS/Fe(II)/UV-C $( 9 6 - 1 0 0 \% )$ ) system notably increased efficiency compared to the PS/UV-C system $( 4 7 - 7 9 \% )$ ). A significant enhancement was also found by adding $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ in the PS and PMS systems for IBP removal. In this case, in the absence of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ , photolytic treatments almost did not reduce the concentration of IBP; in the presence of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ , however, total abatement was observed under almost all of the operating conditions.

For the other target MPs (ATN, BPA, and SMX), abatement results by combination of Fe(II) with PMS and PS were quite similar to those obtained in the absence of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ . For ATN, CFN and SMX, total or almost total abatement was achieved. It is well known that the addition of a transition metal as an activator increases the generation rate of free radicals (Wang & Wang, 2018). The activation of both persulfate salts, PS and PMS, by metal ions is as follows:

$$
\mathrm { \bf S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { \bf O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - } + \mathrm { \bf M } ^ { \mathrm { n } } \longrightarrow \mathrm { \bf M } ^ { \mathrm { n } + 1 } + \mathrm { \bf S } \mathrm { \bf O } _ { 4 } { } ^ { \bullet - } + \mathrm { \bf S } \mathrm { \bf O } _ { 4 } { } ^ { 2 - }
$$

$$
\mathrm { H S O _ { 5 } } ^ { - } + \mathrm { M ^ { n } } \longrightarrow \mathrm { M ^ { n + 1 } } + \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - } + \mathrm { O H } ^ { - }
$$

While silver ion was proven to be the most efficient for activating PS (Anipsitakis & Dionysiou, 2004), for PMS activation, cobalt ion $( \mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + } )$ exhibited the best activated performance (Hu & Long, 2016). Although $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ is not the best activator, its use is strongly recommended because it is environmentally more friendly than $\mathrm { C o ( I I ) }$ or Ni(II), not showing significant differences in comparison with the use of $\mathrm { C o ( I I ) }$ (RodríguezChueca et al., 2017c). Some authors have reported the activation of PMS and PS with heterogeneous iron catalyst. For instance, Xia et al. (2017) achieved the successful removal of phenol in surface water and wastewater by PS activated by natural magnetic pyrrhotite, reaching a removal higher than in treatment without an iron activator, and higher than Fenton-like treatment ( $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } /$ natural pyrrhotite). In these treatments, the reaction time was $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , and no UV radiation was applied. Different authors have reported the activation mechanisms of PS and PMS with transition metal as an electron acceptor (Liang et al., 2009; Teel et al., 2011). It is suggested that the HO bond in the structure of PMS breaks. Because of the different structure of PS with PMS, PS cannot be effectively activated by metal as an electron acceptor (Liang et al., 2009).

However, as shown in Tables 1 and 2, the combination of PMS and PS with $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ is not the most suitable for treatments with a very low UV-C contact time (maximum 28 s), as it is not enough time to observe the rate increase in the generation of sulfate radicals as a consequence of Fe(II) addition.

Figure 1 compares the efficiency of all of the treatments through the use of the inverse of Electrical Energy per Order (1/EEO), defined as the number of kWh of electrical energy required to reduce the concentration of a pollutant by one order of magnitude $( 9 0 \% )$ (Bolton et al., 2001). The EEO $\mathrm { \ k W h } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ order) in continuous mode reactions can be calculated as follows:

$$
\begin{array} { r } { E _ { E O } = \frac { P } { F \cdot \log ( \frac { C _ { i } } { C _ { f } } ) } } \end{array}
$$

where $\mathrm { P }$ is the rated power $( \mathrm { k W } )$ of the AOP system; F is the flow rate $( \mathrm { m } ^ { 3 } / \mathrm { h } )$ ; and $\mathrm { C i }$ and Cf are the initial and final pollutant concentrations, respectively (Bolton et al., 2001). As observed in Figure 1, a clear trend does not exist regarding which is the most efficient treatment for all MPs. In general, It seems that the most efficient operating conditions are the use of $5 \ \mathrm { m M }$ dosages of oxidant and Fe(II) at $3 ~ \mathrm { L / m i n }$ (9 s UV-C contact time). However, the best conditions and treatment strongly depend on each target compound, and therefore the optimal process should be studied cases by case.

# 3.3. Degree of mineralization of organic matter

In terms of mineralization, Table 3 shows DOC removal. The highest DOC abatement was shown for PMS/Fe(II)/UV-C, with values ranging from 49 to $6 2 \%$ in all of the tested operating conditions, while DOC removal was slightly lower with the PS/Fe(II)/UV-C system $( 4 4 - 5 4 \% )$ ).

Moreover, in contrast with that observed in terms of MPs removal, clear differences existed regarding whether or not $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ is added, especially in the case of PMS. In the absence of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ , removal efficiency ranged from 17 to $2 7 \%$ using PMS, being slightly lower than when PS was used $( 2 7 - 5 4 \% )$ .

These high mineralization rates constitute a promising result. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that treatments were applied over real treated effluents, with other substances competing with free radicals, such as organic matter $\mathrm { ( C O D = 2 7 \pm 3 \ m g / L ) }$ ), suspended solids $\mathrm { \overline { { 5 } } \pm 2 \ m g / L } ,$ ), or faecal microorganisms $( E . \ c o l i \approx 1 0 ^ { 4 } - 1 0 ^ { 5 }$ CFU/mL). Indeed, mineralization values could correspond only to the degradation of other organic substances present in water, making it necessary to examine the oxidation by-products generated after these UV-C-based AOPs. Besides, toxicity monitoring should be conducted to assess the global effect of these SR-AOPs over the final treated effluent.

# 3.4. Transformation products analysis and proposed reaction mechanism

TPs formed along with MPs photoxidation systems were detected via HPLC–MS. Table 4 shows the occurrence of transformation products in each tested treatment (HP/UV-C; PMS/UV-C; PS/UV-C; PMS/Fe(II)/UV-C and $\mathrm { P S / F e ( I I ) / U V { - } C ) }$ and under all operating conditions.

Figure 2 presents the different transformation products detected after the five proposed oxidation treatments for atenolol degradation (ATN). The identification of TPs was performed on the basis of the MSn data and in agreement with a previous work (Medana et al., 2008). The three different TPs (m/z 315.1558, m/z 283.1658, and m/z 134.1173) do not appear in all of the proposed treatments. However, all of them were detected after photolysis of hydrogen peroxide and peroxymonosulfate. The lightest compound $( m / z$ 134.1173) was detected after all of the treatments. This compound comes from the molecule cleavage by the ether bond. For the other treatments, two TPs were identified for each treatment.

Six different transformation products were detected (see Figure 3) in the degradation of bisphenol A (BPA). According to Table 4, four of them (m/z 273.0748, m/z 257.0859 A, $m / z 1 4 9 . 0 6 0 5$ , and m/z 135.0472) were detected in all of the treatments. These two lightest compounds are the consequence of molecule rupture at the level of quaternary carbon among the two aromatic rings. It is important to note the occurrence of a transformation product (m/z 165.0432), exclusive of the PMS/Fe(II)/UV-C system, formed after the breakage of the BPA and the incorporation of a hydroxyl group. Sharma et al. (2015)

reported the different transformation products and the degradation mechanisms after the UV-C photoactivation of PMS, being only the product with $m / z ~ 2 5 7$ detected in the present study. In recent a recent work on the photocatalytic degradation of BPA with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and Ce-ZnO (Fabbri et al, 2019) two of the intermediates here reported (m/z 273.0748 and 165.0432) were also identified.

Eleven transformation products were detected after degradation treatment of carbamazepine (CBZ), as shown in Figure 4. Structural attribution was performed in agreement with the data present in the literature (Calza et al., 2012). There were four intermediates formed during all of the treatments, and two compounds were exclusively detected during PS/UV-C treatments.

After the application of treatments, caffeine (CFN) was transformed in four different intermediate compounds (see Figure 5). One of them $( m / z ~ 1 4 4 . 0 7 6 6 )$ is common to all of the applied treatments and is formed as a consequence of the rupture of the imidazole ring. Also as a consequence of the rupture of the imidazole ring, but conserving both amine groups, an intermediate compound (m/z 201.0985) appeared after the application of the PMS/UV-C system. Intermediates at m/z 170.0560 are the only ones that preserved the imidazole ring and involved the rupture in the pyrimidine ring. This compound was detected after photolysis of hydrogen peroxide and peroxymonosulfate. Finally, the lightest compound $( m / z ~ 1 2 8 . 0 4 5 2 )$ was specific to persulfate treatments, appearing after the photolysis of PMS and PS, and their activation with Fe(II).

Figure 6 shows two transformation products identified in the degradation of DCF. Both TPs (m/z 232.0563 and m/z 166.0604) were detected after the performance of all of the tested oxidation treatments. However, there were no specific intermediates found for any treatment.

In the case of IBP, the MS data, in agreement with the information available in the literature, allowed us to detect and identify 14 different intermediates (see Figure 7) (Michael et al., 2014; Rao et al., 2016; Xiang et al., 2016; Fabbri et al., 2019). As can be seen in Table 4, nine out 14 of these intermediates are common to all of the applied treatments. Rao et al. (2016) reported the detected transformation products after the application of PMS activation by Fe(III) and irradiation with UV-C.

Finally, only one intermediate compound (m/z 110.0597) was detected after sulfamethoxazole (SMX) degradation. This intermediate compound is the cation of oaminophenol, and is common for all oxidation treatments, both hydroxyl and sulfate radicals oxidation (Figure 8).

# 4. Conclusions

From the present study on the efficacy and transformation products of different hydroxyl and sulfate radical advanced oxidation processes on the degradation of seven MPs detected in real wastewater samples, the following conclusions can be drawn:

- UV-C-driven SR-AOPs have been proven as a successful technology to reduce micropollutants concentration in treated wastewater effluents. This technology can be easily applied in current UV-C reactors to improve the efficiency of the tertiary treatment for MPs removal for regeneration and water reuse purposes.

The global efficiency of the MPs degradation is very variable. Some compounds, such as ATN and CFN, were easily removed $( 8 4 - 1 0 0 \% )$ ) with low oxidant dosages (0.5 mM) and very low UV-C contact time (9 s). In contrast, other MPs such as BPA, CBZ, DCF, IBP, and SMX presented a removal efficiency strongly dependant on the oxidation system and operating conditions (dose and reaction time). Therefore, system operation in real process should be optimized depending on the MPs chosen to monitor the process efficiency.

As a general trend, the relative order of efficiency of the studied processes is: $\mathrm { P M S / U V - C } > \mathrm { P S / U V - C } > \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { U V - C }$ . The addition of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ as a catalyst clearly enhanced the removal of those compounds that show low removal efficiencies by the direct photolysis of oxidants, allowing the almost total degradation of compounds such as CBZ, DCF, IBP, and SMX. Only in the case of BPA, similar or even lower efficiencies were obtained in the presence of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ .

Finally, different transformation products were identified during the degradation of each MP depending on the oxidation treatment. The use of sulfate radicals leds to the generation of specific intermediates that suggest different degradation pathways in comparison with hydroxyl radicals based processes. This could have an impact on the evolution of the toxicity during the process that should be carefully analysed in future studies.
