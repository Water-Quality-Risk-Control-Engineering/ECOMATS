# . Introduction

The side effect of the continues usage of organics (i.e., antibiotics and raw materials) is the release of these organics into the aquatic system. Most of these organic compounds have detrimental effects to the environmental ecosystems and human lives due to their destructive characteristics and toxic properties [13]. Among the possible treatment methods, persulfate (PS)-based technologies are believed to be an effective solution in the decontamination of organic pollutants. Depending on the structure, PS can be found as peroxymonosulfate (PMS, $\mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ or peroxydisulfate (PDS, $S _ { 2 } 0 _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - \cdot }$ Both PMS $( 1 . 8 2 \mathrm { ~ V _ { N H E } ) }$ and PDS $( 2 . 0 1 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { N H E } } )$ illustrate adequate redox potentials allowing them to directly oxidize organics with electron-rich groups [4]. However, compared with reactive species with higher redox potential (i.e., radicals), the oxidation strength of PS without activation is inferior. Between the possible activation methods, metal (Me)-based catalytic activation shows better efficiency compared to external energy-based activation methods. Meanwhile, heterogeneous catalyst for PS activation is preferred due to its lower susceptibility to environmental influences and the ease of separation and recovery of the heterogeneous catalyst from the treated solution.

Catalytic activation of PS is generally complex and occurs through radical and nonradical pathways in which the activation mechanism of PS depends on the type of catalyst used. Over the last few years, research on the incorporation of Me into carbonaceous materials (Me-C) along with heteroatom (HA) as catalyst for PS activation is emerging as it provides delicate dispersion of the Meatoms and allows a higher exposure of catalytic active Me-sites compared with the agglomerated pristine Me-catalyst [18]. Also, the conductivity of the carbonaceous materials and high specific surface area [19] enhances the electron-transfer processes and promotes better interactions between the Me-sites and the reactants. Meanwhile, HA such as nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and boron, has attracted significant interest due to its effectiveness in enhancing the catalytic activity and properties of carbonaceous catalyst. These heteroatoms are capable of bestowing the catalyst surface with catalytic active sites, tuning the electronic properties, and enhancing the catalytic activity of the carbocatalyst [20]. Recently, several experimental and computational studies illustrate that metal-heteroatoms-carbon (Me-HA-C) materials have enhanced catalytic performance, in which the catalytic reactivity is ascribed to a synergetic effect between the components of the Me-HA-C [2124]. Ma et al. [25] provided an overview on the application of MeN-C materials on PS activation. Other than that, there is a lack in literature on the synergy between the HA (i.e., N, O, S, B) and Me in Me-HAC materials during PS activation. In view of the recent interest in creating Me-HA-C catalyst as PS activator, examination of the synergy of various Me-HA-C catalysts is necessary to showcase the potential of these catalysts as PS activator.

Herein, we provide as short review as an attempt to understand the interaction between Me and HA co-doping on carbocatalysts and review the performance of Me-HA-C catalysts for PS activation. This paper will specifically discuss the effects of the Me-coordination environments, HA effect on the Me-electronic properties, and HA influences on the catalytic PS activation pathway with focus on the synergy between the HA and Me during catalytic performance. This work is believed to shed light on the synergetic effects in Me-HA-C during PS activation and provide valuable insights for future studies.

# 2Interaction in Me-HA-C

# 2.1. Me-N-C

N-doping has been widely used to elevate the catalytic performance of carbocatalyst [26]. Generally, N-doping can (i) increase the electron mobility throughout the carbon lattice, (ii) facilitate the interactivity between the carbocatalyst and PS for its adsorption, (iii) enhance the electron donating capacity of the carbocatalyst, (iv) induce electron deficiency in the adjacent C atoms, and (v) serve as active sites for the radical and nonradical activation of PS [20]. N-doping can introduce various N moieties, including graphitic N, pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, and N oxides moieties into the carbon structure [27]. Among the different N bonding configurations, pyridinic N is believed to be the most befitting bonding configuration to effectively trap the Me to form Me-N-C bonds [7,2831]. Graphitic N is unlikely to directly coordinate with Me as the N in graphitic N is saturated with three C atoms. Similarly, N in N oxides is constrained by the neutralizing effect of adjacent O atoms on N [32], and the congestion of the N oxide sites hinders the ability of Me to form coordination bond.

The virtue of the relatively high electronegativity of N $\dot { \left( \chi _ { \mathrm { N } } = 3 . 0 4 \right) }$ allows the Me (relatively electropositive) to easily coordinate with the N rather than the C atoms $( \chi _ { \mathrm { C } } = 2 . 5 5 )$ at low Me loading [13]. Therefore, catalysts with high N content (e.g. graphitic carbon nitride, $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } .$ can better serve in trapping and coordinating with the Me, enhancing the overall dispersibility of the Me over the catalyst surface. Additionally, formation of Me-N-C allows to alter the electron localization/delocalization states of the bonded metal [33], promotes better dispersion of the Me on the carbocatalyst surface and may hinder the leaching of Me due to the stronger bonding with the electronegative N [34]. N-doping can also reduce the Me particle size. For instance, introducing N was able to reduce the particle size of Fe from nanoparticle clusters to atomically dispersed Fe [35]. Generally, when the metal loading is excessive, the metallic atoms are more likely to form agglomerates and nanoparticles (Me-Me interactions). For instance, Wang et al. [36] prepared a bimetallic (Co and Fe) doped $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ and found that Co with a relatively low loading $( 3 . 7 \ \mathrm { a t } . \% )$ formed single atomic configuration whilst Fe with higher loading $( 7 . 6 \mathrm { a t . } \% )$ formed ultrasmall nanoclusters. Regardless, in each case, the function of the N-atoms will differ, and different synergetic effects may occur.

As for the influence of the coordination states of $\mathrm { C o - N _ { X } - C }$ on the activation pathway, low coordination Co $\left( \mathbf { C o - N _ { 3 } - C } \right)$ was found to activate PMS to degrade norfloxacin (NOR) via the formation of high-valent ${ \mathrm { C o } } ( \mathrm { I V } ) { = } 0$ species $( 5 6 . 7 5 \%$ contribution) and nonradical electron transfer $( 4 1 . 2 2 \%$ contribution), while $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ was generated yet without contributing to the degradation of NOR [39]. In another study, $\mathrm { C o - N _ { 4 } \mathrm { - C } }$ was found to have the affinity of activating PMS via the formation of high-valent Co(IV) and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ [46]. This may indicate that for $\scriptstyle \mathbf { C o - N _ { X } - C } /$ PMS system, high-valent Co(IV) species and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ pathways are more likely to occur in $\mathrm { C o - N } _ { 4 }$ -C, whilst the direct electron transfer may take place with low coordination $\mathrm { C o - N _ { 3 ^ { - } } C }$ as a result of the enhancement of the electron density of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { N } } _ { 3 }$ sites [39] and promoted adsorption of PMS. Also, both $\mathrm { F e - N _ { x } { \mathrm { - C / P M S } } }$ and $\mathbf { C o - N _ { x } { - C / P M S } }$ systems appear to have less contribution of radicals $( S { \bf O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ and $\textstyle \cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } } $ , with higher likeliness of formation of high-valent oxo species, $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 } ,$ and direct electron transfer.

Hence, delicate control of the coordination states can serve as a potential technique to control the activation pathway. The influence of the coordination states on the activation pathway remains limitedly explored. The aforementioned theories are difficult to prove as the reaction conditions, such as the electronic properties of the target organic pollutant [47] and the $\mathsf { p H }$ of the reaction mixture may affect the activation pathway [48]. The spin states of Me are also capable of influencing the catalytic activation pathway. For instance, Zhang et al. realized that the high spin state of $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) { - } N _ { 4 } }$ $S = 2 ,$ preferably activate PMS via a radical pathway whereas Fe(III) $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { 4 } }$ $( S = 5 / 2$ are prone to activate PMS through high-valent Fe-species formation [49]. Therefore, detailed investigation on the effect of the coordination state on the activation pathway through both computational and experimental methods is needed.

As for other metals, Cu has shown satisfactory potential in coordinating with N for PMS activation. Cu coordination with N sites can lower the band gap the of the carbocatalyst [29] which in turn facilitates the electron excitation/migration and enhances the chemical reactivity [6,52]. For example, in an Cu-N-C catalyst, the promoted catalytic activity is accredited to the $\mathrm { C u - N }$ bonding which facilitates the anchoring of Cu-atoms. Also, the synergy between the N- and Cu-atoms was explained by the facilitated transfer of charge carriers from the $\mathrm { N } 2 \mathrm { p }$ orbital to the Cu3d orbital in which the Fermi level was risen, stipulating easier electron transfer in the Cu-modified N-doped biochar compared with the pristine N-doped biochar [52]. Meanwhile, Mn sites in Mn-N-C may serve as PMS adsorption sites. For example, PMS adsorption on Mn$\mathrm { N } _ { 4 ^ { - } } \mathrm { C }$ sites is preferable over adsorption on graphitic N sites [53]. Mn-NC bonds can form electron-rich Mn-sites and foster electron transfer via $\mathrm { M n } ( \mathrm { I I } ) – \pi$ interactions which in time promotes the adsorption of the oxidant molecules [54].

In details, high spin state $\tt M e { \mathrm { - } } \tt N$ sites with greater $\mu _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ promoted coupling with O-containing adsorbates (i.e. PMS and PDS) on the Mesites, which in turn facilitated PS adsorption and expedites electrontransfer. The inconsistency of the catalytic strength between Fe-N-C and Co-N-C in the studies can be attributed to their affinity towards specific reactive species generation. To explain, Gao et al. [55] realized that Fe-N-C was catalytically superior to Co-N-C in in PMS activation when both the catalysts experienced a major contribution of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ for the degradation of bisphenol A (BPA). On the contrary, Miao et al. [56] concluded that Co-N-C was more effective in activating PMS than Fe-N-C (for sulfamethoxazole degradation) when the major reaction pathway was a nonradical electron-transfer one. The dissimilarity in the PMS activation pathway is due to the difference in the carbonaceous allotropes used in each study and the variation in the studied pollutants [47]. Therefore, it can be safely assumed that different Me in Me-N-C will lead to different reactive species formation which will affect the overall catalytic reactivity. Nevertheless, experimental and computational exploration on the effect of Me-choice on the activation pathway of PS using Me-N- is a necessity.

Conversely, when the Me and N are present on the same carbon framework without a direct Me—N bond, an indirect synergy arises. For instance, in a Fe-N-C/PMS catalytic system, pyrrolic N was found to serve as an adsorption site for the organic pollutants which will reduce the mass transfer distance between the organic pollutants and the reactive species generated on the Fe-sites [57]. The migration distance of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ , $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } } ,$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ is estimated to be limited to only $5 4 8 \mathrm { n m }$ $7 3 \mathrm { n m }$ and $9 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively [58]. As a result, the adsorbed organics on pyrrolic N sites near the Fe-atoms in an Fe-N-C catalyst will facilitate productive reactions between these reactive species and the organic pollutant [59]. Similar observations on the synergy between pyrrolic N and Me-sites were found in Mn-N-C/PMS [53] and Ni-N-C/PMS [60] catalytic systems.

In an Mn/Fe-N-C catalyst, graphitic N can activate the adjacent Catoms by electron attraction which will allow the formation of metastable reactive PMS complexes on these C-atoms whilst the ${ \mathrm { M n / F e } }$ species will serve as radical pathway initiators (i.e. $\mathrm { { s O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet } }$ , $s 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet }$ , and $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } , }$ ) via Me redox cycles [61]. Similarly, graphitic N with electron deficient neighboring C-atoms was claimed to be the active site for $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ generation, whereas the Fe-sites were believed to serve as the radical pathway active site [35]. Similar observations were made in a Cu-N-C/PDS system [62]. Overall, it can be stated that in a Me-N-C catalyst in which the Me- and N-atoms are unbonded directly: pyrrolic $_ \mathrm { N }$ facilitate the adsorption of the organic for proximity with the reactive species, graphitic N will activate adjoining C for PMS adsorption and may serve as $\bar { 1 } _ { \mathrm { O _ { 2 } } }$ generation site after amplifying the electron transfer capacity of the catalyst, and the Me atoms will activate PMS to generate radicals, $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ,$ and/or high-valent oxo species. In other words, each constitute will serve separately as an active site resulting in an overall synergy bringing rise to an enhanced catalytic performance.

# 2.2. Me-O-C

Oxygen-containing functional groups such as carbonyl groups · $\scriptstyle ( \mathbf { C } = \mathbf { O } )$ , hydroxyl groups (C-OH), and carboxylic groups (COOH) are capable of effectively activating PS [63,64]. Other than solely serving as catalytic sites, the electronegativity of O surpasses those of C and N (χo $= 3 . 4 4 > \chi _ { \mathrm { N } } = 3 . 0 4 > \chi _ { \mathrm { C } } = 2 . 5 5 )$ which allows O to strongly bind and coordinate with Me atoms. For example, the formation energy of ${ \mathrm { C u } } { - } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ was found to be negative $\left( - 1 . 0 8 ~ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ indicating that Cu coordination with O is thermodynamically favorable [65]. In fact, Duan et al. [66] found that unlike the conventional Fe-Nx-C structure, Fe prefer to coordinate with both N and O atoms. Peculiarly, the formation energy of Fe $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } } \mathbf { - C }$ $\left( - 5 . 6 3 1 \mathrm { e V } \right.$ and  5.636 eV) was found to be significantly lower than that of the conventional Fe- $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { 4 } }$ -C $\left( - 2 . 7 7 \mathrm { e V } \right)$ stipulating the enhanced feasibility of O in coordinating with and trapping the Me. Similarly, the formation energy of $\mathrm { C o - N } _ { 4 } \left( \mathrm { - } 1 . 8 7 \ : \mathrm { e V } \right)$ was reduced in Co$\mathrm { N } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 1 }$ $( - 2 . 5 3 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ signifying the enhanced feasibility of Me-O-C formation [67]. However, despite the viability of Me-O-C bonding formation, coordination of the Me-atoms solely with O-atoms in a carbocatalyst at low Me loading is unlikely to occur. This is simply because the complete surrounding and coordination of the Me with only O will yield Me oxides (e.g. $\mathrm { C o _ { 3 } O _ { 4 } }$ , $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ ) which will be integrated onto the carbocatalyst as a secondary phase forming a heterojunction rather than an integrated MeO-C system. This is especially true as the formation energy of Me oxides is significantly lower than that of Me-HA-C [67]. Therefore, O has a better merit when the Me-atoms are co-coordinated with the O and other HAs (i.e. N, B) in the Me-O-C catalyst.

As for the catalytic activation mechanism, the electronegativity of O and electropositivity of Me in the Me-O-C structure can create electron poor and electron rich moieties and electron centers which allows a facile movement of electrons throughout the Me-O-C bonding, giving rise to a potential to earn and lose electrons. The consecutive movement of electrons enhances the interconversion of $\mathbf { M e ^ { ( n + 1 ) + } / M e ^ { n + } }$ for continual PMS activation and reactive species generation. Nonetheless, when the electrons cease from movement, the Me-O-C bond is cleaved, and O will rearrange to form $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = O / C - O H }$ moieties. This process also promotes PDS activation for reactive species formation [68]. In an Fe/ Co-O-C (Fe/Co-O co-doped $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } )$ , $\mathrm { O } { - } g { - } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ would serve as an electron transfer facilitator, allowing the electron migration between PMS molecules and Fe(II) and promote recycling of Co(II)/Co(III) and Fe(II)/ Fe(III) for continues catalytic activation.

Furthermore, O doping induces a positive charge on the adjacent C which may stimulate a nucleophilic attack by the $\mathrm { o - } \mathrm { O }$ bond of PMS (as electron donor) on the electron deficient C for the formation of $s 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ and subsequent generation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ [22]. The presence of oxygen vacancies $\left( \mathbf { O } _ { \mathbf { v } } \right)$ against lattice oxygen $\left( \mathrm { O } _ { \mathrm { L } } \right)$ can induce fruitful catalytic effects. In a Co-O-C/PMS system, the reactive species contribution was in order of: ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } > \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { * } > \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { * } > { } ^ { * } \mathrm { O H } .$ After catalytic oxidation, the ratio of $\mathrm { { O } _ { V } / \mathrm { { O } _ { L } } }$ was reduced from 1.56 to 0.56 due to the involvement of the $\mathrm { O _ { V } }$ in converting $\boldsymbol { 0 } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ to $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . $\mathrm { O } _ { \mathrm { v } }$ is also believed to facilitate the formation of additional Co(II) to achieve charge neutrality. More importantly, Co-O-C bond can be employed as electron centers due to the formation of electron-poor/rich centers [69]. A similar conclusion was made in an FeO-C/PS system in which $\mathrm { O } _ { \mathrm { v } }$ were claimed to enhance the Fe(II)/Fe(III) redox cycles [70].

# 2.3. Me-S-C

When S is doped into a Me-C composite to form an Me-S-C composite, S-functional groups can react solo to activate PS or may directly interact with the Me. In the former case, sulfur can form thiophenic bonds (C-SC) and oxides $\left( \mathbf { S O _ { x } } \right)$ . Thiophenic sulfur is known for its catalytic activity, whilst sulfur oxides have limited catalytic performance due to its finite electronic transport capacity as a result of the electron withdrawing characteristics of $\mathrm { { { S O } _ { x } } }$ groups [20,71]. As for C-S-C, its coexistence with Me sites in one carbon framework may lead to a synergetic effect or isolated catalytic activation process by each of these active sites. C-S-C species can serve in an isolated form as electron donors to PS while Me is separately reacting with PS via redox cycles. In this case, C-S-C sites will donate electrons to PS for the breakage of the O—O bond and formation of radicals (i.e. $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ ) [72]. On the other hand, when C-S-C moieties serve as viaduct to facilitate the movement of electrons, it allows the Meatoms to better interact with the PS species for electron transfer and the expeditious breakage of the $\mathrm { o - } 0$ bond of PMS. The $\mathrm { C } { \cdot } \mathrm { S } { \cdot } \mathrm { C } / { \cal S } _ { \mathrm { t o t } }$ content was found to be the main contributor to the catalytic activity for

PMS activation with an excellent linear correlation $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 5 3 1 )$ against the reaction rate (Fig. 2d) [23]. Worthy of note, sulfur content must be carefully controlled. Several studies have reported that excessive S content may cause detrimental effects to the catalytic performance of carbocatalysts by restricting the adsorption capacity of the catalyst [73] and breaking the charge balance of the carbonaceous framework [74]. The need of finding the optimum S content has been addressed elsewhere [32].

The second case of which the S moieties directly interact with Me occurs when the S is bonded as $\ S ^ { 2 - }$ , $S _ { 2 } ^ { 2 - }$ and $S _ { \mathrm { n } } ^ { 2 - } \left( n > 2 \right)$ species in the Me-S-C. Particularly, these S moieties with high reducibility will selfsacrifice to reduce the oxidized Me-species during catalytic PMS activation for enhanced recycling of the Me redox loops and continuous catalytic reactions. Studies on Me-S-C are in consensus that S-moieties are able to promote the conversion between Me-redox cycles [23,72,7578]. Taking Fe-S-C and Co-S-C as examples, the reaction with the sulfur moieties and the Me atoms in water is illustrated in Eqs. (12) (15) [23,72,77]:

# 2.4. Me-B-C

Typically, B is an electron-deficient atom $( \chi _ { \mathrm { B } } = 2 . 0 4 )$ . This allows the doped B to be employed as a donor for $\sigma$ -electrons and an acceptor for $\pi$ -electrons [20]. The doped B in carbonaceous materials can generate several moieties such as $\mathrm { B C } _ { 3 }$ $\mathrm { B C _ { 2 } O }$ , and $\mathbf { B C O _ { 2 } } ,$ to which all these species are conducive towards PS activation [27,80]. In the presence of metallic species (Me-B-C), B may also bind and coordinate with the Me or activate PS solely. For instance, in a Fe-B-C/PMS system, $\mathrm { B C _ { 2 } O }$ and $\mathrm { B C O } _ { 2 }$ moieties were the major active site for BPA removal with an excellent linear correlation $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 6 7 7 1 )$ between the removal rate $( \mathbf { k _ { 0 } } \mathbf { _ { 0 } } \mathbf { _ { 0 } } )$ and the $\mathbf { B } _ { \mathrm { B C 2 O + B C O 2 } } / \mathbf { B } _ { \mathrm { t o t a l } }$ (Fig. 3b). The enhanced catalytic activity after B doping was ascribed to B moieties serving as Lewis acid sites to facilitate the interactivity between the catalyst surface and PMS enhancing the formation of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ [79]. B doping can also cause an increment in the valence electrons of the Me-atoms in an Me-B-C [81].

Me can also directly coordinate and bind with B. For instance, in a FeB-C catalyst, Fe—B bond was detected and was ascribed to the formation of a $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 } B O _ { 6 } }$ phase [24]. However, in the presence of O, the Me is unlikely to coordinate only with B, but co-coordinate with both the O- and Batoms. Other than the previous ${ \mathrm { F e } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { B O } } _ { 6 }$ example, the formation energy of $\mathrm { C u - O _ { 3 ^ { - } } C }$ $( - 0 . 9 9 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ declined after B modification in $\mathrm { C u - O _ { 2 } B { - } C }$ (-2.47 eV) signifying better stability and thermodynamic feasibility. When the remaining O atoms were substituted with B in $\mathrm { C u } { \cdot } \mathrm { B } _ { 3 }$ -C, the formation energy increased $( - 1 . 5 7 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ due to relatively lower stability of the structure [65]. This may be ascribed to the weak electronegativity of B, limiting its capacity to fully and solely coordinate the Me. Nevertheless, the formation energy was negative, indicating a certain degree of thermodynamic feasibility.

B can also modify the coordination environment of Me. Other than substituting the adjacent elements of the Me, B can affect the bonding behavior of the Me. For instance, B-doping in an Fe-N,B-C was found to increase the bond length of the Fe—N bond from 1.99 to $2 . 0 3 \mathring \mathrm { \mathrm { A } }$ [82]. Moreover, B in Me-B-C are capable of enhancing PMS adsorption and prolonging its $\mathrm { o - } 0$ bond length, indicating an easier decomposing of PMS molecules for the formation of reactive species and superior catalytic activity [24]. The PMS adsorption selectivity can also be modified by B doping. Introducing B into a Cu-O-C catalyst $\mathbf { \vec { C } u - O _ { 2 } B - C } )$ enhanced the adsorption selectivity for the terminal O atoms of PMS as a result of B tuning the electronic structure of Cu, causing a better decomposing of PMS (Fig. 3c) [65].

Lastly, the presence of B can switch the PS activation pathway. This mainly occurs due to the introduced B-sites altering the interactions between the catalyst's surface and PS molecules. In particular, B-sites serve as electronrecipients which will facilitate the migration of electrons the the adsorbed PS molecules. Also, adsorbed PS molecules on B-sites (i.e. $- \mathrm { B C } _ { 3 } ,$ . $\cdot \mathbf { B } \mathbf { C } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O }$ $\mathbf { - B C O _ { 2 } } \mathbf { \mathrm { _ { 2 } } }$ experience elongated O—O bond which indicates that likeliness of facile decomposition of the adsorbed PS for selective reactive species generation [24]. The carbocatalyst co-doped with Fe and N demonstrates superior efficiency compared to N co-doped with other metals, mainly due to the presence of multiple different active sites (e.g., Fe—N coupling, graphitic, pyrrolic, pyridinic, and oxidized N) for PS activation [99]. Subsequently, the multiple active sites (mainly Fe- $\mathbf { N _ { X } }$ sites) alter the surface electronic structure of Fe-N-C and significantly improve both its conductivity and catalytic activity [100]. Moreover, Fe is relatively nontoxic compared to other transition metals, reducing the risk of secondary contamination due to Fe leaching. Besides, Co-N-C was observed to remove ${ \sim } 9 9 . 9 \%$ tetracycline within $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ through nonradical pathway as the dominant route [87]. Furthermore, the presence of additional metals in conjunction with a single metal type and N to form a bimetallic carbocatalyst could produce additional active sites for PS activation to achieve better degradation performance. For instance, the addition of Co to Fe-N-C may produce additional $\mathrm { C o - N }$ active sites along Fe—N and N moieties, which results in the ${ \sim } 9 2 . 6 \%$ removal of tetracycline within $4 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ [101].

In Me-S-C, the radical pathway is generally predominant and this could be associated with the role of S-containing active sites in co-doped carbocatalyst as electron donors (due to the extra electron possessed by S) causing the breakage PDS and PMS to form radicals [102]. For example, the introduction of S to Fe-containing carbon material (Fe-S-C) promoted electron movement, accelerated the redox cycle of Fe and consequently enhanced the overall efficiency of the PDS catalytic system for oxytetracycline removal [75]. Meanwhile, in Me-O-C, a nonradical pathway is often preferred for the degradation of pollutants such as tetracycline [68], oxytetracycline [68], ciprofloxacin [65], and ofloxacin [69]. This is mainly attributed to the presence of Me-O-C bonds, abundant oxygen vacancies, $\mathbf { \Delta } \mathbf { M e { - } } \mathbf { O } _ { \mathbf { x } }$ sites.

During catalytic PS activation, reusability potential and durability of the catalyst's surface chemistry is vital for obvious sustainability purposes. Generally, the decline in catalytic activity of Me-HA-C can mainly be attributed to (i) the oxidation of the carbon lattice and the heteroatoms moieties by excessive PS molecules [103], (ii) the blockage of the active sites by the pollutants by-products [32], and (iii) the leaching of metallic content. The undesirable oxidation of the catalyst surface sites can be controlled by managing the PS dosage to guarantee optimum PS consumption and to avoid the poising effects by the excessive oxidativereactions. On the other hand, the blockage of the active sites due to the adsorption of the degradation by-products can be controlled by optimizing reaction conditions and prolonging the reaction time to ensure complete mineralization of the pollutants. This leaves the major obstacle of the inevitable leaching of metallic content of the catalyst. Despite that leaching of Me-atoms ability to enhance the overall catalytic performance due to the contribution of homogeneous catalysis, it will still lower the reusability potential of the catalyst and draw us further away from sustainability. It has been claimed that co-doping Me-atoms with heteroatoms, specifically at single-atom configuration, can minimize the dissolution of Me-ions and enhance the overall stability and durability of the catalyst [104]. For example, Zhang et al. [38] found that Fe-N4-C configuration demonstrated better reusability potential compared to Fenanoparticles' configuration in $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ in degrading p-chlorophenol via PMS activation. In another study, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { N } } _ { 3 }$ moieties where found to be unchanged (using X-ray absorption spectroscopy and HADDF-HRTEM) after catalytic PMS activation in which neither agglomeration of Coatoms into Co clusters nor changes in Co valence states were noticed [45] indicating superb catalytic stability. Similarly, the presence of Band N-moieties in an Fe-doped porous carbon catalyst was found to lower the leaching of Fe-ions from 2.9 to $1 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ during catalytic PMS activation [79].

Overall, it is undeniable that Me-HA-C possesses better catalytic performance and lower environmental risk compared to Me-based catalysts in order to remove organic pollutants via PMS activation. However, instead of concentrating on the amount of metal or heteroatom dopant to be used in Me-HA-C, priority has to be given to the investigation of the correlation between different metal-heteroatom combinations and their PS activation mechanisms. This is to identify the best metal-heteroatom combination for PS activation and development strategy to prepare Me-HA-C as usual research directions concentrated on identifying the optimal metal dopant needed for Me-HA-C for maximum catalytic performance.

# Conclusion and prospects

In this work, we provided a concise review on the catalytic performance of Me-HA-C materials for PS activation. The specific focus of this study was on the synergy between the Me- and HA-species during catalytic PMS/PDS activation. In summary, the synergetic effects in MeN-C were found to be: N can (i) tune the electronic state of the Me, (ii) enhance the dispersion of the Me on the catalyst surface and minimize leaching, (iii) lower the Me particle size, and (iv) coordinate with Me at different coordination states which in turn affects the catalytic activity, PS adsorption efficiency, and activation pathway. In Me-O-C, O-atoms may: (i) aid in trapping the Me, (ii) enhance internal electron movement for effective Me-redox cycles by forming electron-poor/rich centers, and (iii) facilitate electron transfer between the Me-sites and PMS molecules. As for Me-S-C, S-species can (i) facilitate electron movement for productive reactivity with PMS molecules, and (ii) enhance the redox cycles of the Me for longer catalytic reactivity. Lastly, B in Me-B-C can (i) directly coordinate with the Me-atoms with the aid of other electronegative HA (i.e., N and O), (ii) increase the valence state of the Meatoms, (iii) enhance PS adsorption selectivity, and (iv) switch the activation pathway between radical and nonradical pathways. Evidently, Me-HA-C catalysts have conspicuous potential in the catalytic activation of PS, yet a more comprehensive understating and detailed investigation into vital research directions must be conducted. These research directions include:

4.1 Tuning the coordination states: as illustrated in this work, the coordination states of the Me-atoms in Me-HA-C can significantly vary from $\mathbf { M e } _ { 6 } – \mathbf { H A – C }$ to $\mathrm { M e } _ { 2 } – \mathrm { H A – C }$ , especially with N coordination. Each coordination state has different properties with distinct catalytic affinities. Also, coordination with multi-heteroatoms is feasible and can lead to different catalytic behavior. Hence, detailed investigations by experimental and computational methods should be conducted to analyze the favorability of different coordination states and how each coordination state will yield a distinct selectivity for specific PS activation pathways.

4.2 Employing multi-heteroatoms: the synergetic effects between multi-heteroatoms doping on the catalytic performance of carbocatalysts in undeniable [27,74,117,118]. Therefore, testing the synergetic effects of co- or tri-heteroatoms doping with the presence of Me-species may be of practical use. Yet, the coexistence of several species may lead to multiplex complex reactions between the HAs and the Me and competitive behavior over bonding sites might take place. Peculiarly, the HAs (i.e. N, S, B) are relatively electronegative whilst the Me (e.g. Co, Fe) are relatively electropositive. In the case of having multiheteroatoms and metal/s in one carbonaceous framework, the electronegative HA-atoms might compete to bind and stabilize the electropositive Me-atoms. This may lead to different bonding configurations of the Me-atoms and having multi-heteroatoms binding with one Me-atom (e.g. $\mathrm { C u - O _ { 2 } B }$ C). Whether such intra-actions will result in constructive or destructive effects to the catalytic performance remains ambiguous. Therefore, an exhaustive exploration of the elemental intra-actions will be needed. Note worthily, as B doping predominantly illustrates $\mathrm { B C O } _ { 2 } /$ $\mathrm { B C _ { 2 } O }$ species, it is productively able to graft O-atoms into the catalyst. Therefore, it should be considered to utilize the B oxides moieties in pushing the Me to specifically coordinate with the O-atoms.

4.3 Exploring new heteroatoms/metals: there are several HAs and Me options that can be employed to investigate their potential in bringing rise to a synergistic effect. For instance, metals such Mo [119], V [120], and Mn [121] displayed great potential in PS activation. Nonetheless, investigation on the synergy between these metals with HA is limitedly explored. Similarly, doping carbocatalysts with heteroatoms such as P [122] and F [123] can enhance the catalytic activity of the modified materials. In particular, F-atom has the highest electronegativity in the periodic table. This may allow it to firmly bind with the Me and trap it in the carbonaceous framework. Also, F-doping is claimed to be able to switch the degradation pathway of Me-based catalysis [124]. Regardless, in-depth investigation into the potential of novel Me-HA-C should be conducted.
