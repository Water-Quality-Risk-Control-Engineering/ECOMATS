# A B S T R A C T

Keywords: Graphitic carbon nitride N-doped porous carbon Peroxymonosulfate Peroxydisulfate Electron transfer

Metal-free activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) has been reported to be more efficient than peroxydisulfate (PDS) due to the asymmetrical structure of PMS. However, we report for the first time that the nitrogen-doped porous carbon (NC-900) templated from graphitic carbon nitride exhibits unexpectedly higher catalytic activity in the activation of PDS than PMS, as evidenced by 1.74-fold greater rate constant of NC-900/PDS $( 0 . 8 0 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 } ) .$ ) than that of NC-900/PMS $( 0 . 4 6 \mathrm { \ m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ ). Moreover, NC-900 shows superior reactivity and stability to existing nitrogen-doped nanocarbons in persulfate activation. Experimental and theoretical results revealed that PMS activation involves PMS reduction over the electron-rich graphitic N and PMS oxidation around the electrondeficient carbon atom adjacent to graphitic N in NC-900, in which PMS oxidation is the main reaction rendering singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ the major reactive species. By contrast, PDS reduction over the electron-poor carbon atom dominates PDS activation on NC-900 for hydroxyl radical (% OH) generation. The large amount of % OH with stronger oxidation capability than $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ contributes to the superiority of NC-900/PDS over NC-900/PMS in organic pollutant degradation. This work provides profound insights into the difference in metal-free PMS and PDS activation and presents fundamental frameworks in the design of advanced carbon-based catalysts toward sustainable environmental remediation.

# 1. Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a representative one of endocrine disrupting compounds (EDCs) that has been widely used in the plastic industry and detected in the environment [1,2]. This emerging organic pollutant has attracted considerable attention due to its high estrogenicity, which gives rise to potential health concerns for both wildlife and humans [2,3]. Persulfate including peroxymonosulfate (PMS, ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } -$ ) and peroxydisulfate (PDS, $\mathsf { S } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - } )$ is considered to be an alternative oxidant that finds widespread application in advanced oxidation processes for the degradation of BPA [4,5]. The peroxymonosulfate and peroxydisulfate ions are derivates of hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ in which one hydrogen atom and two hydrogen atoms are substituted by one $S O _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ group and two $S O _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ groups, respectively. The disparate substitution sites in PMS and PDS give rise to the difference in their structures and properties. In particular, PDS is a symmetrical oxidant with the peroxide (OeO) bond dissociation energy of $1 4 0 \mathrm { \ k J \ m o l ^ { - 1 } }$ , whilst the molecular structure of PMS is asymmetrical and the dissociation energy of the $_ { 0 - 0 }$ bond is estimated to be in the range of $1 4 0 { - } 2 1 3 \mathrm { k J } \mathrm { m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ [6,7]. In theory, the cleavage of $\mathrm { o - } 0$ bond in PMS is more difficult than that in PDS, however, the activation of PMS has been reported to be more efficient relative to PDS over heterogeneous catalysts due to the asymmetrical structure of PMS [5,8–10]. In this regard, clarifying the difference in PMS and PDS activation processes is still rewarding and thus retains great expectation.

Among the various persulfate activators, metal-based materials have been proven to be efficient catalysts for persulfate activation [11–13]. Unfortunately, the issue of metal leaching from metal-based catalysts remains a significant concern for large-scale applications. Very recently, carbon-based materials have stimulated a great deal of interest in the activation of persulfate because of their low cost, environmental friendliness, great tolerance to acid and base, and tunable electronic properties [14–17]. Doping of carbon hosts with a heteroatom, particularly nitrogen (N), can tailor the chemical and electronic structures of carbon materials due to the comparable atomic size and higher electronegativity of nitrogen atom, pronouncedly enhancing the catalytic activity of nitrogen-doped carbon materials in persulfate activation [4,8,18–20]. In general, N-doped carbon materials are primarily obtained by in situ doping and post-treatment strategies, of which the former involves direct pyrolysis of nitrogen-rich compounds or precursors at elevated temperature in an inert atmosphere and the latter refers to treatment of carbon materials with nitrogen-containing reagents such as ammonia gas [21,22]. However, these approaches suffer from problems of low doping efficiency, residual metallic impurity and relatively high cost. Moreover, templating methods, which are commonly adopted to introduce additional porosity during the synthesis of N-doped carbonaceous materials, often require toxic reagents (such as hydrofluoric acid etching in the case of silica templates) or lengthy immersions in organic solvents to remove the template [22,23]. For metal-free activation of persulfate, efficient and environmentally benign routes must be found to synthesize N-doped carbon materials with desirable N-doping level and unique structure.

Graphitic carbon nitride $( \mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } } )$ is a metal-free conjugated organic polymer consisting of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ hybridized carbon and nitrogen atoms with a high N content of $5 7 . 1 \ \mathrm { a t \% }$ [24], which renders it one of most nitrogenrich compounds known. Most commonly, N-doped carbon materials are synthesized at elevated temperature of $9 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , much higher than the decomposition temperature of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ $( \sim 7 5 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , excluding the requirement of postsynthetic removal of the template. Previous result [22] demonstrated the probability of using $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ as both template and nitrogen source to fabricate N-doped carbon nanosheets with ultrahigh specific surface area and high-level nitrogen doping, which not only facilitated mass transport but also created active sites for electrochemical reaction. With these merits, it is reasonable to anticipate that the N-doped carbon material templated from $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ may be a potential candidate for persulfate activation toward organic contaminant degradation. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have yet been reported utilizing $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ as template and nitrogen source to prepare Ndoped carbon materials for the activation of persulfate.

In this work, we report the synthesis of N-doped porous carbon (NC900) via a combined hydrothermal treatment of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ with glucose solution followed by thermal calcination method, in which $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ was used as both template and nitrogen source. The resultant NC-900 was systematically characterized and functionalized as a metal-free catalyst to explore the difference in PMS and PDS activation. In contrast to the previous reports on more efficient activation of PMS than PDS over Ndoped carbon materials, NC-900 delivers unexpectedly higher catalytic activity in the activation of PDS relative to PMS. Moreover, NC-900 exhibits excellent reactivity and stability for persulfate activation, which are superior to the vast majority of recently reported N-doped carbon materials. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed graphitic N and adjacent carbon atoms as active sites for the activation of persulfate. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) tests disclosed that PMS activation consists in simultaneous PMS reduction and oxidation, whereas PDS activation exclusively lies in PDS reduction. On the basis of experimental and theoretical results, the different mechanisms behind PMS and PDS activation on NC-900 and the superiority of NC-900/ PDS over NC-900/PMS in organic contaminant degradation are both clearly clarified.

# 2.2. Synthesis of N-doped porous carbon

The N-doped porous carbon was synthesized via a combined hydrothermal treatment of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { C } _ { 3 } \mathsf { N } _ { 4 }$ with glucose solution followed by thermal calcination method as follows. Typically, $0 . 5 \ g \ g { \bf - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ was put into a $1 0 0 \mathrm { m L }$ Teflon-lined autoclave filled with $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ aqueous glucose solution $( 0 . 3 \ \mathrm { M } )$ . The suspension was stirred for $4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ , and then the autoclave was sealed and maintained at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ in an oven. After cooling naturally, the product $( \mathbf { g } { \cdot } \mathbf { C } _ { 3 } \mathbf { N } _ { 4 } @ \mathbf { G } )$ were collected by centrifugation, rinsing with ultrapure water and ethanol, and then drying at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight. The resultant product was then calcined at $9 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ in a tube furnace under argon atmosphere to obtain the final Ndoped porous carbon (denoted as NC-900). As the template and nitrogen source, $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ was prepared by direct calcination of urea in a muffle furnace at $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ with a ramping rate of $5 \mathrm { ~ } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \mathrm { ~ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ .

# 2.4. Experimental procedure and analyses

Persulfate activation was performed in a $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ beaker with BPA solution $( 3 0 ~ \mathrm { m L } , 0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ , persulfate $( 2 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ and catalyst $( 6 ~ \mathrm { m g } )$ in a constant temperature controlled water bath at $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Of note is that the solution pH was adjusted to the natural $\mathsf { p H }$ of $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ BPA solution $\mathrm { ( p H } = 6 . 7 )$ or other designated $\mathsf { p H }$ values after the addition of persulfate by using perchloric acid and sodium hydroxide solutions. As the reaction proceeded, $^ \mathrm { 1 \ m L }$ aliquots of reaction solution were withdrawn by syringe at designated time intervals, and immediately filtered through a Millipore filter $( 0 . 2 2 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ for analysis. The degradation of organic pollutant was monitored by a 1260 Infinity HPLC (Agilent, USA) equipped with a UV detector and a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column $( 4 . 6 ~ \times ~ 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ , $2 . 7 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } ,$ ). The mobile phases were methanol/water $( 7 0 { : } 3 0 , \mathbf { v } / \mathbf { v } )$ and acetonitrile/water $_ { ( 0 . 0 8 \% }$ phosphoric acid) (50:50, v/ v) for BPA and DP with the respective detection wavelength of 225 and $2 2 1 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ . The flow rate was set as $1 ~ \mathrm { { m L } } \mathrm { { m i n } } ^ { - 1 }$ . The removal efficiency was calculated by the following equation.

$$
\mathrm { c y } ( \% ) = 1 0 0 \ \times \ ( C _ { 0 } - C _ { \mathrm { t } } ) / C _ { 0 }
$$

where $C _ { 0 }$ and $C _ { \mathrm { t } }$ are the initial organic pollutant concentration and the concentration at a reaction time t during the catalytic oxidation process, respectively.

The EPR measurements for in situ detection of hydroxyl radical (% OH)/sulfate radical $\mathrm { ( S O _ { 4 } ) ^ { \cdot - } } \mathrm { ~ , ~ }$ and superoxide radical $( \boldsymbol { 0 } _ { 2 } \cdot \mathrm { ~ \ - ~ } )$ were conducted using DMPO as the spin trapping agent in the aqueous and methanol media, respectively. Singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ was detected by EPR spectrometry with the spin-trapping agent of TMP in aqueous solution. Quenching experiments were performed to identify the reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated in the catalytic systems by adding TBA, ethanol, BQ and L-histidine as chemical scavengers for % OH, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ , $_ { 0 _ { 2 } } \cdot -$ and $^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 } ,$ respectively. Each experiment was carried out in triplicates and the average data with the standard deviations were presented.

# 2.5. Theoretical computation

All the calculations were conducted based on the density functional theory (DFT) calculations using Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) code with the standard frozen-core projector augmented-wave (PAW) method [25]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerh (PBE) function [26] was employed for exchange–correlation potential. The cut-off energy was $4 0 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and atoms were fully relaxed until the Hellmann-Feynman forces were less than $0 . 0 2 \mathrm { e V } \mathring { \mathsf { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ . The effect of van der Waals interactions was considered by using the empirical correction method proposed by Grimme (DFT-D3) with the Becke and Johnson (BJ)-damped. Bader charge analysis was performed to calculate the effective charge for the N-doped carbon materials. The adsorption of persulfate and BPA on graphitic N-doped carbon was simulated using ${ \begin{array} { r l r l } { { 6 } } & { { } } & { \times } & { { } } & { 6 } \end{array} } \qquad $ supercell models $( 1 4 . 7 6 ~ \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ { ~ } ~ } \times ~ 1 4 . 7 6 ~ \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ { ~ } ~ } \times ~ 2 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ { ~ } ~ }$ and $9 \quad \times \quad 6$ supercell models $( 2 2 . 1 4 \mathring { \mathsf { A } } \times 1 4 . 7 6 \mathring { \mathsf { A } } \times 2 0 \mathring { \mathsf { A } } )$ , respectively. A $k$ -mesh of $2 \times 2 \times 1$ was used for the sampling of the first Brillouin-zone. The adsorption energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } \right)$ was defined as

$$
E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } = E _ { \mathrm { N - d o p e d c a r b o n } + \mathrm { m o l e c u l e } } - E _ { \mathrm { N - d o p e d c a r b o n } } - E _ { \mathrm { m o l e c u l e } }
$$

where $E _ { \mathrm { N } }$ -doped carbon $^ +$ molecule, $E _ { \mathrm { N } }$ -doped carbon and $E _ { \mathrm { m o l e c u l e } }$ represent the total energy of N-doped carbon model with persulfate or organic pollutant molecule, N-doped carbon model and free molecule, respectively.

# 3.1. Characterization of samples

Fig. S1 shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the as-prepared samples. Pristine $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ exhibits two typical diffraction peaks at $1 3 . 0 ^ { \circ }$ and $2 7 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ , which are attributed to the in-plane repeated tri-striazine units and the interlayer stacking of conjugated aromatic systems, respectively [27,28]. After hydrothermal treatment with glucose solution, the intensities of these two peaks in $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } @ \mathrm { G }$ apparently decrease, due to the coating of carbonized glucose onto the surface of $g _ { - }$ $\mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ [22]. With respect to NC-900, the characteristic peaks of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ disappear and two new peaks appear at $2 4 . 1 ^ { \circ }$ and $4 4 . 0 ^ { \circ }$ (Fig. 1a), which are indexed to (002) and (100) planes of graphite [29], respectively, indicating the decomposition of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ template and the formation of carbon material. The conversion of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ into carbon material can be further verified by Raman and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analyses. A G band at $1 5 7 3 ~ \mathrm { { c m } } ^ { - 1 }$ in the Raman spectrum (Fig. 1b) corresponding to the graphitic carbon confirms the graphitic structure of NC900. In the meanwhile, the D band centered at $1 3 3 3 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ suggests the existence of disordered carbon [30]. In addition, the appearance of a broad and weak second-order band (2D band) at ${ \sim } 2 8 0 0 \ c m ^ { - 1 }$ indicates the few-layered feature of NC-900 [31]. The FTIR spectrum of NC-900 (Fig. 1c) displays the presence of nitrogen-containing functionalities at 1243 (C-N bonding) and 1580 $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { N }$ bonding) $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ [32], which contains no characteristic absorption peaks for $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ (Fig. S2), suggesting the thermal decomposition of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ and incorporation of nitrogen atoms in the carbon framework. The $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption–desorption measurement was conducted to investigate the porous structure and specific surface area of the sample. The curve of NC-900 shows a representative type IV adsorption–desorption isotherm (Fig. 1d), suggesting the sample is classified as the mesoporous material. The pore size distribution curve (inset of Fig. 1d) further confirms the existence of mesopores in NC-900. Moreover, the BET specific surface area $( S _ { \mathrm { B E T } } )$

for NC-900 is determined to be $3 3 1 . 9 9 \ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , accompanied by the corresponding pore volume of $0 . 5 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ . The porous structure of NC-900 can be also visualized by the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image in Fig. 1e and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image in Fig. 1f. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 1g) manifests the unambiguous lattice fringe with $d$ -spacing of $0 . 3 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , which is consistent with the graphene sheet spacing in graphite [33], further testifying the graphitic structure of NC-900. In addition, the energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping images (Fig. S3a) reflect the dominant carbon distribution with nitrogen and oxygen in NC-900. Moreover, the EDS attached to the TEM (Fig. S3b) further corroborates the uniform distribution of carbon and nitrogen elements in NC-900.

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum of NC900 (Fig. S4a) validates this carbon material is doped by nitrogen atoms, with the nitrogen content measured for $7 . 0 6 \ \mathrm { a t \% }$ . The oxygen peak is attributable to the physisorbed oxygen species on the NC-900 surface [34], as indicated by the appearance of exclusive O 1s peak at 532.4 eV (Fig. S4b) corresponding to the surface adsorbed water [35]. The deconvoluted high-resolution C 1s spectrum of NC-900 displays three bands at 284.8, 285.7 and $2 8 9 . 5 \mathrm { e V }$ (Fig. 2a), which are assigned to ${ \mathrm { C - C / C } } { \mathrm { = } } { \mathrm { C } } ,$ , $\mathrm { c - N }$ and oxidized C species, respectively [36], confirming the successful doping of N into the graphitic carbon matrix. Compared with $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ , the carbon and oxygen contents in $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } @ \mathrm { G }$ (hydrothermal treatment of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ with glucose solution) increase significantly whereas the nitrogen concentration decreases observably (Fig. S5a), substantiating the formation of carbonized glucose coating on the $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ surface via the hydrothermal process [22]. This can be further affirmed by the high-resolution C 1s spectra of $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ and $g \cdot$ - $\mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } @ \mathrm { G }$ . From Fig. S5b, $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ shows two peaks at 284.8 and $2 8 8 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , of which the latter referring to ${ \mathrm { C - N } } { \mathrm { = } } { \mathrm { C } }$ bond is the main contribution. As for $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } @ \mathrm { G }$ , the intensity of $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C - C / C = C }$ peak $( 2 8 4 . 8 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ is drastically enhanced and the ${ \mathrm { C - N } } { \mathrm { = } } { \mathrm { C } }$ peak intensity is significantly attenuated. Additionally, a new peak at 286.1 eV corresponding to C–O bond [35] appears in the C 1s XPS spectrum of $g \cdot$ - $\mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } @ \mathrm { G }$ . These results attest the development of some carbonized organic compounds on the surface of $g { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } @ \mathrm { G }$ , which are in line with the aforementioned XRD results. As shown in Fig. 2b, the N 1s peaks in NC-900 have four components centered at 398.2, 399.1, 400.9 and $4 0 3 . 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , which are attributed to pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, graphitic N and oxidized N, respectively. The dominant proportion of graphitic N $( 5 3 . 6 5 \%$ , Table S1) with high electron-transfer capacity can facilitate the electron exchange between NC-900 and persulfate [16].

Due to the higher electronegativity of N atom with respect to C atom, N doping would give rise to the electron redistribution in the Ndoped carbon material. In this context, density functional theory (DFT) computations were applied to explore the electronic structure of NC900 after doping of N atoms. From the two-dimensional charge distribution images in Fig. 2c-f, graphitic N delivers the highest electron density among the three N-doping configurations, as also evidenced by the largest Bader charge of graphitic N (6.214 e, Table S2). In other words, graphitic N is capable of withdrawing more electrons from the neighboring carbon atoms, resulting in the decrease of electron density of these carbon atoms. The carbon atoms adjacent to graphitic N are numbered as C1, C2 and C3 in Fig. 2g, and the C3 atom shows lower Bader charge (3.632 e) as compared to other atoms of C1 (3.876 e) and C2 (3.634 e). In view of this, the graphitic N with the highest electron density and the adjacent C3 atom with the lowest electron density in NC-900 can act as active sites for persulfate activation through electron transfer pathways. On the basis of above characterization and computation results, the large specific surface area and appropriate mesoporous structure as well as dominant proportion of graphitic N are expected to be conducive to mass and electron transfer between NC-900 and persulfate, and thus the effectiveness of NC-900 in persulfate activation is highly expected.

# 3.2. Persulfate activation on NC-900

The persulfate activation on NC-900 was assessed by catalytic oxidation of bisphenol A (BPA) as a model organic pollutant under different conditions. As shown in Fig. 3a, PMS or PDS alone causes no noticeable BPA decay, indicating that persulfate is unable to oxidize BPA directly. Single NC-900 brings about approximately $3 6 \%$ BPA removal within $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , suggesting a certain adsorption capability of NC900 for BPA due to the large specific surface area. In the presence of persulfate, $9 0 \%$ and $1 0 0 \%$ BPA degradation are respectively achieved in the NC-900/PMS and NC-900/PDS systems after 5-min reaction, signifying the excellent catalytic activity of NC-900 in both PMS and PDS activation. However, NC-900 in the presence of PDS degrades BPA more rapidly, as clearly confirmed by approximately 1.74-fold higher reaction rate constant (fitted by pseudo first-order kinetics) of NC-900/ PDS $( 0 . 8 0 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 9 6 )$ than that of NC-900/PMS $( 0 . 4 6 \mathrm { ~ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 9 8 )$ (Fig. 3b). Considering that the calcination temperature has great influence on the property and reactivity of the catalyst, we prepared the N-doped carbon materials with the same synthesis procedure as NC-900 except with the calcination temperature at 700 and $8 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , which are respectively denoted as NC-700 and NC-800, and also investigated their properties and catalytic activities in persulfate activation. The SEM images in Fig. S6a,b illustrate the attachment of nanospheres on the surface of NC-700 and NC-800, which are derived from the hydrothermal carbonization of glucose solution at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Fig. S6c) [37]. Nonetheless, the carbonized spherical colloids are not observed on the surface of NC-900 (see Fig. 1e), indicating that the lower calcination temperatures such as 700 and $8 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ cannot completely remove the colloidal carbon spheres attached on the surface of catalysts. The $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption–desorption isotherms for NC-700 and NC-800 display the type IV curve (Fig. S6d), suggesting the formation of mesoporous structure. # 3.3. Difference in PMS and PDS activation on NC-900

Persulfate activation commonly involves the generation of various reactive oxygen species (ROS). In this regard, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were carried out to detect ROS generated in the NC-900/PMS and NC-900/PDS systems. During the tests, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was applied as the trapping agent to examine the production of hydroxyl radical (% OH), sulfate radical $\mathrm { ( S O _ { 4 } ) ^ { \cdot - \cdot } }$ ) and superoxide radical $( \mho _ { 2 } ^ { \cdot - } )$ in the systems. As depicted in Fig. 4a, the EPR spectrum of NC-900/PMS exhibits a 1:2:2:1 quartet characteristic signal with the hyperfine splitting constants of $\mathsf { a } _ { \mathrm { N } } = \mathsf { a } _ { \mathrm { H } } = 1 4 . 9 \ : \mathrm { G }$ [38] in the presence of DMPO for the formation of DMPO-% OH adduct, indicating the generation of % OH. In addition, the specific EPR signal of $\mathrm { D M P O } { \cdot } S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot } { ^ { - } }$ adduct $\mathrm { ( a _ { N } = 1 3 . 8 G }$ , $\mathsf { a } _ { \mathrm { H } } = 1 0 . 1 \mathsf { G }$ , $\mathsf { a } _ { \mathrm { H } } = 1 . 4 2 \ : \mathrm { G }$ , $\mathbf { a } _ { \mathrm { H } } = 0 . 8 3 \ \mathrm { G } )$ ) [38] is also observed in the NC-900/PMS system, suggesting $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ production. As regards the EPR spectrum of NC-900/PDS, the peak intensities of DMPO-% OH and $\mathrm { D M P O } { \cdot } S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot } { ^ { - } }$ adducts both increase, reflecting that larger amount of % OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ is produced in the course of PDS activation over NC-900. Fig. 4b illustrates the EPR spectra for $\mathbf { O } _ { 2 } { ^ { \cdot } } ^ { - }$ of diverse systems. Compared with the single PMS or PDS, the addition of NC-900 brings about inconspicuous change in the characteristic signal intensities, which implies the absence of $_ { 0 _ { 2 } } \cdot -$ during the persulfate activation processes. Besides, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TMP) was selected as the spin-tripping agent to check singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ formation in the catalytic systems. As shown in Fig. 4c, the EPR spectrum of NC-900/PMS presents a triplet signal with equal intensities $\mathrm { ( a _ { N } = 1 6 . 9 G ) }$ [39], which corresponds to the oxidized product of TMP (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-N-oxyl, TMPO) [40], indicating the yield of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ in the NC-900/PMS system. In contrast, no discernable signals are detected for the NC-900/PDS system, signifying (i) the incapacity of NC-900 in activating PDS to generate $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and (ii) no existence of carbonyl group in NC-900 since the carbonyl group was proven to be active for persulfate activation toward the generation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ [41,42].

In order to corroborate the generation of ROS in the NC-900/PMS and NC-900/PDS systems, quenching experiments were performed using tert-butyl alcohol (TBA), ethanol, benzoquinone (BQ) and L-histidine as the scavengers for % OH, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ , $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } { : } -$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ,$ , respectively, and the corresponding results are shown in Fig. 4d. In the case of BPA oxidation in the NC-900/PMS system, TBA, ethanol and $\mathrm { L }$ -histidine are all found to suppress BPA degradation, in which L-histidine shows much more significant suppression effect, indicating $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ as the main reactive species in the NC-900/PMS system. However, Lee and co-authors [43] recently reported that the inhibitory effect of $\mathbf { L }$ -histidine in the carbon nanotube (CNT)-activated PMS process was due to the rapid depletion of PMS. In the meanwhile, the CNT-mediated electron transfer from the pollutant to PMS was supposed to be responsible for the degradation of organic pollutant. To elucidate the origin of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ in the NC-900/PMS system, we further investigated the $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ generation in the presence of deuterium oxide $\mathbf { ( D _ { 2 } O ) }$ as the lifetime of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ could be prolonged observably [44]. As shown in Fig. 4e, the $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ signal intensity in the $\mathrm { D } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } \cdot$ based NC-900/PMS system increases significantly as compared to the $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ -based NC-900/PMS system. Moreover, to explore whether the catalyst-mediated electron transfer from the pollutant to PMS contributes to BPA degradation, the interaction of NC-900 and PMS was also investigated (NC-900 was premixed with PMS for reaction in the $1 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ solution. Subsequently, $1 5 \mathrm { m L } 0 . 2 \mathrm { m M }$ BPA solution was added into the suspension of NC-900 and PMS after $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ ). This is because if NC-900 just serves as the electron transfer mediator between BPA and PMS, the premixture of NC-900 and PMS would show inappreciable impact on the degradation of BPA. On condition that NC-900 is able to activate PMS to generate $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , the BPA degradation would be weakened with the constant consumption of PMS and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . As can be seen from Fig. 4f, premixing NC-900 and PMS for 5 min gives rise to obvious attenuation of BPA oxidation, which rules out the contribution of NC-900- mediated electron transfer from BPA to PMS to the degradation of BPA, and thus attests that $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ indeed originates from PMS activation by NC900. With reference to the NC-900/PDS system, the inhibition of BPA oxidative degradation is only observed in the presence of TBA and ethanol, where TBA inhibits the degradation of BPA more pronouncedly. The above results illustrate that (i) % OH, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ are involved in the NC-900/PMS system, (ii) % OH and $S O _ { 4 } \cdot -$ are generated in the NC-900/PDS system and (iii) $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and % OH are the major ROS in the systems of NC-900/PMS and NC-900/PDS, respectively.

To gain further insights into persulfate activation by NC-900, the XPS analysis of NC-900 before and after catalytic reactions was conducted. Fig. 5a displays the high-resolution XPS spectra of N 1s for NC900 and used NC-900. After reaction with PMS or PDS, the graphitic N peak shifts to lower binding energy in the used NC-900, while the peaks of pyridinic N and pyrrolic N in the used NC-900 remain nearly unchanged as compared to those in fresh NC-900. Additionally, in the high-resolution XPS C 1s spectra, the $\mathrm { c - N }$ peak in the used NC-900 after reaction with PMS or PDS exhibits negative shift to lower binding energy (Fig. 5b). Such peak shifts of graphitic N and $\mathrm { c - N }$ species in NC900 before and after catalytic reactions reveal graphitic N and adjacent carbon atoms as active sites for persulfate activation. To further confirm the active sites in NC-900 for the activation of persulfate, the PMS or PDS activation on the N-doped carbon models with different N-doping configurations was investigated by DFT computations. As depicted in Fig. 5c, the adsorption energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } \right)$ of PMS or PDS on graphitic N is higher than that on pyridinic N and pyrrolic N, suggesting graphitic N as preferential site for PMS or PDS accessibility, which is consistent with the highest electron density of graphitic N among the three Ndoing configurations (see Table S2) and the above XPS analysis results. The highest electron density of graphitic N means that graphitic N can attract more electrons from its adjacent carbon atoms, resulting in the decrease in the electron density of these carbon atoms. In consideration of the lowest electron density of the C3 atom next to graphitic N (Fig. 2g), this C3 atom may also act as active site for PMS or PDS activation, and the persulfate activation on the C3 atom was also explored. The adsorption energy of PMS or PDS on the site of C3 atom (Fig. 5c) demonstrates that besides graphitic N, the C3 atom can also serve as active site for PMS or PDS activation, and that the C3 atom is more favorable for PMS or PDS adsorption due to the higher $E _ { \mathrm { a d s } }$ values. The graphitic N with the highest electron density and neighboring C3 atom with the lowest electron density in NC-900 are likely to afford the different electron transfer pathways between NC-900 and PMS or PDS due to the dissimilar structure and property of persulfate. Bader charge analysis was then conducted to look into the electron transfer process. Seen from Table S5, the Bader charge of graphitic N reduces by 0.026 e after adsorbing PMS, which indicates the tendency of graphitic N to donate the electron to PMS. By contrast, the Bader charge of C3 atom increases by 0.094 e after PMS adsorption on this site, indicative of electron transfer from PMS to the C3 atom. This means that PMS reduction at graphitic N site and PMS oxidation over the C3 atom simultaneously occur during PMS activation on NC-900. The reduction of PMS leads to the generation of % OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ , whereas PMS oxidation results in the formation of PMS anion radical $( S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \cdot - } )$ , which subsequently reacts with water molecules rapidly to yield $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ [45]. Moreover, the more feasibility of PMS adsorption onto the C3 atom and appreciable increase of Bader charge of the C3 atom after adsorbing PMS reveal that PMS oxidation is the major reaction in the course of PMS activation over NC-900, rendering $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ the dominant ROS in the NC-900/PMS system, in agreement with the results of EPR measurements and quenching experiments. When PDS was adsorbed on the graphitic N site, the Bader charge of graphitic N changes hardly, indicating nearly no occurrence of electron exchange between graphitic N and PDS. However, after adsorption of PDS onto the C3 atom, the Bader charge of C3 atom decreases by $0 . 0 4 7 \mathrm { e }$ , manifesting electron transport from the C3 atom to PDS. It should be noted that, although the C3 atom has the lower electron density, it is still capable of delivering the electron to reduce PDS to produce free radicals.

In an effort to confirm the above speculation, we investigated the influence of different organic pollutants with dissimilar charge density on the evolution of ROS in the catalytic systems. The BPA with electrondonating group such as phenolic hydroxyl group and diphenhydramine (DP) without such group were applied as target compounds (see Fig. S11 for their molecular structures) [5]. As shown in Fig. 5d, the $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ signal intensity in the NC-900/PMS system reduces markedly after the addition of BPA, but undergoes little change upon the exposure of DP. On the other hand, the presence of BPA increases the intensity of % OH signal in the NC-900/PMS system, while the injection of DP into the suspension of NC-900 and PMS inappreciably alters the % OH signal intensity (Fig. 5e). This is due to the existence of phenolic hydroxyl group in the molecule of BPA, which enables complexation of BPA with the electron-deficient C3 atom in NC-900. The higher adsorption energy of BPA on the C3 atom than that on graphitic N (Table S6) verifies the preferential site of C3 atom for BPA accessibility. The complexation of BPA onto the site of C3 atom impairs the interaction between PMS and NC-900, which hampers electron delivery from PMS to the C3 atom, and thus weakens PMS oxidation toward the attenuation in $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ generation. In the meanwhile, the complexed BPA molecule tends to donate electron to graphitic N through the C-N bond, as evidenced by the increased Bader charge of graphitic N after adsorption of BPA onto the C3 atom (Table S6), facilitating PMS reduction over the electron-enriched graphitic N for the enhancement of % OH formation. When DP, which contains no electron-donating group in its molecule, was presented in the system of NC-900/PMS, no obvious change in the intensity of % OH and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ signals was observed, suggesting no occurrence of complexation between DP and the C3 atom in NC-900. As for the NC-900/PDS system, the presence of BPA or DP brings about negligible alteration in the $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ signal intensity (Fig. 5d). However, the addition of BPA distinctly decreases the % OH peak intensity, while the injection of DP causes a slight attenuation in the signal intensity of % OH (Fig. 5e). This can be attributed to the complexation of BPA with the electron-poor C3 atom in NC-900, which hinders electron transfer from the C3 atom to PDS, thus weakening PDS reduction at the site of C3 atom for the demotion of % OH production. As expected, the NC-900/persulfate system is highly oxidative to BPA but less effective for DP degradation (Fig. 5f). The above experimental and theoretical results provide solid evidence for PMS reduction around the electron-rich graphitic N for the formation of % OH and PMS oxidation over the electron-deficient C3 atom for the generation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ,$ , and PDS reduction at the electron-poor C3 atom toward the yield of % OH.

Therefore, the different mechanisms behind PMS and PDS activation on NC-900 can be proposed in Fig. 6. To be specific, the disparate electron transfer processes between NC-900 and PMS or PDS rest with the diverse chemical and structural properties of persulfate. In particular, PMS can serve as both an oxidant and a reductant [46], empowering PMS reduction over the electron-rich graphitic N and PMS oxidation around the electron-poor C3 atom adjacent to graphitic N in NC-900. Because of the asymmetrical structure, the reduction of PMS leads to the formation of % OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ . However, unlike PMS, PDS cannot easily engage as a reductant and thus the PDS activation prevailingly lies in PDS reduction, which exclusively results in the generation of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ owing to the symmetrical structure of PDS [47]. In this regard, the PDS reduction over the electron-deficient C3 atom neighbouring graphitic N leads to $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot -$ production, and subsequently $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ converts into % OH via reaction with water molecules [48]. In light of stronger oxidation capability of % OH than that of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ [47], the % OH-mediated NC-900/PDS system is more efficient than the $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } .$ - dominated NC-900/PMS system for the degradation of BPA. Based on above results, it can be reasonably deduced that large specific surface area, obvious porous structure and high proportion of graphitic N are beneficial to mass and electron transport between NC-900 and persulfate, contributing to superior catalytic activity of NC-900 in the activation of persulfate, particularly for PDS.

# 4. Conclusion

This work demonstrates the feasibility of utilizing graphitic carbon nitride as both template and nitrogen source for the fabrication of nitrogen-doped porous carbon (NC-900) with large specific surface area, apparent mesoporous structure and desirable nitrogen content (especially high graphitic N proportion). These advantages endow NC-900 with excellent mass and electron transfer characteristics and abundant catalytic active sites. Different from the previous results on more efficient activation of PMS than PDS over the nitrogen-doped carbon materials, NC-900 exhibits unexpectedly higher catalytic activity for the activation of PDS (rate constant of $0 . 8 0 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ) relative to PMS (rate constant of $0 . 4 6 \mathrm { \ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ). Moreover, NC-900 shows superior reactivity and stability to the most majority of nitrogen-doped carbon materials in persulfate activation. On account of dual function of PMS as both an oxidant and a reductant, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis and density functional theory (DFT) calculations as well as electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) tests unravel that PMS activation on NC-900 involves PMS reduction over the electron-rich graphitic N and PMS oxidation around the electron-deficient C atom adjacent to graphitic N, in which PMS oxidation is the primary reaction rendering $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ the main reactive oxygen species in the NC-900/PMS system. On the other hand, due to the nature of PDS which cannot easily serve as a reductant, PDS activation on NC-900 consists in PDS reduction over the electron-poor C atom next to graphitic N for the production of % OH as the dominant reactive oxygen species. The large amount of % OH with stronger oxidation capability than $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ accounts for the superiority of NC-900/PDS over NC-900/PMS in organic pollutant degradation. This study not only details profound insights into the difference in metal-free PMS and PDS activation mechanisms but also provides a useful guidance in the design and development of high-performance nonmetallic nitrogen-doped carbon materials focusing on enlarging specific surface area and porosity as well as graphitic N proportion for broad sustainable environmental applications.
