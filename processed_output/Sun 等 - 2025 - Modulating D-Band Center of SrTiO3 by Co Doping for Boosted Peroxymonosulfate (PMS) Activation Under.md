Abstract: Peroxymonosulfate (PMS)-based advanced oxidation technology has emerged as an effective means for removing organic pollutants from water due to its strong oxidizing ability. However, enhancing the activation efficiency of PMS represents a key challenge at present. $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , a typical perovskite metal oxide, holds potential in the field of the photocatalytic degradation of pollutants, yet its application is limited by the wide bandgap and fast carrier recombination rates. This study optimized the photocatalytic performance of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ by regulating its electronic structure and optical properties through cobalt $( \mathbf { C o } )$ doping. Experimental results (TRPL, TPV, UV–Vis DRS, ESR, etc.) and DFT calculations (GGA-PBE) demonstrated that Co doping shifted the d-band center of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ upwards, optimized the adsorption energy of $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ , enhanced the sunlight response range, and significantly improved carrier extraction efficiency. Under visible light irradiation, 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) could be effectively degraded within $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ in a wide $\mathrm { p H }$ range. Through Fukui function calculation (B3LYP/6-31G\*) and experimental characterization analysis (HPLC-MS and IC), the possible degradation pathways of 2,4-DCP and the mechanism for photocatalysis were investigated. The toxicity analysis (T.E.S.T) confirmed the reduced toxicity of the degradation products of 2,4-DCPs. This study provides a reference for the catalyst design and optimization strategy of PMS-based advanced oxidation technology.

Keywords: photo-fenton reaction; ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ ; PMS activation; carrier extraction

# 1. Introduction

Dichlorophenols (DCPs), as typical persistent organic pollutants (POPs), are widely present in electronic waste treatment solutions, dye wastewaters, and pharmaceutical wastewaters [1–4]. Their high chemical stability and carcinogenic properties pose a serious threat to the ecological environment, making efficient and environmentally friendly wastewater treatment technologies a crucial area of scientific research and technological application. Among these technologies, Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) advanced oxidation processes have demonstrated great potential for removing organic pollutants in water due to their strong oxidizing properties and efficient degradation capabilities. However, one of the key challenges currently faced is how to further enhance the activation efficiency and degradation rate of PMS [5].

Perovskite oxides are promising photocatalysts due to their low cost, favorable optical properties, and high catalytic activity [6–8]. They are stable under harsh conditions because of their flexible crystal structure and the presence of transition metals. Moreover, their structural and physicochemical properties can be easily tailored by adjusting their chemical compositions. This versatility has led to a significant interest in their applications for photocatalytic water remediation [9,10]. ${ \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 } ,$ as a typical perovskite metal oxide, has attracted extensive attention in the field of the photocatalytic degradation of pollutants due to its strong redox ability and good structural stability [11–14]. However, the wide bandgap of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ limits its utilization of visible light, and its rapid carrier recombination rate results in high activation and adsorption energy barriers for $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ [15,16]. Metal doping is an effective method to regulate the electronic structure and optical properties of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ thereby optimizing its photocatalytic performance [17–20]. Notably, the precise modulation of the d-band center position in $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , achievable through the adjustment of dopant type and concentration, directly governs its carrier extraction and transport capabilities [21–24]. Furthermore, the d-band center of transition metals exhibits a strong correlation with their molecular adsorption affinity [25–28]. Consequently, regulating this electronic parameter enables the manipulation of both adsorption strength and configuration on the metal surface towards peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and its reaction intermediates, thereby critically influencing PMS activation efficiency. This electronic structure modulation constitutes a highly significant strategy for enhancing photocatalyst performance.

Cobalt (Co), as an abundant transition metal element on Earth, has significant advantages in regulating the electronic structure and catalytic performance of catalysts [19,29–31]. Co doping can effectively alter the electronic structure of the host material $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , thereby enhancing the activation efficiency of PMS [32–34]. Optimizing the adsorption-activation dynamics of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) through the precise adjustment of cobalt (Co) doping levels facilitates the cleavage of peroxide bonds, thereby generating enhanced concentrations of highly oxidizing species (e.g., sulfate radicals, hydroxyl radicals). This consequently accelerates the degradation kinetics of organic pollutants, such as 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4- DCP) [35–37]. Moreover, Co doping effectively broadens the operational $\mathrm { p H }$ range for efficient organic degradation, ensuring sustained high catalytic activity across diverse aqueous matrices [38,39], thus demonstrating significant application potential in critical environmental remediation fields, including wastewater treatment and groundwater purification.

Based on the above discussion, this paper enhances the activation of PMS and the efficient degradation of 2,4-DCP by regulating the d-band center and carrier extraction efficiency of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ through Co doping. The research results indicate that Co doping shifts the d-band center of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ up by $0 . 7 7 8 \mathrm { e V }$ (compared to the pure sample), optimizes the PMS adsorption energy to $- 1 0 . 2 \mathrm { e V } ,$ increases the carrier extraction efficiency by about six times, and allows for the degradation of 2,4-DCP $( 3 0 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ by $8 8 \%$ under visible light within $6 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . Moreover, understanding how material structure governs degradation pathways is key to revealing material properties [40,41]. Thus, density functional theory (DFT) calculations coupled with the HPLC-MS technique were also conducted to explore the degradation pathway of 2,4-DCP. This research may offer a reference to design an efficient and environmentally friendly method for treating refractory organic pollutants.

# 2.2. Photocatalytic Performance and Durability

To optimize the cobalt doping dosage in $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , pure $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ -X samples (where $X = 0 . 0 1$ , 0.025, 0.05, 0.2, and 0.5) were employed to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) for the degradation of a 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) solution at a concentration of $3 0 \mathrm { m g / L } ,$ as illustrated in Figure 3a. After $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of stirring in the dark, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ -0.5 demonstrated the highest adsorption capacity for the 2,4-DCP contaminant. Moreover, a comparison of the adsorption properties between $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ -X reveals that with increasing cobalt doping levels, the adsorption performance of the corresponding samples improves. This indicates that Co doping enhances the adsorption of contaminants on the surface of strontium titanate, thereby contributing to the enhancement of photocatalytic performance. Meanwhile, photocatalysts with varying amounts of Co doping exhibit different degradation efficiencies under visible light irradiation. The degradation efficiencies of $\mathrm { C o } { \cdot } \mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } { \cdot } 0 . 0 1$ , ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ -0.025, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ -0.05, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ -0.2, and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { - } } 0 . 5$ for 2,4-Dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) are $5 2 \%$ , $7 8 \%$ , $8 5 \%$ , $8 8 \%$ , and $7 5 \%$ , respectively. Notably, Co$\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } – 0 . 2$ (hereinafter referred to as ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ ) demonstrates the highest photocatalytic degradation efficiency of 2,4-DCP, achieving a performance that is 12.6 times greater than that of pure $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ .

As shown in Figure $3  { \mathrm { b } }$ , the photocatalytic efficiency of Co-doped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ combined with PMS activation is significantly higher than that without PMS activation. In contrast, the photocatalytic degradation performance of pure $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ , both alone and in combination with PMS activation, is considerably lower than that of Co-doped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ . Moreover, pure $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and PMS exhibit minimal photocatalytic activity under light irradiation. These results demonstrate that Co-doping significantly enhances PMS activation for pollutant degradation under visible light.

Considering the economic benefits and substrate removal, we investigated the effects of varying dosages of PMS and pollutant concentrations on the photocatalytic efficiency of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ in degrading 2,4-DCP. As the amount of PMS increased (Figure 3c), more active species were generated, resulting in a gradual improvement in degradation efficiency. However, when the PMS dosage exceeded $0 . 0 1 5 { \mathrm { g } } ,$ the removal efficiency for 2,4-DCP did not show a significant enhancement. This may be attributed to the quenching reaction between excess PMS $\left( \mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - } \right)$ ) and free radicals ( $\cdot S { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ and ·OH), leading to a reduction in degradation activity [50]. Therefore, in subsequent experiments, the PMS dosage was maintained at $0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { ~ g ~ }$ . As illustrated in Figure 3d, the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ photocatalytic PMS activation system is capable of adapting to a wide concentration range of 2,4-DCP. When the concentration of 2,4-DCP solution is increased to $4 0 \ \mathrm { m g / L } ,$ the degradation rate of 2,4-DCP by ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ can still exceed $7 5 \%$ within $5 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ .

The activity of advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) is strongly dependent on the $\mathrm { p H }$ value of the reaction system. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate the performance of photocatalytic materials across a wide $\mathrm { p H }$ range. Figure 3e illustrates the effect of various $\mathrm { p H }$ conditions on the removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP in the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ system. ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ exhibited excellent photocatalytic performance (remaining above $8 0 \%$ ) across a wide $\mathrm { p H }$ range $\mathrm { ( p H } = 3 , 5 , 7 , 9 , 1 1 , 1 2 \mathrm { ) }$ , with the highest catalytic efficiency observed at $\mathrm { p H } = 1 2$ . However, without the addition of PMS, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of Co$\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ in all $\mathrm { p H }$ ranges is only less than $4 0 \%$ . This indicates that ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ possesses good $\mathrm { p H }$ universality in acidic, neutral, and alkaline conditions, highlighting its potential for wastewater treatment in complex $\mathrm { p H }$ environments. However, in the absence of PMS, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ across all $\mathrm { p H }$ ranges remains below $4 0 \%$ . This highlights the significant impact of Co doping in enhancing the PMS activation performance of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ for pollutant degradation.

Recognizing that photocatalytic reusability and stability constitute critical performance metrics for wastewater treatment applications, recycling experiments were conducted to quantitatively evaluate these properties of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ . As shown in Figure $\mathtt { S 4 a }$ , after five cycles of photocatalytic degradation experiments, the photocatalytic performance of Co$\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ remained nearly unchanged, with the removal rate of 2,4-DCP consistently around $8 0 \%$ . This indicates that ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ possesses excellent chemical stability and reusability. To further demonstrate the recycling stability of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ , the XRD data of the fresh and used ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ have been provided for comparison (Figure S4b). No phase decomposition or lattice distortion was observed. The retention of identical XRD patterns following five photocatalytic cycles under extended irradiation $\mathrm { ( { > } 4 h ) }$ (Figure S4b) provides definitive evidence for the photothermal stability of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ . This structural robustness, coupled with uncompromised performance, underscores its viability for industrial implementation.

# 2.3. Photo-Electrochemical Properties

The UV–Vis DRS data (Figure 4a) indicate that Co-doped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ exhibits a significantly higher spectral response to visible light in the range of $4 0 0 { - } 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ compared to undoped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ . This finding suggests that cobalt doping effectively modifies the band structure of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ , enhances its absorption of visible light, and increases the generation of photogenerated charge carriers. To investigate the effect of Co doping on the photogenerated carrier separation efficiency of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ , we conducted steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) spectra measurements. As illustrated in Figure $4 \mathrm { b }$ , the fluorescence intensity of Co-doped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ is significantly lower than that of undoped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ . A higher PL intensity correlates with a faster carrier recombination rate, indicating that Co doping effectively inhibits the photogenerated electron-hole recombination in $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ Additionally, as demonstrated in Figure $^ { 4 \mathrm { c } , }$ the carrier lifetime of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ is significantly longer—approximately eight times that of pure $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ . The fitting parameters can be found in Table S2. This observation aligns with previous reports concerning Al doping in $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ [17]. Consequently, it can be concluded that Co doping reduces the n-type behavior of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and, in turn, extends the charge carrier lifetime and diffusion length. Generally, a longer carrier lifetime in photocatalytic materials is associated with a higher efficiency in the separation of photoelectrons and holes, which leads to enhanced photocatalytic activity. Therefore, the results from the time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) spectra further indicate that Co doping improves the charge separation capability of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ .

Transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements were performed to estimate the maximum charge extraction time $\mathrm { ( t _ { m a x } ) }$ , the amount of charge extraction (A), and the attenuation coefficient of the photocharge over time $( \tau )$ for $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ [15,51]. The results are shown in Figure 4d–f. Notably, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ exhibits a shorter $\mathrm { \Delta t _ { m a x } }$ $_ { \textrm { x } } ( 0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { m s } )$ , suggesting that Co doping enhances charge transfer efficiency in $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ . Furthermore, the charge decay constant $( \tau )$ of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ is smaller compared to that of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ , indicating that Co doping increases the number of active sites and shortens charge transport distances in $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ Additionally, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ demonstrates a higher amount of extracted electrons (A) than $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , implying a superior ability to generate photocarriers. Consequently, the surface effective electron number $( \mathrm { n } _ { \mathrm { e f f } } )$ for $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ can be calculated using Equation (1), with the results illustrated in Figure $4 \mathrm { g }$ . It is evident that the effective charge of Co$\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ (1470.10) is approximately six times greater than that of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ (241.65). Therefore, Co doping significantly improves the rapid extraction and transfer of substantial charges from the $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ surface to PMS, which is crucial for effectively degrading pollutants.

The photo-generated carrier separation–migration behavior of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ was further investigated by photocurrent and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). It can be observed from Figure 4h that the photocurrent density of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ is higher than that of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , indicating a greater efficiency in the separation of photogenerated carriers. This finding aligns with the longer lifetimes of photogenic carriers observed in the time-resolved PL spectra (Figure $_ \mathrm { 4 c }$ ). # 2.4. Reaction Mechanism

Based on the UV–Vis DRS test data, the bandgap $\mathrm { ( E _ { g } ) }$ of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ was calculated using Equation (2) proposed by Tauc, where the integer ${ \mathfrak { n } } ,$ which depends on the transition characteristics of the semiconductor, is 2 for indirect transition here [52]. The Tauc plot, shown in Figure 5a, indicates that the band gaps of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ are $3 . 1 8 \mathrm { e V }$ and $2 . 7 3 \mathrm { e V } ,$ respectively, determined by extending a tangent along the curve. It is evident that Co doping reduces the bandgap of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and broadens the light absorption range. For the conduction position, Mott–Schottky curves were tested, as shown in Figure 5b. The conduction band $\mathrm { ( E _ { C B } ) }$ values of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ were found to be $- 0 . 9 0$ and $- 1 . 4 7 \mathrm { V } ,$ respectively. Both materials exhibit negative slopes, indicating that they are n-type semiconductors. Using the equation $E _ { V B } = E _ { C B } + E _ { g } ,$ we can calculate the valence positions of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ to be 2.28 and $1 . 2 6 \mathrm { V } ,$ respectively. Therefore, we can illustrate the band structure of both materials as shown in Figure 5c. ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ cannot generate OH radicals in water effectively because its valence band position does not reach the redox potential of ${ \cdot } \mathrm { O H } / \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ (2.72 V) when using holes $\left( \mathrm { h } ^ { + } \right)$ alone in photocatalytic reactions [53]. Therefore, in the $\mathrm { C o - S r T i O _ { 3 } } + \mathrm { P M S } +$ visible light system, any ·OH generation is likely to come primarily from the decomposition of PMS. Additionally, the conduction band of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ has reached the REDOX potential of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } / { \cdot } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { - } \left( - 0 . 3 3 \ : \mathrm { V } \right) .$ allowing it to reduce dissolved oxygen molecules to superoxide free radicals during photocatalytic reactions using photogenerated electrons [54].

The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) technique was employed to investigate the types of reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in PMS activation. As shown in Figure 5d, no signals for $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ and ·OH were observed in the $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } { + } \mathrm { P M S }$ and $\mathrm { C o - } S \mathrm { r T i O } _ { 3 } \mathrm { + } \mathrm { P M S }$ systems (in the dark). Upon illumination, distinct $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ and ·OH signals emerged in the $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } + \mathrm { P M S } + \mathrm { V i s }$ and $\mathrm { C o - S r T i O } _ { 3 } + \mathrm { P M S } + \mathrm { V i s }$ systems. Furthermore, the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 } +$ ${ \mathrm { P M S } } +$ Vis system exhibited stronger signal intensity. This indicates that $\cdot S { \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ and ·OH are primarily generated from the photocatalytic activation of PMS, and Co doping significantly enhances the activation performance of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ . Similarly, strong $\cdot { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ and $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ radical signals were also detected in the illuminated systems, with the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ system showing a stronger signal (Figure 5e–f). This demonstrates that Co doping also improves the ability of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ to activate PMS and produce $\cdot { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ radicals under visible light. Since holes do not produce ESR response signals, the ESR signals of the trapping agent TEMPO are used to confirm the presence of holes [38]. As shown in Figure $5 \mathrm { g } ,$ , the ESR signal strength is strong under dark conditions due to the absence of hole generation. Upon light irradiation, both $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ exhibit $\mathrm { h ^ { + } }$ signals; however, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ shows a weaker $\mathrm { h } ^ { + }$ signal. This is attributed to Co doping, which enhances PMS activation and improves photocatalytic activity. In addition, previous studies indicate that metal doping promotes Sr segregation from the lattice, thereby increasing O vacancy concentration in $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ , and the potential presence of O vacancies is expected to influence the photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ [55]. We conducted additional electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy to probe the presence and concentration of surface oxygen vacancies. As shown in Figure S5, both $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ exhibit characteristic ESR signals at $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { g } = 2 . 0 0 3 . } \end{array}$ , which is attributed to O vacancies, indicating both $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ host oxygen vacancies. Critically, the ESR intensity of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ is slightly stronger than that of undoped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , indicating a higher concentration of $\mathrm { o }$ vacancies in the Co-doped sample. We attribute this to Co doping, which facilitates the partial segregation of Sr from the lattice, creating additional vacancy sites to maintain charge balance [56]. These oxygen vacancies, as electron trapping centers, positively impact the photocatalytic performance of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 } .$ , effectively suppressing the recombination of photogenerated carriers. This is consistent with the high performance of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ shown in Figure 3.

The significant impacts and optimizations of Co doping on the electronic structure, band gap, work functions, d-band center, as well as the adsorption and activation capabilities of $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ on $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ were discussed through density functional theory calculations. Although DFT calculations at the GGA-PBE level typically yield overestimated bandgap results, a comparative analysis between $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ can still provide reliable qualitative conclusions. Firstly, as evident from Figure 6a,b, compared to undoped $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ , ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ exhibits notable changes in its band gap structure. The band gap, representing the energy difference between the valence band maximum and conduction band minimum, determines the material’s light absorption and emission properties [57]. Upon Co doping, it is observed that ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ possesses a narrower band gap, which facilitates electron transitions between energy bands, thereby enhancing the material’s electrical conductivity and solar light utilization efficiency [58–60]. This is consistent with the UV–Vis DRS results. Additionally, changes in work function values constitute another crucial impact of $\mathrm { C o }$ doping. The work function describes the energy required for electrons to escape from the material’s surface [61,62]. Figure 6c,d compares the work function values of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ with that of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , revealing a decrease in the work function of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ . This implies that electrons more easily escape from the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ surface, favoring charge exchange with external species, such as $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ , and subsequently promoting the adsorption and activation of $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ . Furthermore, as shown in Figure $6 \mathrm { e } { , } \mathrm { f } ,$ Co doping modifies the d-band center of Ti metal. The d-band center is a crucial parameter describing the energy state of the d-band of metal atoms on the material’s surface [63,64]. By comparing the d-band center positions of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ , it is found that Co doping shifts the d-band center of Ti metal up by $0 . 7 7 8 \mathrm { e V } .$ This upward shift favors the adsorption and activation of species like $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ on the material’s surface, as changes in the d-band center alter the interaction strength between metal atoms and anions, thereby optimizing adsorption effectiveness and activation capability [65–67]. Figure 6g,h presents electron localization function and Bader charge images, visually demonstrating the impact of Co doping on charge distribution through different color coding (blue representing hole accumulation and yellow representing electron accumulation regions). These images reveal that after Co doping, the charge distribution within the material becomes more uniform and dense, with the Bader charge transfer data increasing from $5 . 3 3 \mathrm { e } ^ { - }$ to $8 . 2 5 { \mathrm { e } } ^ { - }$ , which is beneficial for the adsorption and charge exchange of external species such as $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ . Finally, Figure 6i shows that under the same conditions, ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ exhibits significantly higher adsorption capacity for $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ compared to $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ (from $- 6 . 5 \mathrm { e V }$ to $- 1 0 . 2 \mathrm { e V }$ ), thereby significantly enhancing the degradation performance of DCPs. The above critical changes brought by Co doping, such as the rise in the Fermi level and the d-band center of titanium (Ti), along with the enhanced adsorption capacity and electron delocalization of $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ on the surface of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } .$ , provide a comprehensive explanation for the remarkable photocatalytic activity of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ . These findings are further supported by results from the ESR test.

Based on the results presented, the reaction mechanism for the degradation of 2,4-DCP through the photocatalytic activation of PMS on ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - } } S { \mathrm { r T i O } } _ { 3 }$ was proposed, as illustrated in Figure 7. Furthermore, the holes present on VB can further oxidize the $\cdot { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ radical into $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ . These active species, identified through ESR testing, energetically attack 2,4-DCP molecules, leading to their compelling degradation into smaller, less harmful fragments. # 2.5. Reaction Pathways and Toxicity of Products

LC-MS was used to identify the degradation intermediates of 2,4-DCP after $1 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of photocatalysis, with the results shown in Figure S6. Based on these findings, three degradation pathways for 2,4-DCP were established (Figure 8a). Initially, the C-Cl bonds in the 2,4-DCP molecule are attacked by active species, such as $\cdot { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ , and ·OH, leading to dechlorination and the formation of products P1 $\left( m / z = 1 3 1 \right)$ ), P7, and P8 $( m / z = 1 4 5 )$ [2,35,58]. Products P2 and P3 are generated from the second stage of desulfurization of P1, which then undergoes further decomposition into smaller molecules through dehydroxylation and ring-opening reactions. At the same time, product P1 can produce P6 when it is attacked by $\cdot { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ radicals and holes $\left( \mathrm { h } ^ { + } \right)$ . Products P7 and P8 are degraded to form P9 through a ring-opening reaction. Ultimately, products P5, P6, and P9 break down into smaller molecular products and $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ .

To further analyze the degradation pathway of 2,4-DCP, we calculated the electrostatic potential (ESP) and Fukui functions. In the ESP plot (Figure 8b), which is based on the optimized molecular structure shown in Figure 8a, the blue and red regions indicate electron-rich and electron-deficient areas, respectively. The electron-rich regions (blue) are susceptible to electrophilic attacks, while the electron-deficient regions (red) are susceptible to nucleophilic attacks. Notably, the deepest blue regions appear near the two chlorine (Cl) atoms, suggesting that these are the most vulnerable sites for attacks by electrophilic species. This is consistent with the discovery of dechlorination products (P1, P2, P3, P7, and P8) in HPLC-MS results. Additionally, the Fukui function was also utilized to predict the reaction sites of dichlorophenol molecules. The calculated values for the nucleophilic index $( f ^ { + } )$ , electrophilic index $( f ^ { - } )$ , and free radical attack index $( f ^ { 0 } )$ are summarized in Table S3. The $\cdot { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ tends to attack sites with higher $f ^ { + }$ values, while $\mathrm { h ^ { + } }$ prefers sites with higher $f$ —values. Furthermore, ·OH is likely to attack not only sites with higher $f$ —values but also those with $f ^ { 0 }$ values [2,70]. The calculation results indicate that the chlorine (Cl) atoms, as well as carbon atoms $C _ { 4 }$ and $C _ { 5 } ,$ are the primary sites for the attack of active species. This finding is consistent with the results obtained from ESP analysis and the detection of the ring-opening reaction product P9 through HPLC-MS. To conduct a preliminary evaluation of the safety of the $\mathrm { C o - S r T i O _ { 3 } + P M S + V i }$ s system for the removal of dichlorophenol from aqueous environments, we employed T.E.S.T. software (version 5.1) to predict the toxicity of intermediates identified through HPLC-MS analysis. The results, presented in Figure $8 \mathrm { g - j } ,$ indicate that most intermediates possess lower toxicity than 2,4-DCP, with several displaying non-toxic characteristics. Furthermore, to enhance the assessment of 2,4- DCP detoxification, we quantified the concentration of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ ions in the 2,4-DCP degradation solution through ion chromatography (Figure S7). The findings revealed a significant increase in $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ ion concentration during the photocatalytic reaction, further supporting the conclusion that 2,4-DCP was being effectively degraded into less toxic compounds.

The chloride ion concentration in the 2,4-dichlorophenol solution was measured using an ion chromatograph (IC, ICS 1100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The spin-trapping agent 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was used to detect $\cdot { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ and ·OH radicals in $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ and $\cdot { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ radicals in methanol. The agents 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) were employed to detect $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ non-radicals and photogenerated hole radicals $\left( \mathrm { h } ^ { + } \right)$ , respectively.

# 3.4. Measurement of Photocatalytic Activity

The photocatalytic performance was evaluated by measuring its degradation efficiency towards 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) under visible light irradiation. The photocatalytic reaction was conducted using a photochemical reaction device (CEL-PCRD300-12, Au-light Co., Beijing, China). A $3 0 \mathrm { m L }$ solution of 2,4-DCP and $0 . 0 2 { \mathrm g }$ of the photocatalyst were added to a quartz glass tube and stirred in the dark for $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ to achieve adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, light from a white LED lamp $3 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m A }$ , $1 3 2 \mathrm { m W } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } ,$ ) was introduced along with $0 . 0 1 5 ^ { } \mathrm { g }$ of PMS. Two milliliter samples were extracted every $1 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ A $0 . 4 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ filtration membrane was used to separate the 2,4-DCP solution from the solid catalyst. The concentration of 2,4-DCP was determined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer $( \lambda = 2 8 5 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ ) [70]. Intermediate products of 2,4-DCP degradation were identified using LC-MS (Thermo Fisher Vanquish-Q Exactive Plus). # 3.5. Theory Calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations, which included the electron localization function, Bader charges, and adsorption energies, were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) version 5.4.4 [71]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional was utilized [72]. Self-consistent electronic calculations were performed with a plane-wave cutoff energy set to $4 5 0 \mathrm { e V } .$ The Fukui function values were calculated using the Gaussian 16 program at the B3LYP/6-31G\* level of theory [73,74]. Additionally, the Hirshfeld charge was computed with the Multiwfn 3.8 program [75].

# 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully enhanced the degradation activity of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ in 2,4-DCPs through cobalt doping, which primarily attributed to the following aspects: (1) Adjusting the metal (Ti) d-band center to improve the PMS activation capability and the charge transfer ability to PMS; (2) optimizing the energy band structure and work function of ${ \mathrm { S r T i O } } _ { 3 } ,$ thereby enhancing solar light utilization and carrier extraction capability; (3) modifying the surface morphology of $\mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ to increase the specific surface area and the number of active sites; and (4) broadening the $\mathrm { p H }$ response range and improving the stability of the catalyst, making it possible to maintain catalytic activity under different water quality conditions, and thus, holding broad application prospects. This research not only provides new catalyst design and optimization ideas for PMS-based advanced oxidation technology but also offers an efficient and environmentally friendly solution for treating refractory organic pollutants in industrial wastewater.
