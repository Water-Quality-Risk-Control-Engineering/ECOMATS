# Photothermal Coordination Polymer with Temperature-Induced Molecular Deformation for Efficient Interfacial Evaporation and Catalysis

Kail Shi, Kai Sheng, Jingwei Hou, MiaiaoTian, Yatao Zhang,\*Bart Van er Buggn, and Junyong Zhu\*

Solar-powered interfacial evaporation presents a sustainable approach for water purification, but the presence of volatile organic compounds (vOCs like phenol) poses significant challenges to its efficacy. Herein, an innovative catalytic evaporator constructed from Balsa wood functionalized with coordination polymers (cobalt-1,5-diamino-4,8-dihydroxyanthraquinone, Co-DDA) is introduced. This multifunctional Co-DDA exhibits both conductive and catalytic characteristics, facilitating photothermal evaporation and photothermal catalysis within an integrated platform. The resultant evaporator achieves an impressive evaporation rate of $3 . 2 3 \mathrm { ~ k g } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { ~ h } ^ { - 1 }$ and an energy efficiency of $8 9 . 0 \%$ under one-sun irradiation. Notably, the local heat-induced molecular deformation of the Co-DDA polymer facilitates the reduction in the energy barrier of persulfate adsorption, as supported by experimental and simulation data. This effect promotes persulfate activation to generate more reactive oxygen species, resulting in a remarkable $9 7 . 0 \%$ degradation for $5 0 m \mathbf { g } \mathbf { L } ^ { - 1 }$ phenol. Furthermore, the Co-DDA-Balsa evaporator demonstrates exceptional long-term salt resistance and high antimicrobial activity, ensuring efficient, stable, and eco-friendly solar-driven water purification. This work sheds light on the development of multifunctional and sustainable catalytic evaporators for highly efficient VOC removal during interfacial evaporation.

# 1. Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 2.1 billion people worldwide lack access to safe water due to population growth and rapid economic development. This global water scarcity crisis requires innovative technologies to harness unconventional water sources, such as desalination and wastewater treatment.[1] Solar-driven interfacial water evaporation, capable of locating the heat generated by solar energy to the evaporation surface for efficient water evaporation, has emerged as an environmentally sustainable and promising technology to clean water production.[2] Despite substantial advancements in the solar-driven interfacial water evaporation process, challenges remain in purifying wastewater containing volatile organic compounds (VOCs). For example, phenol, a crucial raw material in textile production and pharmaceuticals, is broadly applied in industry despite its high toxicity and recalcitrance to complete degradation.[3] During solardriven water purification processes, VOCs like phenol can vaporize concurrently with water, leading to their accumulation in the distillate and potentially causing secondary pollution.[4] This limitation underscores the inadequacy of single-function evaporators for treating complex wastewater.

Recent advancements in solar-driven water evaporation have introduced strategies for efficient VOC removal. For example, molecular sieving and solution diffusion mechanisms, when integrated with solar-powered evaporation, can effectively eliminate high VOC concentrations from wastewater.[4] Peng et al. presented a metal-organic framework (MOF)/polyaniline (PAN) nanofiber array composite photothermal membrane for molecular sieving-based VOC removal during evaporation.[4b] The incorporated zeolite imidazole framework-8 (ZIF-8) layer functions as a molecular sieve to reject VOCs with larger molecular sizes than water. While molecular sieving enables solar-driven VOC removal, its processing capacity is limited by operating conditions such as evaporation rate and pollutant molecule size. On the other hand, photothermal processes based on catalytic degradation have demonstrated potential for VOC removal from wastewater.[5] From the perspective of reaction heat utilization, Li et al. highlighted the synergistic effect between photothermal heating and catalytic heat generation, leading to an ultrahigh evaporation rate of $1 2 . 7 \mathrm { ~ L ~ m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { ~ h } ^ { - 1 }$ and efficient 4-Nitrophenol degradation.[6] However, most existing degradation processes are limited to low VOC concentrations $( < 1 0 \ \mathsf { p p m } )$ [7] The development of multifunctional evaporators that excel in both evaporation and VOC degradation is critical for addressing water/energy scarcity and environmental concerns.

New semiconductors with conductive and catalytic properties offer potential solutions for overcoming the challenges associated with VOC removal. The solar absorption capacity and photothermal conversion capacity of these materials are primarily determined by the characteristics of photoexcited electron transitions.[] Their broadband light absorption range and appropriate bandgap enhance the solar energy absorption, facilitate electron transfer from the ground state to excited states, and promote non-radiative relaxation, ultimately boosting photothermal conversion efficiency[8] In the evaporation process, the process of light absorption and heat generation inherently transforms catalytic reactions from a single photocatalysis to photothermal catalysis.[9] The approach effectively leverages the light/heat interface and thermal/mass gradients between the evaporator and the bulk water, overcoming the limitations of single photocatalysis and reducing the energy consumption of thermocatalytic reactions.[10] However, in photothermal catalytic systems, a key challenge lies in optimizing semiconductors for both efficient light absorption and high photoredox activity. This necessitates identifying a suitable band gap within a single semiconductor to concurrently enable efficient photothermal conversion and robust photoredox reactions toward a high-performance catalytic evaporator.

To this end, a novel photothermal polymer with conductive and catalytic properties, cobalt-1,5-diamino-4,8- dihydroxyanthraquinone (Co-DDA), was in situ grown on Balsa wood for efficient seawater desalination and sustainable wastewater purification. Balsa wood, featuring favorable hydrophilicity and aligned hierarchical channels, facilitates effcient water/steam transport while also offering favored thermal insulation and energy localization at the air-water interface.[11] This unique combination of properties has led to its widespread application in various fields, including energy-efficient buildings (transparent wood),[12] optoelectronic devices,[13] and photothermal evaporation.[8,14] In addition, Balsa wood is rich in cellulose, enabling the anchoring of Co-DDA to the channel walls through electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions (Figure 1a). Combining it with the novel photothermal material Co-DDA not only enhances the photothermal conversion capability of the evaporator but also reduces the equivalent evaporation enthalpy, significantly increasing the interfacial evaporation rate (Figure 1b). Unlike previously reported single-function photothermal materials, Co-DDA integrates solar energymediated photochemical processes and solar light-induced thermal catalysis into a single unit, creating a complementing and synergistic effect, and improving catalytic performance. Notably, the molecular deformation of the Co-DDA polymer induced by local heat facilitates the activation of persulfate to generate more reactive oxygen species, thereby enhancing the catalytic reaction (Figure 1c). The resulting Co-DDA-Balsa exhibited an evaporation rate of $3 . 2 3 \mathrm { k g } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 }$ under one solar irradiation, with ultra-high salt rejection in simulated seawater and excellent long-term stability. Furthermore, when treating high concentrations of phenol $( 5 0 \ \mathrm { p p m } )$ , the system achieved a removal efficiency of $9 7 . 0 \%$ , effectively mitigating the accumulation of contaminants in the condensate. This work provides an effective strategy for the design of an integrated solar-driven interfacial water evaporation process with photothermal evaporation and photothermal catalysis to remove VOCs from water.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Characterization of Co-DDA Powder and Co-DDA-Balsa Evaporator

To augment the performance of natural wood in solar evaporation and VOC removal, Co-DDA particles were in situ grown within the pores of delignified wood, creating a multifunctional woodbased photothermal evaporator (Figure 1a). Here, cross-cut Balsa wood, a substrate with capillary pumping capabilities, was utilized. Although lignin in natural wood can hinder water diffusion and upward transport due to its hydrophobicity and air pocket formation,[15]alkaline delignification effectively addresses this issue while preserving the tubular structure. Delignification of Balsa wood results in the formation of a microfiber array that enhances multiple internal light reflections and reduces backscattering, thereby boosting the light absorption of the evaporator.[16] Moreover, the removal of lignin exposes active groups inside the wood channel walls, facilitating stronger interfacial binding between cellulose and ${ \mathrm { C o } }$ -DDA through electrostatic and H-bond interactions.[17] The subsequent in situ growth of Co-DDA using $\mathrm { C o C l } _ { 2 }$ and 1,5-diamino-4,8-dihydroxy-9,10-anthraceneedione (DDA) precursors feasibly converted the delignified Balsa wood into a photothermal material at room temperature.

In this work, the six-coordinated DDA was selected as the organic building block for coordination reaction with ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ , with the detailed reaction mechanism depicted in Figure S1 (Supporting Information).[18] The synthesis of ${ \mathrm { C o } }$ DDA involves the deprotonation of hydroxyl groups and the oxidation of amino groups. Hydroxyl groups undergo deprotonation upon dissociation, while amino groups are oxidized by atmospheric oxygen to form secondary amines. These secondary amines and deprotonated hydroxyl groups subsequently coordinate with ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ to form CoDDA. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed ${ \mathrm { C o } }$ -DDA nanoparticles with a size range of $2 0 0 { - } 2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , and elemental distribution (EDS) mapping confirmed the even distribution of Co, C,

N, and O (Figure 2a; Figures S2 and S3, Supporting Information). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis indicated the successful coordination of Co and DDA, as evidenced by the appearance of the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { O } }$ bond at $5 2 7 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and the $\mathrm { C o - N }$ bond at $1 0 9 6 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ (Figure S4, Supporting Information).[19] The coordination reaction between ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ and DDA involves the oxidation of amino groups, as evidenced by the -NH stretching vibration peak at $3 5 1 8 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , aligning with the proposed mechanism. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed the presence of C, N, O, and Co in the wide scan spectrum of Co-DDA (Figure 2b). High-resolution $\mathrm { C o } 2 \mathrm { p } \mathrm { X P S }$ spectra revealed the coexistence of Co(II) and $\operatorname { C o } ( \operatorname { I I I } )$ valence states, with binding energies of 782.1, 801.8, 781.1, and $7 9 6 . 4 \ : \mathrm { e V } _ { \mathrm { : } }$ respectively (Figure 2c).[20]

As illustrated in Figure 2d, during the photothermal process, Co-DDA, a semiconductor material, absorbs the energy of photons and excites electrons to transition from valence state (VB) to conduction band (CB), generating a multitude of thermal electrons and thermal holes. A portion of these thermally excited thermal carriers subsequently relaxes back to the ground state through different relaxation channels, accompanied by releasing heat and establishing a temperature distribution.[2a] In parallel, another portion of the hot carriers is directly injected into the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest occupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the reactants molecule for redox reaction.[9] UV-vis-NIR spectroscopy and VB-XPS spectra demonstrated the optical property and band structure of CoDDA. As depicted in Figures S6 and S7 (Supporting Information), Co-DDA powder exhibits full spectral absorption capacity and a direct bandgap of $1 . 9 6 \mathrm { e V } .$ This appropriate bandgap width promotes the excitation and generation of photogenerated carriers, thereby enhancing photothermal conversion and photothermal catalysis.

To impart the photothermal properties to Balsa wood, CoDDA was in situ grown on the surface and pore walls of the wood at room temperature (Figure S8, Supporting Information). UV-vis-NIR spectroscopy of the wetted Co-DDA-Balsa evaporator revealed a $9 4 . 3 \%$ absorption across the entire solar spectrum (Figure 2e), markedly surpassing the original Balsa by nearly $20 \%$ As illustrated in Figures 2f and S9a (Supporting Information), SEM images evidence the abundance of multi-level channels in Balsa, including vertical tracheids $( 2 0 { - } 6 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ , relatively large vessels $( 1 7 0 - 2 2 0 \mu \mathrm { m } )$ , and horizontal pit within the cell wall $( 1 { \cdot } 2 \ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ , forming a 3D interconnected structure.[21] These hydrophilic microchannels not only facilitate efficient water transport through capillary action but also enable spontaneous interchannel salt exchange during desalination.[22] In contrast, the resultant wood-based evaporator demonstrated an open and wellpreserved channel structure with numerous Co-DDA nanoparticles evenly distributed on the channel walls (Figure $2 { \mathrm { g } } ;$ Figure S9b, Supporting Information). This increases the surface roughness of the evaporator, promoting multiple internal light reflections and increasing light absorption. EDS mapping confirmed the even distribution of Co, N, C, and O elements on the surface of the timber channel wall (Figure 2i), indicating the successful loading of Co-DDA onto Balsa wood. Additionally, the increase in peak intensity of the ${ \mathrm { N } } { - } \mathrm { H }$ bond at $1 6 5 0 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ and the appearance of the $\mathrm { C o - N }$ bond at $1 0 9 6 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ confirmed the successful assembly of Co-DDA to Balsa wood (Figure S10, Supporting Information).

# 2.2. Interface Evaporation Behavior of Co-DDA-Balsa

The Co-DDA-Balsa was supported by one heat-insulated polyethylene foam sheet for a solar-driven evaporation test (Figure 3a). Water evaporation rates were recorded under onesun irradiation. For reference, the same measurements were performed on bulk water and untreated Balsa wood (Figure 3b,c). Under light conditions, bulk water exhibited an evaporation rate of $0 . 5 2 \pm 0 . 1 \mathrm { k g } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 }$ at an equilibrium temperature of $2 9 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ consistent with the reported data.[2a] When using control Balsa wood as the evaporator, the equilibrium temperature reached only $3 0 . 9 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and the evaporation rate was $1 . 7 \pm 0 . 0 6 \mathrm { k g } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 }$ . Despite its low light absorption capacity, we systematically investigated and optimized the evaporation test area and evaporator thickness to maximize the evaporation performance of the control Balsa and subsequently improve the performance of the modified evaporator (Figure S15, Supporting Information). Thus, under identical solar conditions, the surface temperature of the wet Co-DDA-Balsa evaporator rose from 17.4 to $3 9 . 8 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Figure 3c,d), and the evaporation rate reached $3 . 2 3 \pm 0 . 0 3 ~ \mathrm { k g }$ $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { ~ h ^ { - 1 } }$ , exceeding the evaporation rate of bulk water by more than six times. The vaporization enthalpy of interfacial water was estimated, and the evaporation efficiency of the evaporator was calculated using thermometric method (Figure S16, Supporting Information). Under one sun of irradiation, the photothermal efficiency of Co-DDA-Balsa was determined to be $8 9 . 0 \%$ , and the water vaporization enthalpy at the evaporator surface $( 3 9 . 8 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ was calculated to be $9 9 2 . 1 5 \mathrm { k J \ k g ^ { - 1 } }$ , approximately two-fifths of the bulk water vaporization enthalpy $( 2 4 1 2 \mathrm { k J } \mathrm { k g } ^ { - 1 }$ at $4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ .[23] Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements revealed that the equivalent enthalpy of Co-DDA-Balsa evaporation was $9 6 3 . 5 3 \mathrm { ~ k J ~ k g ^ { - 1 } }$ , consistent with the thermometrically calculated value (Figure 3e). The reduced vaporization enthalpy facilitates the phase transition of water at the evaporation interface, allowing more water molecules to evaporate with lower energy input.

To explore the influence of metal ions on evaporation performance, various metal ions $\mathsf { S c } ^ { 2 + }$ , $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ , $\mathsf { A g } ^ { + }$ , $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ , $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + }$ , $\mathrm { M n } ^ { 2 + }$ , ${ \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 + }$ , ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ were introduced to fabricate the $\mathrm { M } { \cdot } ( \mathrm { D D A } ) _ { \mathrm { n } }$ evaporator through the coordination with DDA. The distinct dorbital configurations of different transition metal ions result in varying $d \cdot d$ transitions upon photon absorption, leading to diverse light absorption wavelengths and subsequent evaporation performance.4) Figures 3f and S17 (Supporting Information) demonstrate that the Co-DDA-loaded evaporator evinces the darkest color, absorbs visible light most readily, and delivers the best evaporation performance. Subsequently, the impact of in situ synthesis time on the performance of Co-DDA-Balsa was studied. Figure S18 (Supporting Information) illustrates that the evaporation rate of Co-DDA-Balsa prepared a 1-hour reaction was suboptimal due to the limited Co-DDA loading and low photothermal conversion efficiency. The evaporation rate of the Co-DDA-Balsa reached its optimum at 6 h of reaction time. This finding was corroborated by SEM analysis (Figure S18b, Supporting Information), which revealed the highest Co-DDA loading within Balsa wood at this point. However, extending the reaction time beyond $^ { 6 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ (to 912 h) resulted in a decline in a diminished evaporation rate under one-sun irradiation. This decrease was attributed to the gradual deposition of DDA monomer over the Co-DDA layer, thereby reducing sunlight absorption. These observations underscore the dynamic equilibrium between DDA monomer and polymer loading, which exerts a significant influence on the evaporation rate as a function of reaction time.

Figures S19 and S20 (Supporting Information) give the effects of reaction solvent ratio and $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { D D A }$ concentrations on the evaporation rate of the Co-DDA evaporator. The increase of the material loading resulted in a rise in the evaporation rate, attributed to its ability of trapping water and activating free water into intermediate water, reducing the energy required to break hydrogen bonds during evaporation. Figure S21 (Supporting Information) shows the Raman spectrum of $\mathrm { C o _ { x } D D A _ { y } }$ -Balsa. Analysis of these spectra, using Gaussian function fitting, yielded IW/FW ratios of 0.73, 0.78, 0.93, and 0.84 for $\mathrm { C o } _ { 0 . 9 } \mathrm { D D A } _ { 0 . 7 5 }$ Balsa to $\mathrm { C o } _ { 1 . 8 } \mathrm { D D A } _ { 1 . 5 }$ -Balsa, respectively. These results suggest that judicious incorporation of Co-DDA can increase the ratio of IW, thereby enhancing water evaporation efficiency. However, excessive material loading $( \mathrm { C o } _ { 1 . 8 } \mathrm { D D A } _ { 1 . 5 } )$ may inevitably render the evaporator overly hydrophilic. This often resulted in a layer of water covering its surface, increasing heat loss and decreasing the evaporation rate (Figure 3g).[25] Through rational regulation of the reaction conditions, the evaporation rate of Co-DDA-Balsa not only surpasses the theoretical limit of the 2D interfacial solar evaporators but also outperforms existing wood-based evaporators (Figure 3h).

# 2.3. Photothermal Catalytic Behavior of Co-DDA-Balsa

A xenon lamp was used as the light source for photothermal catalytic VOC removal in the condensate collection device. Prior to condensate collection, the evaporator was immersed in $2 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of phenol-contaminated water $( 5 0 \mathrm { p p m } )$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ in the dark to establish adsorption-resolution equilibrium. Following pretreatment, the condensate collection process was initiated under one-sun irradiation (Figure S22, Supporting Information). The phenol concentration in the collected distilled water was analyzed using a UV spectrophotometer at $\lambda = 2 7 0 \mathrm { n m }$ .

The photothermal catalysis process integrates solar-mediated photochemical processes and sunlight-induced thermocatalytic reactions on the photothermal surface, establishing a synergistic effect between photothermal conversion and photothermal catalysis to enhance the catalytic activity. This approach addresses the limitations of single photocatalytic, such as high electronhole recombination rates and low conversion rates,[28] while also reducing the high energy demand associated with single thermocatalysis.[29] Consequently, photothermal catalysis offers distinct advantages over single-component-driven photocatalytic and thermocatalytic processes (Figure 4a).

A proof of concept was performed to evaluate PMS activation using Co-DDA-Balsa under visible light for phenol degradation. Using the evaportor with the best-performing $\mathrm { C o } _ { 1 . 5 } \mathrm { D D A } _ { 1 . 2 5 }$ Balsa, the optimal PMS dosage for a 50-ppm phenol concentration was determined. Figure S24 (Supporting Information) reveals that a PMS dosage of $7 . 3 5 \mathrm { m m o l }$ yielded the highest degradation, i.e., $9 7 . 0 \%$ .As shown in Figures $4 \mathrm { b }$ and S25 (Supporting) Information), phenol degradation was $3 7 . 5 \%$ and $4 5 . 5 \%$ in the PMS+Vis and Co-DDA-Balsa ${ \bf \mathrm { + P M S } }$ systems, respectively. These results suggest that both Vis and Co-DDA-Balsa show a certain PMS activation effect. Notably, in the absence of PMS, the phenol degradation in the Co-DDA-Balsa $+ \mathrm { V i s }$ system was only $1 3 . 5 \%$ , indicating that Co-DDA-Balsa alone lacks the ability to degrade phenol. Based on these findings, three primary mechanisms for PMS activation during phenol degradation are hypothesized: Vis activation, Co-DDA-Balsa activation based on the electronic transition of semiconductor materials, and local thermal activation at the photothermal evaporator surface.

To demonstrate the role of heat generated by the evaporator in PMS activation, mass transfer was evaluated using superresolution confocal fluorescence microscopy. # 2.4. Photothermal Catalytic Mechanism of Co-DDA-Balsa

To investigate the impact of photothermal interface temperature on the local coordination environment of Co-DDA, in-situ FTIR spectroscopy was applied to analyze the surface chemical composition at varying temperatures. Results revealed significant redshift and peak intensity increases in the characteristic $\mathrm { C o - N }$ bond peaks with rising temperature, indicating the local environmental changes on the surface of Co-DDA at the photothermal interface(Figure 5a).[31] Upon gradual decrease in the temperature, the $\mathrm { C o - N }$ bond peaks gradually returned to their initial state, suggesting that the temperature-induced molecular structure distortion of Co-DDA was reversible (Figure S29, Supporting Information). Previous studies have shown that the surface strain of the material can shift the $d$ -band center, modulating the electronic structure of catalysts and influencing interfacial reaction kinetics.[32] This prompted us to explore whether the subtle structural deformation induced by photothermal temperature would alter the band structure of Co-DDA. Figure S30 (Supporting Information) reveals that the visible bandgap of Co-DDA at $3 9 . 8 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ is $1 . 9 3 \mathrm { e V } ,$ which is smaller than that of Co-DDA at room temperature $( 1 . 9 6 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ Figure S7, Supporting Information). The reduction of the band gap indicates that Co-DDA has stronger light absorption capacity and hot carrier generation ability under local heat, which is conducive to catalytic reactions. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the influence of interfacial local heat on the catalytic activity of Co-DDA-Balsa in photothermal catalysis. These calculations revealed that Co-DDA molecules situated at the photothermal interface underwent twisted intramolecular charge transfer, with a dihedral angle changing from 0 to $2 5 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ . This structural change subsequently leads to a redistribution of electron density within the Co-DDA molecule, thereby impacting its overall reactivity [33] As depicted in Figure S31 (Supporting Information), a noteworthy consequence of this intramolecular distortion is a discernible shift of the $d$ band center toward the Fermi level. This shift, from an initial value of $- 1 . 3 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ to a final value of $- 1 . 3 2 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ ultimately enhances the catalytic reactivity of the Co-DDA moiety.[34] Differential charge density analysis demonstrated that the electron density is significantly stronger in Co-DDA than in PMS, suggesting electron transfer from Co-DDA to PMS within the catalytic system (Figure ${ 5 } \mathrm { b , c } )$ . # 2.5. Practical Applications of Solar Steam Generation Systems

To assess the practical feasibility of the Co-DDA-Balsa evaporator, a large-scale evaporator $( 8 \times 8 \times 1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } .$ was assembled for freshwater production. The evaporation system was deployed on the grass in front of the School of Chemical Engineering at Zhengzhou University (June 23, 2024). Due to the rapid evaporation of the Co-DDA-Balsa evaporator, condensate droplets quickly formed on the glass walls of the collection unit (Figure 6a). As shown in Figure 6b, when moved outdoors, the evaporator achieved an ultra-high evaporation rate of $3 . 7 3 ~ \mathrm { k g ~ m } ^ { - 2 } ~ \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 }$ at 13:30, primarily attributed to the higher ambient temperature (up to $3 9 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ compared to indoor conditions $( 3 . 2 3 \mathrm { k g } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 } )$ . In addition, the quality of the desalinated water was assessed using inductively coupled plasma (ICP) spectroscopy. The concentrations of the four main ions $( \mathrm { N a ^ { + } } , \mathrm { M g ^ { 2 + } } , \mathrm { K ^ { + } } , \mathrm { C a ^ { 2 + } } )$ in the desalinated water were reduced by two orders of magnitude, meeting the World Health Organization (WHO) international drinking water standard (Figure 6c). In addition, a long-term indoor desalination experiment was conducted using $3 . 5 \mathrm { w t . \% }$ saline water in an open environment. The Co-DDA-Balsa evaporator maintained a consistent water evaporation rate of $2 . 5 1 \pm 0 . 1 1 \mathrm { { k g } \ m ^ { - 2 } \ h ^ { - 1 } }$ over $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { h }$ of one-sun irradiation, with no observable salt precipitation. This observation demonstrates its capacity for longterm solar desalination without the need for periodic cleaning cycles (Figure 6d). Antibacterial experiment revealed significantly few bacterial colonies in the medium of Co-DDA-Balsa compared to control Balsa (Figure 6e), demonstrating the superior bactericidal activity of Co-DDA-Balsa against E. coli $( 9 9 . 3 \%$ $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { v s } } 2 . 9 \%$ , Figure 6h). The loaded Co-DDA contributes to this antibacterial effect by interfering with the metabolism and reproduction process of E. coli (Figure S33, Supporting Information).[38]

Industrial wastewater often contains complex and high concentrations of vOCs, posing significant treatment challenges. To systematically investigate the degradation performance of CoDDA-Balsa, particularly for water contaminated with high concentrations of vOCs, the initial phenol concentration ranged from 10 to 50, 100, and $2 0 0 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ . After evaporation through CoDDA-Balsa, phenol concentrations in the distillate were significantly reduced (Figure S34, Supporting Information), achieving a degradation of $8 2 . 7 \%$ , $9 7 . 2 \%$ , $9 5 . 0 \%$ , and $9 5 . 0 \%$ ,respectively (Figure 6f). In addition to phenol, Co-DDA-Balsa was also applied to treat other VOC aqueous solutions, including p-nitrophenol, aniline, p-phenylenediamine, and p-nitroaniline. Co-DDA-Balsa demonstrated a degradation exceeding $9 3 . 0 \%$ for these pollutants (Figure 6g; Figure S35, Supporting Information), confirming its feasibility for real-world freshwater production and water purification applications.

# 3. Conclusion

A multifunctional evaporator with photothermal/photothermal catalytic properties was developed by functionalizing Balsa wood with conductive, catalytic Co-DDA. The rational control of CoDDA growth onto Balsa wood through adjustments to solvent ratio, reaction time, and $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { D D A }$ concentration enabled optimization of structural morphology and Co-DDA loading. This eventually, enhanced the photothermal conversion capacity of Co-DDA-Balsa and declined the equivalent enthalpy of evaporation of water during interfacial evaporation. In addition, the thermal localization capability of Co-DDA-Balsa integrated photothermal conversion and photothermal catalysis within the evaporation interface, fostering a complementary synergistic effect that facilitated the catalytic reaction. Experimental and simulation results revealed excellent photothermal conversion ability of Co-DDA-Balsa. Interfacial local heat not only promoted the mass transfer of pollutants within the evaporator but also induced a subtle molecular deformation of Co-DDA. This structural deformation reduced the activation energy for reactive oxygen species generation and augmented the catalytic reaction. The fabricated Co-DDA-Balsa evinced a high evaporation rate of $3 . 2 3 \mathrm { k g }$ $\mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 }$ under one-sun irradiation while maintaining exceptional long-term salt resistance in simulated seawater. Moreover, CoDDA-Balsa exhibited an excellent removal of $9 7 . 0 \%$ when treating 50 ppm of phenol, preventing secondary pollution in the condensate. These outstanding integrated features render Co-DDABalsa catalytic evaporation highly competitive for solar-powered water purification in complex water environments.
