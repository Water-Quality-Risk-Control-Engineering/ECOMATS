Metal-biochar composite is considered as a promising alternative for future carbocatalysis in environmental decontamination. Nevertheless, unavoidable metal leaching impedes its scaling-up application and remains an environmental concern in the present scientific progress. Herein, a hierarchical Fe biochar (Fe/CBC) derived from cellulose was fabricated via a hydrothermal carbonization coupled with microwave irradiation and $\mathrm { N H } _ { 3 }$ activation. Several exterior organic electron shuttles (i.e., ascorbic acid, oxalic acid, tartaric acid, and hydroquinone) were accommodated onto Fe/CBC to introduce more electroactive functionalities (i.e., C–O and ${ \mathrm { { C } } } = 0$ ). In particular, comprehensive material characterization was performed to elaborate the physicochemical properties of tartaric acid-treated biochar catalyst (Fe/CBC-TA). Synergies of inherent hierarchical structure, well-developed carbon π-electron network, and accommodated electron shuttle could mitigate the Fe leaching from 2.44 to $0 . 5 7 8 \ \mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ in the peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation system for catalytic degradation of bisphenol A. Based on the results of scavenging experiments and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) analysis, the catalytic mechanisms transformed from a one-phase pathway (mainly • OH) for the Fe/CBC system to a two-phase pathway (first phase: $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ;$ second phase: • OH) for the Fe/CBC-TA system. The increased activation energy and improved catalyst recyclability of the Fe/CBC-TA in the redox reaction further pinpointed its environmental sustainability. Overall, this work offers new strategies to fabricate efficient metal-biochar catalyst and insights into its sustainable electrocatalysis.

# 1. Introduction

Disposal of tremendous amounts of agricultural and forestry biowastes has become one of the most long-standing issues associated with population growth and anthropogenic activities in modern era [1,2]. With the increasing demands for environmental sustainability in resource recovery and circular bioeconomy, these refractory organic wastes that consist of stable lignocellulosic substances are recommended to be exploited for producing value-added products via green and sustainable conversion technologies [3,4]. Utilizing biomass waste via less energy-intensive and more cost-effective hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) to fabricate porous carbonaceous material at lower temperatures under auto-pressurized water condition is promising in environmental fields including energy storage [5], soil amendment [6–9], and environmental decontamination [10–12].

As a versatile porous black carbon, biochar possesses favourable adsorption capacity, surface electronegativity, hydrophobicity, biological compatibility, and high pH value [13–18]. Recently, biochar has also emerged as an economical catalyst in advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) for environmental remediations [19–22]. Traditional AOPs catalysts (e.g., metal ions, metal oxides, graphene, reduced graphene oxide, carbon nanotubes, nanodiamond) suffered from several critical shortcomings, i.e., severe metal dissolution, poor affinity towards reactants, thermal sintering [23–25], cost-intensive fabrication, and high energy/chemical inputs [26]. Although raw biochar exhibits relatively low surface area and deficient electroactive sites [27], low-cost biochar derived from biowastes has demonstrated its preponderance as a costeffective and tuneable carbonaceous scaffold with diverse modification methods [28,29].

Most of the studies focus on the synthesis of metal/biochar composite structure to modify inherent surface chemistry and electrochemical properties of biochar [22,30–34]. It has been revealed that biochar framework can readily enhance electron mobility over its conductive matrix and distribute exterior metal catalytic sites owing to its surface oxygen-containing functionalities [20,21,35]. Thus, utilising biochar to harbour different transition metals (iron (Fe) [10,21], cobalt (Co) [36–38], and copper (Cu) [22,30,32], etc.) to generate catalyst can be promising for catalytic AOPs, because biochar not only improves the reactivity but also coordinates a carbon substrate to mitigate the aggregation of metals. The most common metal and metal oxide combined with biochar is Fe, which can provide sufficient free-flowing electrons and endow magnetization to the resultant biochar composite for easy separation. In addition, the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ redox cycle is conducive to achieving higher recyclability in the long-term operation. A myriad of studies have employed Fe-biochar in various applications owing to its extraordinary electrochemical properties [39,40]. Nevertheless, the inevitable leaching of Fe ions into the water body still impedes its scaling-up applications [23]. The heterogeneous redox systems based on iron oxides are also limited by the sluggish recovery rate from $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ in crystal lattice (solid phase), not to mention the generation of excessive sludge for post-treatment.

Recently, exterior organic electron shuttles, e.g., acetate, humic acid, and quinone were introduced to a homogeneous Fe/persulfate activation system and facilitated the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ redox cycling [41,42]. Xu et al. also found that the adsorbed low-molecular-weight organic acids on the biochar surface could enhance $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathrm { V I } )$ reduction [43]. It is plausible that proper electron shuttles could reach complementary coupling and reduce metal leaching to achieve better sustainability of metal-biochar composites. Moreover, compared with the oxygen functional groups that are prone to lowering reducibility and impeding electrocatalysis of biochar framework, the introduction of nitrogenous dopants can reallocate surface charges and spins to tailor the electrochemical nature of well-ordered crystalline carbon lattice [44,45]. The introduced basic nitrogen dopants may synergize with electron rich metal centres to accelerate the electron migration owing to their high electronegativity compared with substituted carbon atoms (Pauling scale: $\chi _ { \mathrm { N } } = 3 . 0 4 > \chi _ { \mathrm { C } } = 2 . 5 5 )$ [22,27]. However, the specific roles of different organic electron shuttles and nitrogen dopants on the surface chemistry, catalytic performance, and metal leaching of Fe-biochar composite in the degradation of organic pollutants remains unclear.

Herein, hierarchical Fe-biochar composite was synthesized via a coupled process of HTC and microwave irradiation using cellulose and Fe salt as precursors. Then, a facile and economical ammonia posttreatment was employed to enhance the basicity of biochar surface and to increase its affinity towards electron-rich organic electron shuttles (i.e., ascorbic acid, oxalic acid, tartaric acid, and hydroquinone). The asprepared hierarchical Fe biochars (Fe/CBCs) were employed as highefficiency metal-incorporated catalysts in a ubiquitous peroxymonosulfate (PMS)-based activation system as a representative strategy for environmental remediation. Reactive oxygen species (ROSs) including sulfate $\mathrm { ( { ' } { 8 } { O _ { 4 } } ^ { - } }$ , $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { E } } ^ { \circ } = 2 . 5 { - } 3 . 1 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ and hydroxyl (• OH, $\mathbf { E } ^ { \circ }$ $= 1 . 9 – 2 . 7 \mathrm { ~ V } )$ radicals could be readily generated after the homolytic cleavage of peroxide bond in PMS upon catalysis for the rapid degradation of organic pollutants [46]. Bisphenol A (BPA), as a toxic endocrine disruptor that is widespread in wastewater and poses risk to human health [47], was selected as the target organic pollutant in this study.

The objectives of this research were to (i) characterize the prepared hierarchical Fe biochars with various techniques and evaluate their feasibility to act as effective catalysts in AOPs; (ii) explore the synergies between electron shuttles and hierarchical Fe biochar, and investigate their environmental sustainability in terms of metal leaching, activation energy, and recyclability; (iii) and identify the plausible mechanisms in both theoretical and experimental manners. This research will unveil the correlations between Fe biochar and electron shuttles, advance our understanding of electron transfer regime on biochar, and facilitate the application of hierarchical Fe biochar with minimal leaching in the future.

# 2. Methods and materials

# 2.1. Chemicals

Cellulose, as the main component in plant and wood biomass, was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and employed as biochar precursor without further treatment. All chemicals used in this study were of analytical reagent grade and solvents were of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade, including (1) $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 }$ , bisphenol A (BPA, $\mathrm { C _ { 1 5 } H _ { 1 6 } O _ { 2 } ) }$ , Oxone (potassium peroxymonosulfate (PMS) precursor, $2 \mathrm { K H S O _ { 5 } { \cdot } K H S O _ { 4 } { \cdot } K _ { 2 } S O _ { 4 } ) }$ , and ascorbic acid (AA, $\mathrm { C _ { 6 } H _ { 8 } O _ { 6 } } \mathrm { \Delta }$ ) from SigmaAldrich; and (2) oxalic acid (OA, $\mathrm { C _ { 2 } H _ { 2 } O _ { 4 , } }$ , methanol (MeOH, $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O H } )$ , ethanol (EtOH, $\mathrm { C _ { 2 } H _ { 5 } O H ) }$ , tartaric acid (TA, $\mathrm { C _ { 4 } H _ { 6 } O _ { 6 } } )$ , hydroquinone (HQ, $\mathrm { C } _ { 6 } \mathrm { H } _ { 6 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } )$ , and ${ \mathrm { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ from Aladdin, China., etc. Ultrapure water (UW, $1 8 . 2 \mathrm { M } \Omega \mathrm { c m } ,$ ) obtained from a Millipore Milli-Q Water Purification System (Milford, USA) was adopted for configuring all experimental aqueous solutions if not specified.

# 2.2. Preparation of hierarchical Fe biochars

Cellulose feedstock was sieved through a 120-mesh sieve (particle size $< 0 . 1 2 5 \mathrm { \ m m }$ ) prior to carbonization. The prepared cellulose powders were put in a Teflon-lined autoclave and underwent HTC at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 6 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . Then, $_ { 5 \ g }$ black carbons were impregnated with $_ { 1 5 ~ g }$ potassium hydroxide (KOH) and $_ { 0 . 5 ~ g }$ ferric chloride (FeCl3) in UW contained in beakers and the solvent was evaporated overnight. Subsequently, $1 0 \mathrm { - m i n }$ microwave radiation at $8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { W }$ was employed to achieve further graphitization with a high energy efficiency. A posttreatment was performed by irradiating the biochar sample with $8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { W }$ microwave under $\mathrm { N H } _ { 3 }$ atmosphere for $8 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ to tailor its surface basicity, followed by washing with 0.5 M HCl and deionized water (DI) until the eluent reached neutral pH value. The obtained biochar was denoted as Fe/CBC. The metal-free biochar sample was also produced as a control group without metal incorporation and post treatment, denoted as CBC.

Hierarchical Fe biochar coated with various electron shuttles (i.e., AA, OA, TA, and HQ) was prepared via a facile process without adding any chemical cross-linking agent. Briefly, $2 0 0 \mathrm { ~ m g ~ L ^ { - 1 } }$ Fe/CBC and $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ organic electron shuttle were added to $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ UW in a conical flask with a poly-tetra-fluoroethylene (PTFE) paddle to provide homogeneous mixing for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ at $8 0 ~ \pm ~ 1 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in a water bath. After reaction, the mixture underwent filtration $( 0 . 4 5 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ , MCE, Millipore) and the separated solids were transferred to vacuum dryer at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ after repetitive washing using DI and EtOH to avoid the release of electron shuttles during catalytic reaction, respectively. The extracted supernatant was used for the measurement of electron shuttle adsorption efficiency, and the dried samples were stored in a desiccator for future use. The biochar pre-treated with electron shuttles (i.e., AA, OA, TA, and HQ) was designated as Fe/CBC-AA, Fe/CBC-OA, Fe/CBC-TA, and Fe/CBC-HQ, respectively. The detailed schematic illustration of preparation procedure is presented in Fig. S1.

# 2.3. Characterization

Characteristics of the Fe/CBC composites were identified employing the scanning electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), high resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area and BarrettJoyner-Halenda (BJH) porosity, ultimate elemental, X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses. Detailed information on the material characterization is available in the Supporting Information (Text S1). The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, Bruker, Germany) spectra was performed with different spin-trapping agent including 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrrolidine N-oxide (DMPO) or 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidone (TEMP), and the specific experimental conditions are described in Supporting Information (Text S2).

# 2.4. Catalytic removal of BPA by the Fe/CBCs

The catalytic performance of all the biochar samples was assessed using a typical batch experiment procedure with PMS as oxidant for model contaminant (BPA) removal in aqueous solution. Specifically, $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ solution containing excess amount of BPA $( 1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ m g ~ L ^ { - 1 } } )$ was put in a $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ conical flask covered with foil. Then, $2 0 0 \mathrm { \ m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ biochar and $1 \ g \ L ^ { - 1 }$ Oxone powder (corresponding to $3 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ PMS) were added sequentially to initiate the reaction. The $\mathsf { p H }$ value of solution was adjusted with $0 . 1  { \mathrm { ~ M ~ N a O H } }$ or $\mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 }$ without buffer, and measured using a digital $\mathsf { p H }$ meter (Thermo, USA). All experiments were carried out at $2 5 ~ \pm ~ 1 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (if not specified) with vigorous stirring at $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ in a thermostat oscillator. At predetermined time interval, an aliquot of $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ m L } }$ solution was withdrawn from solutions and transferred into HPLC vials after passing through a $0 . 4 5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ pore-size mixed cellulose ester membrane filter (MCE, Millipore), and $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ MeOH was then promptly mixed with the extracted solution to quench the excessive radicals in bulk solution. After reaction was terminated at the end, all solutions were centrifuged at $5 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ for $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ to separate the catalyst for further regeneration (Supporting Information, Text S3), and the extracted supernatant was preserved for the determination of PMS consumption. Scavenging experiments were conducted in a similar protocol. Specifically, different radical scavenging agents were added in BPA solution to reach desired concentration (2 M EtOH, $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ TBA, and $1 5 \mathrm { m M N a N } _ { 3 } )$ , respectively. Then, catalysts and Oxone were added to initiate the catalytic reaction, and aqueous samples were also taken regularly as aforementioned. It is noted that the dosages of catalyst and oxidant should be further optimized before practical application because the pollutant usually exists in trace level under natural conditions. All experimental procedures were conducted at least in duplicate, and data were presented as mean values $\pm$ standard deviations.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Microscopic characterization of the CBC and Fe/CBC composites

Exterior organic electron shuttles with distinct molecular structures and functionalities were selected to be deployed on the Fe/CBC surface. It is worth noting that the Fe/CBC without ammonia activation was inert for accommodating electron shuttles due to electroneutrality of the highly graphitized surface and deficient basic functionalities [19,48]. As shown in Fig. 1, the adsorption efficiencies of the four selected organics (i.e., AA, OA, TA, and HQ) all transcended $5 0 \%$ , suggesting a good affinity and compatibility of biochar surface towards electron-rich substances after nitrogen incorporation. Two inherent factors of the electron shuttles (molecular weight and $\scriptstyle { 0 / \mathrm { C } }$ ratio) were plotted against the adsorption efficiency with linear fitting, which indicated that the molecular weight of organics exhibited weak correlation $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 2 7 )$ with the adsorption efficiency, while a strong linear correlation $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 1 9 )$ ) could be found for the organics without benzene ring (i.e., AA, TA, and OA). This reveals that the nitrogenous dopants tend to bind with the oxygen-containing groups (i.e., hydroxyl functional group) due to its stronger electronegativity. Besides, HQ showed the highest adsorption efficiency with complete adsorption, probably resulting from the strong $\textstyle \pi - \pi$ interaction between its benzene ring and the graphitized biochar matrices.

The XRD patterns of the Fe/CBC and Fe/CBCs accommodated with different organic electron shuttles are presented in Fig. 2a. The pattern of the pristine Fe/CBC demonstrated two sharp peaks at $2 6 . 6 4 ^ { \circ }$ and $4 4 . 9 9 ^ { \circ }$ , which were assigned to the (1 0 1) plane of $\mathrm { { S i O } _ { 2 } }$ (JCPDS PDF#46–1045, Fig. S2) and the (0 3 1) plane of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ (JCPDS PDF#35–0772, Fig. S2), respectively. The existence of $\mathrm { { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ was attributed to the ash content in the biochar, which showed no effect in the electrocatalytic redox reactions. The appearance of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ pointed towards the successful incorporation of Fe content, and the Fe metal centres could bind to carbon matrices via inner-sphere surface complexation with strong bonding. After deploying the four selected organics on the Fe/CBC surface, the two characteristic peaks corresponding to $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ decreased significantly, suggesting the washing-out of ash content and the coverage of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ contents with an organic layer. The small peak at $2 6 . 6 4 ^ { \circ }$ with a broader full width at half maximum (FWHM) value, which was assigned to the overlapping peak of the (0 0 2) plane of stacking graphite generated in pyrolysis [49,50], was not completely erased. This scenario further verified that a few graphite layers were generated and the Fe/CBCs were highly graphitized. Compared with the peak of amorphous carbon (i.e., broad peak at $2 0 { - } 2 5 ^ { \circ }$ [19,49]) in the pristine Fe/CBC, all the Fe/CBCs accommodated with electron shuttles indicated a wider peak range, and the increments of peak range could approximately correspond to their adsorption levels (i.e., $\mathrm { A A } \ < \ \mathrm { T A } \ < \ \mathrm { O A } \ < \ \mathrm { H Q } )$ . These results confirmed the successful accommodation of electron shuttles on the Fe/ CBC surface. Besides, a relatively sharp peak at $2 3 . 1 4 ^ { \circ }$ , assigned to the (0 1 1) plane of oxalic acid (JCPDS PDF#20–1817, Fig. S2), appeared on the Fe/CBC-OA pattern. This might indicate that OA was entrapped in the porous structure of the Fe/CBC via pore filling and partition rather than surface complexation. Comparatively, the other three organics were more likely to bind with the Fe/CBC surface via surface complexation in view of the absence of crystalline peaks.

The SEM images of the prepared Fe/CBC (Figs. S3a–b) showed a typical morphological feature of biochar with porous structure and an average particle size smaller than $2 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ . No Fe aggregation was observed on the biochar surface, suggesting a good dispersion of metal centres within the carbon support. A close view by HRTEM (Fig. 3) unveiled that a bonding interaction of the Fe and CBC and a hierarchical porous structure with an outside layer of ${ \sim } 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ could be clearly observed. The outside carbon layer with a crystalline spacing of $0 . 2 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ was assigned to the stacking graphite, whereas the inner core mainly consisted of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ with a crystalline spacing of $0 . 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . The strong covalent interactions between Fe and carbon were beneficial to mitigate the potential Fe release upon application [27]. Therefore, the Fe/CBC exhibited a well-developed hierarchical structure with an Fe core and a well-ordered graphitic carbon layer. After accommodation of the organic shuttles, the SEM images (Figs. S3c–f) revealed that AA and HQ remarkably reduced the amount of macro- $\mathrm { ( < 5 0 ~ n m ) }$ ) and mesopores $\left( 2 \mathrm { - } 5 0 \mathrm { n m } \right)$ ) on the Fe/CBC. The deposition of organics might cover the accessible active sites and account for the resultant unfavourable electrocatalysis (data shown later). In contrast, a wrinkled and corrugated layer-like structure was formed on the Fe/CBCs-TA surface without compromising its original porosity.

As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the Fe/ CBC curve demonstrated a type-Ⅳ isotherm with hysteresis loop located at 0.45–0.95 relative pressure $\left( \mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 } \right)$ , indicating a typical mesoporous feature [51]. The Fe/CBC-TA curve levelled off below $0 . 2 \mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 }$ and exhibited a smaller hysteresis loop. Besides, the pore size distribution (PSD, Fig. S5) suggested the consumption of mesoporous structure. These results confirmed the critical role of mesopores in TA accommodation. Compared with mesopores for mass transfer in adsorption process, micropores mainly act as catalytic sites and electronrich structural defects in AOPs. Thus, the preservation of micropores could be conducive to carbocatalysis.

# 3.2. Catalytic removal of BPA by the Fe/CBCs

The leaching of transition metals into aqueous solution is a critical factor that determines the sustainability of metal-biochar composites in redox-based processes. The degree of metal leaching is tightly subjected to pH change due to metal complexation and passivation phenomena [23]. In this regard, the metal leaching amount in the AOPs systems with different pH values was selected as the indicator. As shown in Fig. 5, the leaching amount of Fe from the Fe/CBC typically followed a unimodal trend with the peak amount $( 2 . 4 4 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L } ^ { - 1 } )$ ) centred at pH value of 6. This peak amount exceeded the legal limit of Fe discharged into water body imposed by the European Union $( 2 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L } ^ { - 1 } ) $ ). Intriguingly, the Fe leaching under alkaline conditions $\mathrm { \Phi } _ { \mathrm { { P H } } } = \mathrm { \ } 8$ and 10) were found to be higher than those under acidic conditions $\mathbf { \tilde { p } H } = 2$ and 4). This phenomenon was different from that of PMS and zero-valent iron (ZVI) biochar systems, where $\mathtt { F e } ^ { 0 }$ tends to undergo acidic dissociation with $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ acting as the desirable homogeneous catalysts for peroxide activation [10,11,21]. Several factors might account for this phenomenon: (a) the accommodation process of electron shuttle could remove most of amorphous Fe owing to the acidic nature of TA; (b) hierarchical structure could mitigate the acidic corrosion of Fe centre; (c) PMS molecule $\left( \mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - } \right)$ would be deprotonated in an alkaline condition which was not favourable for the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ recycling, because ${ \mathsf { S } } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 5 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ $( \mathrm { E } ^ { \circ } ( \mathrm { S O _ { 5 } } ^ { 2 - } / \mathrm { ~ S O _ { 4 } } ^ { 2 - } ) \ = \ 1 . 2 2 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ possessed a lower reactivity compared with ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ $( \mathrm { E } ^ { \circ } ( \mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - } / \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } ) = 1 . 7 5 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ [59]. The total amount of Fe leaching in this study was relatively low (maximal $2 7 . 7 \%$ , suggesting that the Fe/CBC might directly donate electrons to activate PMS without involving the phase transformation of Fe species [60,61]. The sustainability of the Fe/CBC accommodated with a representative electron shuttle (TA) was subsequently examined. The amount of Fe leaching from the Fe/CBC-TA was further reduced by 2.90–6.84 times in comparison with that of the Fe/CBC, indicating the favourable improvement of Fe/CBC’s sustainability after TA accommodation. Reduced metal leaching could mitigate the environmental concern and preserve more active metal centres for redox cycling, thus promoting electrocatalysis of the Fe/CBC.

There was only ${ \sim } 2 0 \%$ removal of BPA in $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ (Fig. S6) by PMS alone in the absence of catalysts. As demonstrated in Fig. 6a, the presence of the pristine Fe/CBC dramatically elevated the removal rate to $8 1 . 6 \%$ ; however, adsorption behaviour of the $\mathrm { F e / C B C }$ played a vital role $( 7 5 . 6 \% )$ in electrocatalysis. Many studies have also reported that adsorption is the rate-limiting step and determines the occurrence of electron transfer on carbon surface [19,30,49]. The catalytic performances of the Fe/CBC-AA (or -OA, -TA, and -HQ) were compared accordingly. The accommodation of AA, OA, and HQ decreased both adsorption capacities $( 3 4 . 1 - 5 6 . 2 \% )$ and total removal $( 5 7 . 8 \mathrm { - } 7 4 . 1 \% )$ regardless of their adsorption efficiency $( 5 8 . 1 \mathrm { - } 1 0 0 \% )$ ). The overdosed electron shuttles (i.e., $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ AA, OA, TA, and HQ, respectively) demonstrated similar catalytic performances (Fig. S6, ${ \sim } 5 0 \%$ BPA removal) in the homogeneous system, indicating the four selected organics possessed similar catalysis to produce ROSs individually. Interestingly, the Fe/CBC-TA demonstrated the highest removal rate of $9 5 . 1 \%$ , in contrast with its slightly decreased adsorptive performance $( 6 7 . 1 \% )$ due to the decreased $S _ { \mathrm { B E T } }$ . This phenomenon suggests a distinct mechanism of the Fe/CBC-TA from the other electron shuttles. Moreover, individual addition of Fe/CBC and TA showed a competition rather than a positive synergy (Fig. S6), and the activated ROS by free TA undesirably caused oxidative corrosion on the biochar surface (Fig. S4h), Recently, Xu et al. also found the adsorbed TA on biochar prepared at $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ could increase its surface C–O content and thus enhance the electron mediation for $\mathrm { c r ( V I ) }$ reduction [43]. Given the XPS spectra and FTIR curve of the Fe/CBC-TA, C–O was considered as the key oxygen-containing moiety in the catalytic performance. Therefore, the Fe/CBC-TA with the highest BPA degradation performance was chosen as the representative catalyst for subsequent investigation.

# 3.3. Catalytic mechanisms of the Fe/CBC-TA

Several scavenging agents were selected to $\dot { \boldsymbol { { u } } } _ { \mathbf { \lambda } }$ -situ quench respective target ROSs, i.e., EtOH towards both $\cdot _ { 8 0 _ { 4 } } -$ and • OH $( k _ { \bullet O H } =$ (1.2–1.8) $\times \ 1 0 ^ { 9 } \ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ , $k _ { S O _ { 4 } ^ { * - } } = ( 1 . 6 \mathrm { - } 7 . 7 ) \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } ~ s ^ { - 1 } )$ , TBA towards • OH $( k { \bullet } _ { O H } = \quad ( 3 . 2 \mathrm { - } 7 . 6 ) \quad \times \quad 1 0 ^ { 8 } \quad { \bf M } ^ { - 1 } \quad s ^ { - 1 } ,$ $\begin{array} { r l } { k _ { S O _ { 4 } ^ { * - } } } & { { } = } \end{array}$ (4.0–9.5) $\times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { ~ M ~ } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 } )$ , and $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ towards singlet oxygen $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ $( k _ { 1 O _ { 2 } } = 1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ , respectively. All kinetic data agreed well with pseudo-first-order kinetics model. As illustrated in Fig. 6b, the addition of EtOH significantly reduced the reaction rate constant $( k )$ of the Fe/CBC-TA from 0.175 to $0 . 0 6 1 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , indicating the coexistence of $\mathrm { \hbar } \cdot \mathrm { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ and • OH. The results of TBA addition $( k ~ = ~ 0 . 0 8 1 ~ \mathrm { \ m i n ^ { - 1 } } )$ showed that • OH accounted for a large fraction of involved reactive species. After the addition of $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ , the reaction rate declined to $0 . 1 0 9 \mathrm { { m i n } } ^ { - 1 }$ , indicating the evolvement of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . However, ${ \mathrm { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ cannot exclusively confirm the participation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , as it could simultaneously consume • OH and PMS molecules. Thus, EPR technique was employed to determine the generated ROSs.

Two different spin-trapping agents, namely DMPO towards $\mathrm { { \cdot } } _ { \mathrm { { S O } _ { 4 } } } \mathrm { { ^ - } }$ a well as • OH and TEMP towards $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ,$ were adopted to in-situ capture the generated ROSs in catalytic reactions. The Fe/CBC was also set as a control group to verify the critical role of TA. As illustrated in Fig. 6c, the captured species were identical for both Fe/CBC and Fe/CBC-TA when using the DMPO agent. The appearance of DMPO–OH (hyperfine splitting: $\mathbf { \Delta a } _ { \mathrm { H } } = \mathbf { \Delta a } _ { \mathrm { N } } = 1 4 . 9 \mathrm { ~ G } )$ and DMPO– ${ \mathcal { S } } 0 _ { 4 }$ (hyperfine splitting: $\alpha _ { \mathrm { N } } = 1 3 . 2 \textrm { G }$ , $\mathsf { a } _ { \mathrm { H } } = 9 . 6 \mathrm { ~ G ~ }$ , $\alpha _ { \mathrm { H } } = 1 4 . 8$ , and $\alpha _ { \mathrm { H } } = 0 . 7 8 \ \mathrm { G }$ adducts reinforced the conclusion in scavenging experiments. The evolvement of radicals for the Fe/CBC-TA procrastinated to $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ (peak intensity: 1724.6 a.u. at $5 \mathrm { { m i n } } \mathrm { { \dot { \Omega } } }$ ) in contrast to the rapid generation of ROSs for the Fe/CBC (peak intensity: 2451.6 a.u. at $1 \ \mathrm { m i n } $ ). The parallel EPR tests were performed with TEMP as the spin-trapping agent, and the results showed a distinct two-phase mechanistic route, in contrast to a onephase mechanism of the Fe/CBC. Characteristic peaks of TEMPO adduct appeared (hyperfine splitting: $\Omega _ { \mathrm { N } } \ = \ 1 6 . 9 \ \mathrm { G }$ ) in the Fe/CBC-TA curve and maintained an increasing trend with intensity value from 660.7 to 937.2 a.u. in $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . These results confirmed a two-phase generation of ROSs in the Fe/CBC-TA and PMS system, i.e., $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ governed the initial phase and then $\mathrm { { \cdot } } _ { \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } } \mathrm { { ^ - } }$ as well as • OH radicals took over in the following phase. This distinct two-phase mechanism was probably responsible for the smaller amount of Fe leaching from the Fe/CBC-TA, because $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ is an interfacial ROS generated on carbon surface in association with electron transfer regime. The higher RSE value of the Fe/CBC-TA (Table 1, $2 7 7 \ \pm \ 1 3 . 7 )$ compared with that of the Fe/CBC $( 2 6 3 ~ \pm ~ 9 . 5 )$ provided an additional basis for a higher efficiency of PMS utilization.

# 3.4. Recyclability of the Fe/CBC-TA

Recyclability of the Fe/CBC and Fe/CBC-TA was examined to verify the depletion level of catalytic sites. All catalytic runs for the Fe/CBC

TA outperformed those of the pristine Fe/CBC (Fig. 8b), especially in the second run with the total BPA removal rate of $7 3 . 0 \%$ vs. $4 7 . 6 \%$ after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . These results suggested that the metal leaching and passivation in multiple reaction cycles could be significantly alleviated due to the introduction of TA. After three-time recycling, the BPA removal rates of the Fe/CBC and Fe/CBC-TA both declined to $\textless 5 0 \%$ .

Compared with high temperature treatment $( > 8 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ that required high energy input, the low temperature treatment $( 2 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , exceeding the evaporation point of BPA) was employed to release deposited materials, preserve hierarchical structure, and reduce energy consumption. With thermal annealing regeneration (Supporting Information, Text S4), the catalytic performance of the Fe/CBC-TA could be recovered to $6 7 . 9 \%$ (i.e., 1.30 times of the 3rd run) because of the well-preserved Fe content. In contrast, there was a much lower efficiency of the Fe/CBC even after regeneration (i.e., $4 2 . 9 \%$ , 1.08 times of the 3rd run). The regeneration process could also recover its graphitization feature with lower $I _ { D } / I _ { G }$ ratio (0.87) and distinctive $G ^ { 2 }$ band compared with the Fe/CBC-TA (Fig. S8).

# 4. Conclusions

A novel hierarchical Fe-incorporated biochar was fabricated via a facile hydrothermal carbonization coupled with microwave irradiation process and $\mathrm { N H } _ { 3 }$ activation. After accommodating TA on the as-prepared Fe/CBC, a protective functional layer was formed on the biochar surface and reduced the amount of Fe leaching from 2.44 to $0 . 5 7 8 ~ \mathrm { m g }$ $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ at circumneutral condition. A large amount of C–O bond was found to be readily introduced on the Fe/CBC-TA while the well-developed core–shell structure could be successfully preserved. Its catalytic performance was evaluated using PMS activation system for the removal of BPA, in which the Fe/CBC-TA exhibited the highest removal rate of $9 5 . 1 \%$ compared with $8 1 . 6 \%$ by the Fe/CBC. The catalytic pathway was found to transform from a one-phase mechanism (mainly radicals) for the $\mathrm { F e / C B C }$ to a two-phase mechanism (hybrid ROSs with the emergence of mild $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } \mathrm { , }$ ) for the Fe/CBC-TA. The calculated activation energy (15.1 vs. $1 0 . 0 \mathrm { \ k J \ m o l ^ { - 1 } } \mathrm { \Omega }$ ) and the recyclability experiments also demonstrated the superiority of the Fe/CBC-TA. Overall, the newly evolved two-phase catalytic mechanisms by Fe/CBC-TA showed a promising environmental sustainability owing to its minimized metal leaching and enhanced recyclability.
