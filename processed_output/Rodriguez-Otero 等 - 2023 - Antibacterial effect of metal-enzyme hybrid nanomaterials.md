# . Introduction

Surface waters are particularly susceptible to bacterial contamination as they are exposed to animal and human waste, thus endangering the health of any living being exposed to them. As contaminated water can cause adverse health effects such as diarrhea that can lead to bleeding and gastrointestinal problems, currently estimated by the World Health Organization (WHO), at least 2 million people drink contaminated water worldwide, and as a result more than 829,000 people die from diarrhea and related diseases every year [1].

And despite the fact that in western countries this disease is not lethal, it is in other parts of the world. Consequently, it is vitally important to preserve both the health of the present and those of the future, when water stress hardens, to intensify water regeneration, as it will be a necessity rather than a choice. In Spain, the situation of water resources is considerably concerning, given its geographical location and current climatic emergency. It is estimated that $7 0 ~ \%$ of its surface area has a semi-arid climate, so this turns into a climate in which water availability is decreasing. As a study by the European Environment Agency shows, annual renewable freshwater resources in Spain fell by $6 5 ~ \%$ between 1990 and 2017 [2]. This trend is set to become even more rapid, as rainfall is also estimated to fall by $3 0 ~ \%$ in 2060, this, together with the fact that the rate of evaporation is increasing every year, results in a decrease in renewable water resources. Reuse of wastewater could provide a solution to the water shortages planned in the future and, therefore, treated water must be adapted to the degree of quality required for its intended use. Microbiological control and therefore the use of disinfection techniques is mandatory in water regeneration [3].

There are different species of bacteria that can be found in wastewater; however, the most commonly used parameter to determine the level of fecal contamination of water is Escherichia coli. However, other bacteria, such as Enterococcus sp. and Clostridium perfringens, are also considered important indicators. Furthermore, compared to other enteric bacteria such as E. coli, these can survive for a long period of time. In fact, fecal enterococci, such as E. faecalis and $E _ { i }$ faecium, have been detected in multiple wastewater treatment plant effluents (WwTP)

where they have not been adequately eliminated [4].

Although most regulations refer only to E. coli, in drinking water the concentration of Enterococcus sp. in drinking water has been shown, as in the last decade some bacteria such as this species have shown greater resistance to disinfection treatments than E.coli [5]. Specifically, some studies have shown that Enterococci can be resistant to chlorination and that low doses (less than 22 mg·min/L) can lead to reactivation of the bacterial population [6]. Moreover, conventional water disinfection to eliminate these pathogenic microorganisms is usually by application of ultraviolet light, ozonation, or chlorination. This step of inactivation of microorganisms is usually carried out in the final parts of the water treatment, and although chlorination is the treatment that has been applied most commonly in the past, alternatives are currently being sought as by-products of disinfection (DBPs) of possible carcinogenic character are formed [7].

Nanomaterials based on transition metals such as copper, cobalt, and iron have been shown to effectively eliminate bacteria in wastewater; moreover, most of the described materials have superparamagnetic properties, which is an attractive feature as it facilitates their recovery from water streams [813]. Although most of these nanomaterials studied for wastewater disinfection operate through activated oxidation processes by the generation of sulfate and/or hydroxyl radicals, copper-based nanomaterials such as CuFeO/CNT and Cu-NSs achieve wastewater disinfection by a different mechanism[8,10]. Some metallic nanomaterials have antimicrobial capabilities that can be applied in wastewater disinfection. Although the mechanisms have not yet been fully described, the most accepted idea is that due to the contact between the catalyst surface and the bacterial membrane, the membrane is broken and allows the access of copper ions or carbon nanotubes, which break into the metabolic processes of the cell causing cell stress [11,14].

However, traditionally different organic solvents and synthesis temperatures and pressures different from those of the environment have been used to obtain these materials [9]. Therefore, to develop sustainable disinfection processes, it is necessary to synthesize effective nanocatalysts under ambient conditions and without the use of environmentally harmful compounds. The high demand for the development of sustainable processes has encouraged research into the development of alternative processes [12,15]. The synthesis of NPs from biological organisms allows obtaining materials from compounds that are not harmful to the environment and in a relatively simpler way since moderate conditions of pressure and temperature can be used. In addition, some biomolecules of microorganisms have the capacity in many cases to stabilize nanoparticles, as in the case of enzymes, which are proteins that are naturally in contact with metal ions known as cofactors. Likewise, enzymes are biomolecules with catalytic functions in microorganisms; for example, catalase catalyzes the transformation of hydrogen peroxide into water, or lipase has the function of hydrolyzing fats. The effect of using different enzymes, of different sizes and origin, in the synthesis of metal hybrid nanoparticles has been extensively studied by Benavente et al. [16]. One of the main advantages of using enzymes is that they induce the formation of different metal species and morphologies depending on the enzyme used to synthesize the hybrid [1719] In addition, they favor ordered metal deposition on the protein matrix because they selectively coordinate with the carboxylic groups of some amino acids, avoiding aggregation between the NPs and also conferring greater stability against oxidation [20,21]. This hybrid production system has been applied by the same group also for the synthesis of copper nanoparticles [22] where similar results were obtained; thus, it is intuited that this synthesis method can be adaptable to the synthesis of NPs of other metals. In addition, these catalysts have demonstrated catalytic activity in degrading organic pollutants [23,24]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no such nanocatalyst has been tested in bacterial inactivation reactions in wastewater.

The main objective of this work is to evaluate the disinfection capacity of wastewater using novel metal-enzyme hybrid nanomaterials based on cobalt and copper species. The synthesis and characterization of the catalysts, as well as their application in the process of wastewater disinfection, are discussed in this work. Finally, qualitative studies on the presence of oxidizing species were also carried out using different scavengers, as well as studies on the recovery and reuse of the synthesized catalysts.

# 2.6. Disinfection reaction

The disinfection reactions were carried out in batch reactors using $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ of simulated wastewater inoculated with a wild strain of Enterococcus sp. to obtain an initial concentration of $1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { C F U / m L }$ . The wild strain of Enterococcus sp. was isolated from a WwTP located in Madrid (Spain). Enterococcus sp. (Gram-positive) is selected as the target contaminant, owing to its higher resistance to treatments compared to other Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli or Staphylococcus aureus [27,28]. The agitation of the reactions was carried out using magnetic stirrers (LLG- Labware, Germany), except in the case of materials with magnetic properties, where a paddle stirrer (Heidolph, Germany) was used. The reaction was initiated by adding different nanohybrids in different loads $( 0 . 0 2 5 - 0 . 1 \ { \mathrm { g / L } } )$ . Some of the treatments were intensified using PMS in different concentrations $( 0 - 0 . 5 \ : \mathrm { m M } )$ , and artificial solar radiation provided by the SUNTEST CPS solar chamber (Heraeus, Germany), and equipped with a 1500 W Xenon lamp with a radiation intensity of $9 0 \mathrm { m W } \mathrm { \cdot c m } ^ { - 2 }$ Temperature in the reactor was controlled around $2 0 \mathrm { { ^ \circ C } }$ , using a recirculation water bath.

# 2.7. Microbial methods

Simulated wastewater is inoculated with Enterococcus sp. to obtain an initial concentration similar to that of real water samples $( 1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { C F U / m L } )$ . For this purpose, $1 \ \mathrm { m L }$ of a wild-type Enterococcus sp. strain was incubated in a $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ Falcon tube in $2 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of Luria Bertani broth (Scharlau, Spain) at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ to ensure that the bacterial population reached the stationary phase. Then the Falcon tube content was centrifuged for $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $4 5 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ , the supernatant was discarded, and the bacteria were re-suspended with a sterile saline solution at pH 7 $( \mathrm { N a C l } 0 . 9 \% )$ at a concentration of approximately $1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { C F U / m L }$ and stored in the refrigerator until use.

The method used to count bacterial colonies is by counting $1 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ drops of 3 serial 10-fold dilutions in sterile $0 . 9 ~ \%$ NaCl in a Petri dish. Furthermore, to increase the detection limit, seeding beads were used to spread $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ of the undiluted sample (Standard Method 9125C). The detection limit (DL) of this experimental procedure was $1 0 \mathrm { C F U / m L }$ .For this purpose, Petri dishes with Slanetz&Bartley agar (Scharlau, Spain) are used and incubated for $4 8 \mathrm { { h } }$ at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , and subsequently the number of colonies is counted. The assays are performed in triplicate.

# 2.8. Recovery and reusability of materials

The materials were recovered in various ways. Those without magnetic properties were first recovered by sedimentation to remove as much liquid as possible, followed by centrifugation to remove as much supernatant as possible. In the case of magnetic materials, the recovery is much more efficient, since using a neodymium magnet the solid particles are retained while the supernatant is discarded. To study the loss of efficiency of the materials, three consecutive experiments are carried out with a duration of $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ each with the recovered material. In addition, a part of the catalyst was recovered after a single use, the water was removed using an oven, and the changes in its structure were analyzed by XRD, and ICP-OES characterization carried out at the ICPCSIC facilities.

# 2.9. Metal leaching analysis

Samples of $2 \mathrm { m L }$ were collected from the relevant reactions to analyze the amount of metal released into the water during the reaction by ICP-OES analysis. For $4 0 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ of the sample, $5 \mathrm { m L }$ of hydrochloric acid was digested followed by the addition of another $5 \mathrm { m L }$ of distilled water to obtain a final volume of $1 0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ .

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Synthesis and characterization of nanohybrids

Two different metal hybrid nanomaterials have been synthesized using the enzyme-induced metal nanoparticles technology [17,22].

First, a CoNPs-hybrid was prepared starting from cobalt chloride salt, an enzyme (GOx) at bicarbonate $0 . 1 \mathrm { { M } }$ at $\mathtt { p H } 1 0$ (see experimental section). After incubation, a reducing step was applied which after lyophilization gave finally the solid material. This material was characterized by the X-ray diffraction (XRD). XRD spectrum showed two peaks at $2 \theta = 2 3 ^ { \circ }$ and $4 6 ^ { \circ }$ , which can be attributed to the (003) and (104) planes of CoOOH (JCPDS card, 070169) (Fig. 1A, Fig. S3) [29]. The transmission electronic microscope (TEM) analysis (Fig. 1B) exhibited the formation of flowers structures with a crystalline area on the surface of the spheres and a diameter distribution size of 72.9 $\pm \ : 0 . 5 \ : \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. 1C). The high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) image (Fig. 1D) revealed an interplanar distance of $0 . 2 4 5 \mathrm { n m }$ , which correspond to CoOOH species as previously reported [29]. The ICP-OES elemental analysis revealed that CoNPs-hybrid contained $5 7 . 1 3 \%$ (w/w) of cobalt. In addition, magnetic properties were observed in this material which represent an advantage in the recovery of the catalysts in the reaction.

Secondly, a CuNPs-hybrid was synthesized starting from copper sulfate salt, and an enzyme at phosphate buffer at $\tt { p H } 7$ (see experimental section). After incubation, reducing step was applied and the solid was lyophilized directly after washing to get finally the solid material. XRD analysis revealed a pattern corresponding to copper phosphate with characteristic peaks of $\mathrm { C u } _ { 3 } ( \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 }$ (matched well with JCPDS card no. 000220548) as unique copper species (Fig. 2A, Fig. S4). In addition, the XPS spectra was performed, showing that the only existing species was the one corresponding to $\mathrm { C u } _ { 3 } ( \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 }$ (Fig. S5). Scanning electronic microscopy (SEM) revealed the formation of mesoporous structure (Fig. 2B). Transmission electronic microscopy (TEM) revealed the formation of cubic highly crystalline nanoparticles on the protein network with a nanoparticles size distribution around $3 2 \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. 2C-E). In this case catalase, a tetrameric enzyme with $3 8 5 \mathrm { k D a }$ , was used as protein scaffold in comparison with GOx, which has $1 6 0 \mathrm { k D a }$ (Fig. S6). In a recent previous work, hybrid synthesis using catalase as protein scaffold and without reducing agent gave the lowest cytotoxicity [17]. The ICP-OES elemental analysis revealed that this material contained $4 5 \ \%$ (w/w) of copper.

# 3.3. Intensification of disinfection by using PMS and solar radiation

In a first approach, the individual effect of PMS on the inactivation of

Enterococcus sp. is studied. For this purpose, different concentrations of PMS $( 0 . 0 1 { - } 0 . 5 \operatorname { m M } )$ are applied in the absence of solar radiation. Based on the results shown in Fig. S8, it was decided to choose the concentration of $0 . 1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ PMS as this is the maximum PMS concentration at which the bacterial population is not affected after $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ of contact.

Fig. 4 shows the combination of CoNPs-hybrid and CuNPs-hybrid with PMS in the reduction of Enterococcus sp. population, using a catalyst loading of 0.05 or $0 . 1 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ and a PMS concentration of $0 . 1 \mathrm { m M }$ previously selected. As can be seen, the combination of CoNPs-hybrid with PMS does not notably increase the removal performance of Enterococcus sp., which does not reach a minimum performance of 1-LR, which is the minimum to consider that a treatment has a disinfectant effect. Although this result is superior to that observed with PMS and CoNPs-hybrid alone.

While, in the case of combination of CuNPs-hybrid, in the presence of PMS at $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ the rate of Enterococcus sp. elimination is 2.81-LR. This result contrasts with that observed in the absence of PMS, i.e., the only CuNPs-hybrid activity, in which the detection limit was reached after $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ , or in other words, 4-LR were reached. The results indicate an inhibitory effect of PMS towards copper nanoparticles of both materials, which are able to inactivate Enterococcus sp. cells by themselves. The inhibition by PMS may be since $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ may be reacting with $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ ions released into the medium by PMS.

The results in Fig. 4 therefore show that either the current operating conditions are not optimal for the cobalt materials, or the catalysts are not suitable for PMS activation and thus bacterial inactivation. For this reason, it was decided to incorporate artificial solar radiation into the system, with the aim of testing whether there is any synergy between PMS, CoNPs-hybrid, and solar radiation itself.

Therefore, it was studied the inhibition of Enterococcus sp. by the application of solar radiation using the SUNTEST solar chamber to different loads of Co-NPs hybrid, as some cobalt materials have been shown to have photo-catalytic capacity [11,30]. These results are depicted in Fig. 5A, showing that the efficiency is better when Co-based nanomaterials are combined with solar radiation than under dark conditions. Furthermore, comparing with effect of the catalyst in absence of light above, it is clear that solar radiation has a positive effect on the catalytic ability of Co-NPs hybrid. Achieving with $0 . 0 5 g / \mathrm { L }$ around 2.5-LR decrease after $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction. This finding substantiates the absence of photocatalytic capability in the material under the investigated conditions, as solar radiation alone yields superior performance compared to the synergistic combination of both agents. An increase in the catalyst load results in a lower yield in the presence of solar radiation, as it generates a higher turbidity in the water sample, causing a greater dispersion of the radiation and generating shaded areas that serve as a refuge for the bacteria so that they are not inactivated.

The combined PMS/CoNPshybrid/Solar radiation system does not work as expected, obtaining a much lower performance than that obtained by the PMS/Solar radiation system, so it can be affirmed that the cobalt nanomaterials do not have the capacity to activate the PMS under the conditions studied.

To better understand the mechanism, a recyclability study of CoNPshybrid and CuNPs-hybrid was carried out, and it is represented in Fig. 6.

As it is observed in Fig. 6A, for CoNPs-hybrid, a rather low recyclability, especially in the third cycle of use where it barely inhibits 1-LR after $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ of reaction, when in a first cycle it doubles this result after $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Therefore, it is interesting to know the state of the catalyst after its use in the reaction, as well as the amount of metal released to the medium. The ICP-OES analysis showed a concentration of 0.96 $\pm 0 . 0 1 \mathrm { m g / L }$ of cobalt in water after $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction, which represents $1 4 \%$ of the amount of cobalt initially present in the catalyst. This loss of metal in the medium explains part of the decrease in efficiency with each cycle. On the other hand, it was compared the XRD pattern after the use of the nanomaterial, with the initial pattern showed in Fig. 1. In this case, it is observed a clear difference between both patterns, two peaks at 20 at $3 4 ^ { \circ }$ and $6 0 ^ { \circ }$ have increased considerably, being now the main peaks. While the previously main peak at $4 9 ^ { \circ }$ has decreased along with the one at $2 3 ^ { \circ }$ (data not shown).

To identify the change that has occurred in the catalyst during the reaction, an attempt was made to identify the species to which the 20 $= 3 4 ^ { \circ }$ , ${ 6 0 ^ { \circ } }$ peaks belong. As mentioned above, if it is true that compounds such as CoOOH and other cobalt oxides are found in the CoNPshybrid, the XRD in could indicate the formation of some other type of cobalt oxide. For example, the peaks $2 \theta = 3 4 ^ { \circ }$ ${ 6 0 ^ { \circ } }$ could fit with the (012) and (018) planes of the $\mathbf { \beta { \cdot } C o ( O H ) _ { 2 } }$ structure, which according to the literature is a product of the reduction of CoOOH [31].

On the other hand, the mechanism by which copper comes into contact can be understood from reuse experiments of the catalysts. In Fig. 6B can be seen how efficiency is lost in both cases with each cycle. It is especially noteworthy that in the third cycle the 2-LR reduction is not exceeded for both catalysts.

This loss of efficiency could be explained by the hypothesis that copper ions are released into the medium and are not recovered by the NPs. To confirm this hypothesis, ICP-OES analysis was also performed, being detected $1 . 3 8 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ of copper in water after $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction. This represents a loss of $1 2 . 5 \%$ of copper.

In conjunction with the ICP-OES analysis, XRD analysis was also performed after its use in the reactions, and it was determined that the XRD pattern before and after is identical for both cases. Thus, the stability of the copper species in the NPs is proved, therefore, it is proposed that the mechanism of disinfection of bacteria occurs by releasing ions originally of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ that are oxidized to $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ also generating reactive oxygen radicals.

# 3.5. Determination of reaction mechanisms

To determine the specific mechanism of action of both catalysts, and the exact contribution of each actor, were used as scavengers for the radicals; tert-Butyl alcohol (TBA) to inhibit the hydroxyl radical and furfuryl alcohol (FFA) to inhibit the superoxide radical ion in a molar excess ratio of 1:10 [11,32]. And as a scavenger for $\mathrm { C u ^ { + } }$ the metal chelator EDTA was used in a molar ratio of 1:20 as indicated in the article by Chatterjee et al. where the mechanism of antibacterial activity of copper nanoparticles is studied [26].

Fig. 7A shows the effect of the scavengers over the system CoNPshybrid/Solar radiation. It can be observed that FFA is the major inhibitor of the reaction, which implies that the $\boldsymbol { 0 } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet }$ radical ion has a greater relevance in the deactivation of the bacterial cells, however, there is also a slight contribution from hydroxyl radicals. Furthermore, the fact that the presence of TBA and FFA does not completely reduce the activity of the catalyst could indicate the existence of other species involved in the CoNPs-hybrid disinfection process. One of the hypotheses generated in the characterization of CoNPs-hybrid is that there may be a CoOOH surface layer surrounding the nanoparticles. The use of CoOOH as a semiconductor with the ability to generate the superoxide species by transferring electrons to the medium is reported in the literature [33]. Furthermore, in the study by Qiu et al., CoOOH is used as a coating for an electrolytic anode due to its high capacity as a photocatalyst to achieve water electrolysis [34].

While, in the case of CuNPs-hybrid, during the experiments, when EDTA was added to copper nanoparticles in water, the solution changed from an off-white to a greenish-blue color, an a priori indicator of Cu (II)- EDTA chelate formation. The graphs in Fig. 7B support this hypothesis, where the inactivation of the bacteria is completely palliated by the inhibitory effect of EDTA. Therefore, the action of Cu (II) ions in the disinfection reaction is clearly predominant. This result is very similar to that obtained by Chatterjee et al., in which it is concluded that the effect of reactive oxygen species is very small compared to that of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ . Therefore, this disinfection mechanism of CuNPs-hybrid is basically independent of species other than copper itself [26].

Therefore, considering the metal leaching results observed in Section 3.4., and the results observed in Section 3.5. after the application of different scavengers, Fig. 8 shows a proposal for the inactivation mechanism of Enterococcus sp. for the exposed treatments is made.

The copper catalysts CuNPs-hybrid succeeded in inhibiting Enterococcus sp. by an antibacterial mechanism, somewhat like the case of CuNSs [10]. Alshareef et al. [10], reached total disinfection after $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ of treatment, using a load of $2 . 5 ~ \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ of Cu-NSs. As it can be seen in Table 1, the disinfection rate is much higher for the catalysts synthesized in this work, also, if it is considered that the amount of material required for CuNPs-hybrid is lower to achieve higher efficiencies, so their superiority as a disinfectant is even more evident. Similarly, the method of synthesis of Cu-NSs requires the use of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as a surfactant, ascorbic acid as a reductant and a heating of the solution up to $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Whereas both MeNPs-hybrid is synthesized in aqueous medium at room temperature, only with a final lyophilization treatment. This last step could be the most relevant when comparing the sustainability of synthesis of these catalysts with others.

Regarding the efficiency of CoNPs-hybrid, when compared with other catalysts reported in literature, it is evident that it is not a particularly efficient catalyst. When compared with the disinfection rate of nZVI (3.5-LR in $9 0 \mathrm { m i n } \dot { }$ ), which also acts in the presence of UV light, the same efficiency is not achieved, although in neither case complete removal is achieved [35]. On the other hand, Rodríguez-Chueca et al. [11], reported the use of ${ \mathrm { C o F e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles on the removal of Enterococcus sp. Again, no effect of the catalyst in combination with UV-A radiation was observed, in the same way as in this work. However, in contrast to the CoNPs-hybrid, the ${ \mathrm { C o F e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles in combination with PMS allowed total elimination of Enterococcus sp. to be achieved within $4 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Thus, it appears that cobalt in the form of cobalt ferrite achieves higher efficiencies than on its own.

# 4. Conclusions

In this work, heterogeneous copper (CuNPs-hybrid) and cobalt (CoNPs-hybrid) nanohybrid catalysts were synthesized in a novel way following an enzymatic synthesis route to obtain catalytically active nanoparticles. Based on the methods for obtaining copper nanoparticles previously described by the CSIC laboratory, multiple routes were studied to obtain cobalt nanohybrids using Glucose oxidase solution (GOX), and copper using catalase from Aspergillus niger (CAT) solution (Catazyme). The complete structural characterization of both nanohybrids made it possible to determine for CoNPs-hybrid the formation of flowers structures with a crystalline area on the surface of the spheres and a diameter distribution size of $7 2 . 9 \pm 0 . 5 \ : \mathrm { n m }$ and revealed that it contained $5 7 . 1 3 ~ \%$ (w/w) of cobalt, and magnetic properties. While in the case of CuNPs-hybrid, it was revealed the formation of cubic highly crystalline nanoparticles on the protein network with a nanoparticles size distribution around $3 2 \mathrm { n m }$ , and the ICP-OES elemental analysis revealed that material contains $4 5 \ \%$ (w/w) of copper.

Studies on the disinfectant capacity of nanohybrids showed mixed results. While $0 . 1 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ CuNPs-hybrid was able to reduce the entire population of Enterococcus sp. within $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of contact, CoNPs-hybrid remained unchanged. For this reason, and since the TOF value of CoNPs-hybrid showed catalytic activity, it was decided to intensify the process with PMS and solar irradiation but with limited success.

Finally, the studies of species acting in the process showed that in the case of CuNPs-hybrid, practically the bulk of the antimicrobial activity is given by the presence of Cu, with the participation of reactive oxygen species being a minority. Whereas, in the case of CoNPs-hybrid, the major contribution comes from $\boldsymbol { 0 } _ { 2 } ^ { - \bullet }$ radical ion coming from the transference of electron to the medium from the CoOOH layer present in the material. Besides, there is also a slight contribution from hydroxyl radicals. In addition, the material recovery and reuse study showed better performance for the CuNPs-hybrid case, with little loss of efficiency after three uses. For both Co and Cu nanohybrid, leaching of metals to water is observed, representing $1 4 ~ \%$ and $1 2 . 5 ~ \%$ mass loss respectively.
