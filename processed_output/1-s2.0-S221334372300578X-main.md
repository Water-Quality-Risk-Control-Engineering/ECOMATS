# . Introduction

Water connects all the Earth's environments. Despite the benefits of transporting and exchanging nutrients with other environmental compartments, water is also responsible for the dissemination of both chemical and biological contaminants. In fact, the increase of anthropogenic activities allied with the current unpredictable weather conditions, such as severe droughts and floods, have been contributing to freshwater scarcity and pollution [1]. To avoid this type of pollution and ensure human and environmental health, efforts have been made to control the quality of water bodies. For instance, a final disinfection step has been introduced in wastewater treatment plants (WwTP) to control the levels of hazardous microorganisms in the receptor water bodies [2], that, sometimes, serve as the source of drinking water treatment plants (DWTP) [3,4].

Processes such as activated carbon adsorption, membrane filtration, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, ozonation and chlorination, alone or in combination, are commonly employed as water disinfection technologies in both WWTPs and DWTPs [57]. Despite their ability to reduce the water microbial loads, drawbacks, such as high operational and maintenance costs, formation of toxic byproducts and absence of residual effect, have been leading to the search for alternative disinfection processes, namely, effective advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) and technologies (AOTs) [2]. AOTs rely upon the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ , the superoxide radical $( \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ , the sulfate radical $( \mathsf { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet } )$ and the hydroxyl radical $( \mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } } )$ , among the most commonly generated ROS [810]. The generated ROS oxidize the organic compounds, including those of cell structures, producing water with low content of both chemical contaminants and microorganisms [2,8].

The high reduction potential of the sulfate radical $( \mathrm { E } _ { 0 } = 2 . 5  – 3 . 1 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ r for $\mathrm { { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot } }$ , against $2 . 8 \mathrm { e V }$ for HO•) and stability (half-life of $3 { - } 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ s for $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet }$ , against $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 8 } s$ for HO•) have been attracting the attention for its utilization in water treatment [1114]. The sulfate radical can be generated by the activation of the persulfate ion $( S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - } )$ . The fundamental principle of persulfate activation is to promote the breakdown of the covalent bond between the two oxygen atoms of the persulfate anion, producing $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ [15]. Generation of the sulfate radical can be enhanced by energetic activators (ultrasound, heat, and UV radiation), transition metals $( \mathrm { F e ^ { 2 + } , ~ } \mathrm { F e ^ { 3 + } } )$ or other chemical activators (KOH, NaOH), and carbon-based materials, such as graphene derivatives, carbon nanotubes and nanofibers [1518].

The use of carbon-based heterogeneous catalysts has been gaining popularity in recent years owing to their many advantages, such as lowcost production, high chemical stability, no toxicity and excellent catalytic activity [1921]. The mechanism of persulfate activation using carbon materials involves both radical and non-radical pathways. The predominantly known mechanism for persulfate activation is described by the electron (e') transfer between the active sites on the surface of the carbon material (e.g., electron-donor functional groups or electron-rich regions caused by structural defects) and the persulfate ion through the reaction described in Eq. 1 [22]. Reduce graphene oxide (rGO) is one among many materials derived from graphene. In order to increase the inherent catalytic activity of rGO, a recently studied alternative is the doping with heteroatoms (i. e., N, B and P), which are known active sites for persulfate activation [26, 27]. In particular, N-doping can enhance both the adsorption capacity and catalytic activity for persulfate activation  e.g., by tailoring the charge distribution of adjacent carbons, which in turn will have a better affinity for bonding to the negatively charged oxygen atoms in persulfate and thus activate or cleave the peroxide OO bond [28]. A nitrogen-doped rGO material, obtained by using melamine as a doping agent (rGO-M), has already been shown by our research group to be very effective for persulfate activation and degradation of different organic pollutants [29].

A possible solution to overcome the main disadvantage of using powder carbon-based catalysts in water treatment by AOTs (i.e., the need for an additional separation step to recover the catalyst from the treated water), but also to avoid the main disadvantage of membrane separation systems operating in continuous mode (i.e., membrane fouling), is to integrate AOTs and membrane separation in the same treatment process. Since AOTs exhibit an efficient degradation of organic pollutants, the hypothesis is that immobilizing a suitable catalyst on the membrane will enable performing filtration and advanced oxidation simultaneously, thus allowing to operate with pore sizes larger than those typically employed in filtration applications, which will ultimately prevent membrane fouling. This hypothesis was indeed successfully tested in a recent study of our research group, in which robust catalytic membranes were prepared upon incorporating rGO-M within a PVDF - poly(vinylidene fluoride) matrix [30] and compared to those obtained by simple vacuum filtration of the same rGO-M active phase into a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) support [30]. Briefly, the composite membrane (rGO-MPVDF) was shown to activate persulfate and promote the degradation of a mixture of 3 organic pollutants in continuous mode without notorious changes in both membrane flux and structure being observed; whereas the PTFE-supported membrane underwent partial desintegration [30]. The present work aimed to demonstrate the performance of the most robust treatment system for water disinfection in a continuous flow mode of operation. The contribution of each system component (PVDF membrane matrix, rGO-M catalyst and persulfate activation) in cell retention/removal was studied using a defined suspension of Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecalis and operating the treatment system in continuous flow mode for 6 h in each experiment. Then, the efficiency of this system was investigated to disinfect river water collected nearby the admission point of a DWTP, in this case during $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ operation. In addition, the microbiological stability of the treated river water was confirmed after storage for 7 days. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time carbon-based membranes are shown effective for water disinfection in a continuous mode of operation.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Reagents

Melamine $( 9 9 \mathrm { { w t \% } } )$ , 1-methyl-2pyrrolidone (NMP; $9 9 . 5 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ , polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP; MW: $4 0 , 0 0 0 \ g \mathrm { m o l ^ { - 1 } } \Upsilon$ , poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF; MW: $2 7 5 , 0 0 0 \ g \ \mathrm { m o l ^ { - 1 } } .$ and monosodium phosphate $\mathrm { ( N a H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } ) }$ were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium persulfate (SPS; $9 8 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ was purchased from MP Biomedicals. Disodium hydrogen phosphate $\mathrm { ( N a _ { 2 } H P O _ { 4 } }$ ; $9 9 \ \mathrm { w t } \% $ and N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine sulfate (DPD; $9 9 \ \mathrm { w t } \%$ )were obtained from Fluka. Absolute ethanol, sodium chloride (NaCl; $9 8 \ \mathrm { w t } \% )$ , 2-propanol $( 9 8 ~ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ , culture media Faecal Coliforms agar (m-FC), Slanetz Bartley Agar (SBA), and Plate Count Agar (PCA) were obtained from VWR. Demineralized water was produced in a Milli-Q water purification system $\mathrm { ( p H } = 5 . 9$ ; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

# 2.2. Synthesis of nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (rGO-M)

Nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (rGO-M) was synthesized as described elsewhere [29]. Briefly, $0 . 5 \ : g$ of graphite oxide and $0 . 4 \ g$ of melamine were dissolved in $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of absolute ethanol. This mixture was kept in an ultrasonic bath for $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ , distributed into crucibles, dried at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 5 \mathrm { h } }$ and kept at room temperature overnight. The resulting material was then subjected to thermal treatment at $3 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in a muffle furnace (SNOL) under static air, with a heating ramp of $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . After cooling, the resulting material was washed with $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ L ~ } }$ of demineralized water under vacuum filtration $( 0 . 4 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ ; Whatman), and dried at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight.

# 2.3. Fabrication of pristine PVDF and rGO-M-PVDF membranes

rGO-MPVDF membranes were fabricated by adapting a procedure previously reported [30]. For that purpose, $_ { 0 . 3 5 g }$ of PVP and $0 . 4 6 4 \ g$ of rGO-M were dissolved in $6 \mathrm { m L }$ of NMP, corresponding to 5 and $6 . 4 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ of PVP and rGO-M in the membrane forming materials, respectively. Then, the mixture was kept for $^ { 3 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ in an ultrasonic bath. Thereafter, $1 . 0 7 6 5 \ : g$ of PvDF was added to the mixture, which was then maintained at $4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with constant stirring for $4 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ . The polymer solution was left unstirred overnight at room temperature to let the air bubbles escape. Then, the polymer solution was placed on a glass plate and spread using a casting knife applicator (Elcometer 3580, Warren, MI), which allowed selecting the hickness of the cast polymer solution $( 5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ . This thickness was selected according to our previous work related with the degradation of organic micropollutants [30]. Subsequently, the glass plate with the polymeric solution was immersed in demineralized water to complete the polymerization reaction. After that, the flat membrane sheet of rGO-MPVDF was removed from the water, cut into circles with ca. $2 . 5 \mathrm { c m }$ of diameter and stored in sterile demineralized water until further use. Pristine PVDF membranes were also prepared without adding rGO-M in the fabrication procedure.

# 2.4. Membrane characterization techniques

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed in a Netzsch STA 49. PC/4/H Luxx thermal analyser. The sample was heated under an inert $\Nu _ { 2 }$ flow from $5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ .

Then, it was placed in a membrane cell and subjected to an increasing $\Nu _ { 2 }$ gas pressure in one side of the membrane until forming a gas bubble on the other side. # 2.5. Defined bacterial suspensions and environmental samples

Disinfection experiments were carried out with defined mixed bacterial suspensions or river water samples. Strains Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Enterococcus faecalis DSM 2570 were used to prepare the bacterial suspensions. These organisms were stored in $1 5 \%$ glycerol/ nutrient broth solution at - $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ When needed, the strains were thawed and pre-inoculated on PCA at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ . The biomass of each strain was used to prepare a suspension containing $1 \times 1 0 ^ { 3 }$ colony forming units (CFU) $\mathrm { \bar { m } } \mathrm { \bar { ^ { - 1 } } }$ in sterile saline solution ( $0 . 8 5 \%$ NaCl, w/v). The final defined bacterial suspension was obtained by mixing the same volume of each individual suspension, and immediately used as the feed for disinfection experiments.

River water samples were collected nearby the admission point of a

DWTP located in Northern Portugal. All samples were immediately transported at $4 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ into the laboratory and processed as the feed.

# 2.6. Experimental set-up for disinfection assays

Water disinfection by persulfate activation assays was carried out at ${ 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } }$ inside of a flow chamber (Telstar), using the experimental set-up shown in Fig. 1. In a typical run, the initial sample (bacterial defined mixed suspension or river water) was supplemented with $2 5 0 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ of the persulfate precursor (SPS), and continuously fed to the membrane cell (holding the PVDF or rGO-M-PVDF membranes with an effective area of $2 . 1 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ , at a flow rate (Q) of $0 . 1 2 \mathrm { m L } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . After the passage through the membrane cell, the treated water (i.e., the permeate) was collected in a sterile glass bottle. The water flux in the system was monitored with a $5 \mathrm { m L }$ sterile measuring cylinder.

Cell retention experiments were also performed with the pristine PVDF membrane in the absence of SPS. Furthermore, to assess the contribution of rGO-M on disinfection, assays with rGO-M-PVDF membranes in the absence of SPS were carried out. To study the effect of the catalytically active phase (i.e., rGO-M), experiments were performed with the PVDF and the rGO-M-PVDF membranes with added SPS. To assess the effect of SPS, initial samples with added SPS were re-sampled at the end of the experiments (6 h and/or $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ ). All the experiments were performed without pH adjustment, i.e., at the natural pH of the bacterial defined suspension $\mathrm { ( p H = } 5 . 9 \mathrm { ) }$ and river water $\mathrm { ( p H = 7 . 4 ) }$ . Each set of experiments was performed with three independent initial samples (mixed bacterial suspension or river water, $n = 3 \AA$ ). Detailed experimental conditions are shown in Table S1.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Characterization of the membranes

The rGO-M-PVDF membrane has already been extensively characterized in our previous publication [30], and the main characterization results are discussed herein. Briefly, (i) scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs allowed measuring the thickness of the membranes $( 2 9 2 \pm 2 0 \mu \mathrm { m } )$ and observing surface pores leading to a network of cross-sectional channels; (ii) transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the uniform distribution of rGO-M on both the pores and cross-sectional channels of the PVDF matrix; (iii) X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed that the interlayer spacing $( 0 . 3 3 5 \mathrm { n m } )$ of graphene sheets within rGO-M is unaffected during membrane fabrication; (iv) Raman spectroscopy demonstrated that no major structural changes occur upon the incorporation of rGO-M within the PVDF matrix; and (v) water contact-angle measurements revealed that the rGO-MPVDF membrane possesses a less hydrophilic character (water contact-angle $= 6 9 \pm 3 ^ { \circ } .$ when compared to that of rGO-M (water contact-angle bellow $2 0 ^ { \circ }$ , mostly due to the presence of carboxylic acids demonstrated by temperature-programmed desorption)[30].

Additional characterization was herein performed. In particular, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the membranes under study allowed confirming the presence of rGO-M in the catalytic ones (rGOM-PVDF). As observed in Fig. S1a and S1b, both PVDF and rGOMPVDF membranes are thermally stable up to around $3 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ As previously reported [31], the decomposition of PVP could be observed between 300 and $4 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , whereas the sharp decrease in the weight loss between 450 and $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ can be assigned to the decomposition of PVDF in both types of membranes. At $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , the rGO-MPVDF membrane showed a lower overall mass loss $( 6 0 \% )$ than the PVDF membrane $( 7 8 \% )$ , which can be explained by the presence of rGO-M ( ${ \cdot } 6 . 4 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ in the composite membrane).

The size of the largest pore of the PVDF and rGO-MPVDF membranes was 4.34 and $3 . 4 7 \ \mu \mathrm { m } ,$ respectively, which are within the typical range of microfiltration processes [35].

# 3.2. Bacterial retention by the pristine PVDF membrane

To assess the contribution of the membrane in the retention of the bacterial cells, a first assay was performed using the PVDF membranes without the addition of SPS. As observed in Fig. 2, the abundance of the total cultivable heterotrophs in the treated water (i.e., the permeate) was $1 . 9 8 \pm 0 . 5 1$ log lower than in the feed. PVDF is an inert polymer that do not exhibit antimicrobial properties, as reported before [36,37]. However, considering the largest pore size of the PVDF membrane $( 4 . 3 4 \mu \mathrm { m } )$ , and the diameter of E. coli and Ent. faecalis cells (about O.57 and $0 . 5 8 \mu \mathrm { m }$ , respectively), it is possible that some fraction of suspended microorganisms could have permeated through the membrane while other fraction may have been retained in the membrane surface. In addition, PVDF has been shown to have high affinity for bacteria adhesion due to its hydrophobic, rough, and less negatively charged surface [38]. Despite conflicting reports in the literature regarding the correlations between bacteria and surface properties, the negative charge of the phosphate groups of the phospholipid bilayer of the cell outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli, in this study) and of teichoic acids of the thick peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria (Ent. faecalis, in this study) have been pointed out as conferring adhesion properties to abiotic surfaces [3942].

# 3.3. Bacterial removal by the rGO-MPVDF membrane

To assess the contribution of rGO-M on the removal of the bacterial cells, assays were performed using the rGO-MPVDF membrane without the addition of SPS. As can be observed in Fig. 3, the presence of rGO-M contributed to decrease the abundance of the bacterial cells in the treated water (i.e., the permeate) $( 3 . 7 1 \pm 0 . 6 6 \log$ total heterotrophs removal) in a greater extent than with the pristine PVDF membrane $( 1 . 9 8 \pm 0 . 5 1 $ log total heterotrophs removal). The lower size of the largest pore of the rGO-MPVDF membrane $( 3 . 4 7 \mu \mathrm { m } )$ than that of the PVDF membrane $( 4 . 4 3 \mu \mathrm { m } )$ may have contributed to a more efficient cell retention. Additionally, rGO materials were shown to possess antimicrobial properties [4346]. On the one hand, the edge plane of rGO sheets can work like a knife, penetrating the cell membrane and thus leading to physical disruption of the phospholipid layer of the cells and cell death [40,47]. On the other hand, graphene-based materials were shown to induce oxidative stress in cells by electron exchange between biomolecules and rGO-M, increasing the concentration of intracellular ROS which can lead to the oxidation of cell macromolecules and cell death [48,49]. Therefore, the high decrease of cell density obtained when employing the rGO-MPVDF membranes (Fig. 3) can be ascribed to several factors occurring simultaneously, namely adsorption, size exclusion, and the expected antimicrobial properties of rGO-M.

# 3.4. Bacterial removal by pristine PVDF and rGO-M-PVDF membranes with SPS

To assess the capacity of the immobilized rGO-M of the composite membrane (rGO-M-PVDF) to activate the persulfate, the feed bacterial suspension was supplemented with SPS. The efficiency of the process on the removal of the bacterial cells was compared to the control, i.e., a run in the same conditions but with a pristine PVDF membrane.

# 3.5. Comparison of the number of cells retained by the pristine PVDF and rGO-MPVDF membranes

Fouling phenomena are important when considering membrane applications. The water flux remained practically constant during all the experiments (i.e., 6 h each), with no significant differences $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ being observed among the different operating conditions (PVDF or rGOMPVDF membranes with and without SPS) (Fig. S2). Nevertheless, due to the previously discussed ability of the rGO-MPVDF membrane to decompose SPS and promote bacterial cell destruction (Section 3.4), the amount of bacterial cells physically retained in each membrane was expected to vary depending on the membrane employed in the presence of SPS. To test this hypothesis, the cultivable microorganisms retained in the PVDF and rGO-MPVDF membranes were enumerated at the end of the experiments depicted in Figs. 4a and 4b). As observed in Fig. S3, the average percentage of cells retained in the rGO-MPVDF membrane $1 2 \%$ , $1 0 \%$ and $1 8 \%$ of total heterotrophs, E. coli and Ent. faecalis of the feed, respectively) were lower than those retained in the PVDF membrane $1 7 \%$ , $2 0 \%$ and $5 5 \%$ of total heterotrophs, E. coli and Ent. faecalis of the feed, respectively). The methodology employed for the enumeration of retained cells may have led to some variability among experiments, as confirmed by the relatively high standard deviations depicted in Fig. S3. As a consequence, no significant differences were observed $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ between the results obtained with the different membranes. Nevertheless, it is our understanding that the clear trend depicted in Fig. S3 confirms the ability of the rGO-MPVDF membrane to promote the elimination of a portion of bacterial cells retained during continuous operation in the presence of SPS. This feature is expected to enhance membrane resistance to fouling, and thus should be the object of future studies.

# 3.6. River water disinfection

To confirm the capacity of the proposed treatment system to disinfect environmental water, river water collected near the admission point of a DWTP was used as the feed. In this case, a large diversity of microorganisms and dissolved organic matter are expected, which can interfere with the treatment performance. Nevertheless, the results obtained for total heterotrophs, enterobacteria and enterococci show that the proposed system (i. e. , rGO-MPVDF membrane and SPS) was able to reduce the microbial abundance of the river water to values below the LOD (0.5 CFU $\mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ ) regardless of the microbiological parameter considered (Fig. 5). Indeed, the treatment system was able to maintain the disinfection performance even when the operation time was increased from 6 to $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ (Fig. 5a and Fig. 5b). Therefore, it is possible to conclude that the treatment performed during $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ in continuous mode with the rGOMPVDF membrane and SPS was able to comply with the microbiological quality parameters established in the EU Directive on water intended for human consumption [52], thus evidencing the stability of the rGO-M-PVDF membrane for up to $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ of continuous operation under realistic conditions. Indeed, only one study on carbon-based catalytic membranes operating in continuous flow mode has been reported in the literature so far, according to a recent comparative analysis [53]. Nevertheless, additional studies should be performed to test long-term stability of the proposed system. Likewise, the residual concentration of SPS in the treated water $( 1 9 1 . 9 \pm 5 . 8 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ should be the object of optimization in future studies.

# 3.6.1. Microorganism reactivation

According to recent reports, the goal of disinfection processes shoul shift from sterilization to achieving microbiological stability of the treated waters [54,55]. Briefly, these studies have shown that complete abatement of the microbial population actually provides better conditions for resistant (i.e., harmful) bacteria to proliferate due to lack of competition. Therefore, microbial regrowth $\mathbf { \tau } ^ { ( \mathbf { R } ) }$ )under storage conditions is a parameter that should be considered when pursuing water disinfection as an indication of microbiological stability of the treated waters [54,55]. Taking this into account, the reactivation of both cultivable microorganisms and selected genes (16S rRNA, intl1, $b l a _ { \mathrm { T E M } }$ and sul1) in the permeate collected after $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ of operation of the rGO-MPVDF/SPS catalytic treatment was evaluated after storage for 7 days (R-TW24, Fig. 6). River water (RW) samples before (RW0 and RW24) and after 7-day storage without previous treatment (R-RW0 and R-RW24) were run in parallel as controls. Briefly, the quantification of 16S rRNA with universal primers was done to evaluate the abundance of total bacterial cells [56]. The intl1 gene, which encodes the class 1 integrons integrase, was quantified because it is a potential marker for anthropogenic pollution, commonly linked to antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) found in pathogenic and commensal bacteria of mammals [57]. The $b l a _ { \mathrm { T E M } }$ and sul1 genes, respectively encoding resistance to Beta-- lactam and sulfonamide antibiotics, were selected since they are among the ARGs commonly found in the environment [58,59]. The presence of 16S rRNA in the treated river water after 7-day storage (R-TW24) at the same levels of those initially found in the river water (Rwo) is an additional indication of the microbiological stability of the treated river water obtained through the proposed treatment methodology.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, this study permitted to conclude that the pristine PVDF membrane was responsible for retaining $1 . 9 8 \pm 0 . 5 1 \log$ of the faecal indicator bacteria present in the feed $( 3 . 8 7 \pm 0 . 0 1 \log )$ but cannot activate persulfate by itself. The intrinsic antimicrobial properties of the rGO-M material were evidenced when the system operated with the rGO-M-PVDF composite membrane without added persulfate, the treatment system removing $3 . 7 1 \pm 0 . 6 6 \log$ of faecal indicator bacteria present in the feed. However, the bacterial abundance in the permeate was below the limit of detection $( 0 . 5 \mathrm { C F U } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 \cdot }$ only when the rGO-MPVDF was tested with SPS, showing a log reduction of $3 . 9 0 \pm 0 . 0 5 \log$ between the feed and the permeate. These results demonstrate the ability of the immobilized rGO-M material to activate persulfate, and the potential of the proposed system in water disinfection despite the pore size of the rGO-M-PVDF membrane $( \leq ~ 3 . 4 7 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ si.e., in the microfiltration range) being larger than those typically found in nanofiltration membranes for disinfection applications. Moreover, the percentage of the faecal indicator bacteria remaining on the surface of the rGO-MPVDF membrane with SPS was, on average, lower $( 1 8 \% )$ than that remaining on the pristine PVDF membrane $( 5 5 \% )$ . These results confirm that the hypothesis raised in the Introduction is also true for water disinfection and suggest that rGO-M promotes the elimination of a fraction of the bacterial cells retained on the membrane surface, thus mitigating fouling phenomena.

The capacity of the proposed treatment system was also demonstrated to disinfect river water as a source of drinking water. Total heterotrophs and faecal indicator bacteria were below the limit of detection $( < 0 . 5 \mathrm { C F U } \mathrm { \ m L } ^ { - 1 \cdot }$ in river water treated in continuous flow mode $^ { 2 4 \mathrm { h } }$ of steady-state operation). In addition, the microbiological stability of the treated river water was shown, with 7-day stored treated water containing levels of total bacteria and total heterotrophs similar to those found in the initial river water, but without detection of anthropogenic pollution markers (faecal indicators and the intl1 gene).
