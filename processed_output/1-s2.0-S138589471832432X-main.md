Different iron-containing materials (named as S1–S5) obtained through an oxidative digestion in acid media of basic oxygen furnace (BOF) slag were studied for the activation of sodium persulfate (SPS) to generate active radicals for the oxidation of propylparaben (PP), a model endocrine disruptor, in water. It was found that the materials with magnetite in its composition (S1, S2 and S3) effectively catalyze SPS for the removal of PP, and the reaction followed a pseudo-first-order kinetic pattern $( \mathrm { r } ^ { 2 } > 0 . 9 6 )$ . Within $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , a removal of approximately $9 0 \%$ of $0 . 4 \mathrm { m g / L }$ of PP was accomplished by ${ } ^ { 1 } { \ g } / \mathrm { L }$ of SPS in presence of $5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ of S1 catalyst with negligible iron leaching. Besides, the oxidation of PP was favored by higher dosages of S1 catalyst and SPS oxidant, lower initial PP concentration and neutral or alkaline pH. The generation of sulfate and hydroxyl radical was indirectly validated from the scavenging tests, using tert-butanol and methanol as scavenging agents. Based on the XPS analysis, it can be pointed out that the radical generation was due to the presence of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } { - \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } }$ on the surface of the catalysts. A degradation pathway based on the transformation products (TPs) resulting from PP degradation was also proposed.

# 1. Introduction

Basic oxygen furnace (BOF) slag is one of the main by-products generated during the process of converting carbon-rich molten pig iron into steel in Linz-Donawitz process [1]. In this sense, $0 . 2 \mathrm { k g }$ of BOF slag are approximately produced for each $\mathbf { k } \mathbf { g }$ of steel manufactured [2]. Taking into account that, according to the World Steel Association, the global crude steel output for 2017 amounted to $1 . 6 4 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 2 } \mathrm { k g }$ , the effective management of this slag is essential in making steel production a sustainable process [1–4]. BOF slag is a highly heterogeneous material, which is composed of calcium, iron, silicon, aluminum and magnesium. Currently, this slag is principally being reused as a building material: i) as aggregate in concrete, asphalt mixture, cement mortars or road pavements [1,5–7] and ii) as belite sulphoaluminate cement [8]. Nevertheless, this reuse represents around $6 5 \%$ of the produced steel slag, the remainder being disposed of by landfilling [9–11]. Therefore, additional routes of valorization should be investigated, including its use as an adsorbent material [12], and particle electrode [13–15]. It is worth noting that the presence of iron oxide in its composition together with its porous structure and easy recovery from water through a clarification process due to its high density $( \sim 3 . 7 { \ g } /$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } )$ [16,17], makes BOF slag a potential heterogeneous catalyst to be used in advanced oxidation processes [11,18,19]. Due to its properties, different organic compounds, such as alachlor [20], atrazine [21], trichloroethylene [22], 2-naphthalenesulfonate [23], sulfamethazine [24], azodye [25], polyethylene glycol [26], chlorophenols [18,27] and nitrophenol [28] have been successfully removed using Fenton or Fenton-like processes in presence of slag as heterogeneous catalyst.

In recent years, the use of persulfate as oxidizing agent is gaining increasing attention since it can be activated to produce sulfate radical $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } )$ in order to degrade organic pollutants in a selective way [29]. This activation can be carried out through either transition-metal heterogeneous catalysis using $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ or base-catalysed reactions. Therefore, both the presence of iron in its composition and its alkaline character makes BOF slag an attractive activator of persulfate [29]. In fact, BOF slag has been reported as a suitable activator of persulfate in the oxidation of groundwater polluted with tetrachloroethylene, 1,2-dichloroethane and methyl tert-butyl ether [6,30].

However, to the best of our knowledge, the activation of persulfate by BOF slag to remove endocrine-disrupting compounds, such as propylparaben (PP), has not been studied yet. These compounds are widely dispersed in water and other environments, and adversely affect the functioning of endocrine systems of humans and animals, because of their structural resemblance to natural hormones [31]. Besides, the environmental fate of these species is largely unknown; it can persist in the environment at unexpected levels, undergo bioaccumulation and even react with other compounds to generate new unpredictable contaminants [32–35]. This is the reason why there is a growing concern about endocrine disruptors and their presence in waters. Therefore, finding efficient and environmentally friendly methods for the degradation of this type of pollutants is vital to tackle this problem.

In this regard, the purpose of this work is to evaluate, for the first time ever, the use of chemically modified BOF slag, as heterogeneous catalyst for the activation of sodium persulfate, aiming at the effective degradation of propylparaben (PP), a model endocrine disruptor. Special attention is paid to the effect of synthesis conditions on the resulting catalytic properties and the subsequent activity towards PP degradation.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Steel slag and reagents

The sample of steel slag used in this study was provided by a steel plant located in Asturias (North of Spain). The slag is a highly heterogeneous material, mainly composed of calcium, iron, silicon, magnesium, aluminium, manganese and phosphorus. Other constituents, including titanium, potassium and sodium were also present at very low concentrations. The detailed chemical composition measured by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF) can be found in Table S1 of the Supplementary Material. Its granulometric distribution lies between 1.25 and $1 0 \mathrm { m m }$ . It was necessary to mill and sieve the slag prior to the characterization and the synthesis of the catalysts. The fraction with sizes below $3 1 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ was used in both cases. All reagents needed for catalyst preparation, namely nitric acid $( \mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 } \geq 6 5 \% )$ , hydrochloric acid $\mathrm { ( H C l } \geq 3 7 \% \big )$ and hydrogen peroxide $\mathrm { ( H } _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } \ 3 0 \% \mathrm { ) }$ ) (puriss. grade in all cases), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Propylparaben (PP, $\mathrm { C } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { H } _ { 1 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 } \geq 9 9 \% ]$ , sodium persulfate (SPS, $\mathrm { N a _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } } \geq 9 9 \% )$ , sodium chloride (NaCl $9 9 . 5 \%$ ), sodium bicarbonate $\mathrm { ( N a H C O _ { 3 } ~ 9 9 . 7 \% ) }$ ), methanol $\mathrm { \ C H _ { 3 } O H }$ anhydrous $9 9 . 8 \%$ ) and tert-butanol $\mathrm { ( C _ { 4 } H _ { 1 0 } O }$ anhydrous $\geq 9 9 . 5 \%$ ) were also obtained from SigmaAldrich.

# 2.2. Synthesis of the steel-slag derived catalysts by oxidative digestion in acidic media

Five catalysts, named as S1–S5, were prepared by oxidative digestion in acidic media, using steel slag as precursor. Different digesting agents, including a mixture of HCl and $\mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 }$ and/or $\mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 }$ (diluted or concentrated) were used, and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ (diluted or concentrated) was employed as oxidizing agent. Digestion and oxidation times varied between $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ and $^ { 7 2 \mathrm { h } }$ . These conditions were selected in order to improve the textural properties of the catalysts, namely the BET surface area and pore volume, in an attempt to maximize the catalytic activity. The synthesis procedure was as follows: in all cases, $3 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of distilled water was added to $5 0 g$ of steel slag and the resulting blend was mixed for $1 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $1 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ . After this stage, $7 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of diluted $( 5 \mathbf { M } )$ or concentrated $\mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 }$ (12 M) or $\mathrm { H C l } / \mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 }$ (3:1) were added slowly at a flow rate of $0 . 5 \mathrm { m L / m i n }$ at $3 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ for digestion times of 24, 48 or $^ { 7 2 \mathrm { h } }$ , depending on the material prepared (see Table 1). It was necessary to add the acid/s slowly because the reaction was vigorous. Next, $3 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of diluted $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ $( 1 5 \% \mathrm { w / w } )$ or concentrated $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ $3 0 \%$ w/w) were added at a flow rate $0 . 2 5 \mathrm { m L / m i n }$ at $2 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ for oxidation times of $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ , $4 8 \mathrm { h }$ and $^ { 7 2 \mathrm { h } }$ (see Table 1). The addition of hydrogen peroxide was also performed very slowly because the reaction is even more vigorous than the digesting one. The last step was the centrifugation of mixture at $_ { 1 3 , 0 0 0 g }$ for $2 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . After this, the solid was washed several times with distilled water, centrifuged and dried at $1 2 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $4 8 \mathrm { h }$ .

# 2.3. Degradation experiments

A stock solution of PP $\mathrm { \Omega } 8 0 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ was prepared and the appropriate volume was mixed with ultrapure water (UPW) to make PP solutions in the range $0 . 2 \mathrm { - } 1 . 6 \mathrm { m g / L }$ . Experiments were conducted in a cylindrical glass vessel which was loaded with $6 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of the PP solution. The appropriate amounts of SPS and catalyst were then added to achieve concentrations in the range $_ { 0 - 2 } \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ and $0 { - } 5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , respectively and the reaction took place under magnetic stirring at ambient temperature and solution pH; the solution was not buffered and its $\mathsf { p H }$ value dropped from ca 6.2 at the beginning of the experiment to no lower than $4 \pm \ : 0 . 2$ after $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of reaction. An additional experiment was performed in commercially available bottled water (BW) containing various ions, mainly bicarbonate at $2 5 0 \mathrm { m g / L } ;$ ; in this case, the matrix pH dropped from 5.8 to 5 after $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of reaction. Samples of ca $^ \mathrm { 1 m L }$ were periodically withdrawn from the vessel, quenched with methanol, filtered and finally analyzed by liquid chromatography.

# 2.4. Characterization techniques of the steel slag and the derived catalysts

Shimadzu EDX-720 energy dispersive X-Ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer was used to determine the chemical composition of steel slag. A powder diffractometer, PANalyticalX’Pert Pro, using Cu Kα radiation $\langle \lambda _ { \mathrm { \tiny ~ K a } } = 1 . 5 4 0 6 ~ \mathring { \mathrm { A } } \rangle$ ) and a graphite secondary monochromator, was employed to determine the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. These data were registered for 2θ values from ${ { 1 0 } ^ { \circ } }$ to $8 0 ^ { \circ }$ using a step of $0 . 0 2 ^ { \circ }$ , the scan step time being 1 s. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the catalysts were measured on KBr matrix, using a Perkin-Elmer PARAGON 1000 spectrometer in the range of $4 0 0 0 { - } 4 5 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ with a resolution of $4 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ . The carbon content was determined using a Perkin Elmer 2400 elemental analyzer. Micromeritics ASAP 2020 instrument was used to perform nitrogen adsorption-desorption tests at $7 7 \mathrm { K } ,$ in order to determine the specific surface area and porous structure of the materials. Before such tests, it was necessary to degas the catalysts at $1 2 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ \mathrm { 1 0 h }$ . Mesoporosity was analysed through the BJH method [36]. The microstructure of the catalysts was determined using a JEOL JMS-6610LV scanning electron microscope (SEM) with an accelerating voltage between 0.3 and $3 0 \mathbf { k } \mathbf { V }$ . Prior to the SEM analyses, the catalysts were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold. Transmission electron micrographs (HRTEM), high angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis were carried out on a MET JEOL-JEM 2100F microscope with an operating voltage of $2 0 0 \mathrm { k V }$ . Before the analysis, the catalysts were dispersed into ethanol, sonicated for 2 min and dropped onto carbonated copper grids.

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a SPECS spectrometer equipped with a Phoibos $1 0 0 \mathrm { M C D }$ analyzer and a monochromatized X-ray Mg Kα (1253.6 eV). High resolution spectra were taken with an energy pass of $3 0 \mathrm { e V }$ and an energy step of $0 . 1 \mathrm { e V } .$ . An estimation of the intensities was done after a calculation of each peak integral, S-shaped background subtraction and fitting the experimental curve to a combination of a Lorentzian $( 3 0 \% )$ and Gaussian $( 7 0 \% )$ lines. Binding energies referenced to the C1s line at $2 8 4 . 8 \mathrm { e V }$ , have an accuracy of $\pm \ 0 . 1 \mathrm { e V }$ .

An Agilent 7500ce ICP-MS spectrometer was used to measure the percentage of iron in the catalysts. The internal standard employed was Rhodium $\mathrm { ( ^ { 1 0 3 } R h ) }$ . Prior to ICP-MS analysis, it was necessary to carry out a microwave digestion of the catalysts in acidic media using a Perkin Elmer Multiwave 3000. A hundred mg of each of the catalysts were added to $8 \mathrm { m L }$ of aqua regia (3HCl: $\mathrm { 1 H N O _ { 3 } }$ mixture) in closed teflon vessels.

Propylparaben was analyzed by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC: Alliance 2695, Waters) using a Kinetex XB-C18 100A column $( 2 . 6 \mu \mathrm { m }$ , $2 . 1 \mathrm { m m } \times 5 0 \mathrm { m m } )$ ) and a $0 . 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ inline filter (KrudKatcher Ultra), both purchased from Phenomenex. The mobile phase was a 50:50 water:acetonitrile mixture eluting isocratically at $0 . 3 5 \mathrm { m L / m i n }$ and $4 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , while the injection volume was $4 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ . Detection was achieved at $2 5 4 \mathrm { n m }$ using a photodiode array detector (Waters 2996 PDA detector).

An LTQ-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) was used for the characterization of transformation products (TPs) of PP. The analyses were run on a C18 Hypersil Gold, $1 0 0 \mathrm { m m } \times 2 . 1 \mathrm { m m }$ i.d., $1 . 9 \mu \mathrm { m }$ particle size (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, USA), kept at $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Injection volume was $2 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ and flow rate $3 0 0 \mu \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m i n }$ . # 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. XRD analysis

The crystalline structure of the catalysts S1–S5 was determined through XRD measurements. Fig. 1 shows the diffraction patterns of the steel slag-derived catalysts.

The XRD pattern of sample S1 (Fig. 1a) shows three strong sharp diffraction peaks indicating a significant degree of crystallinity. The first peak at $2 6 . 6 ^ { \circ }$ can be assigned to the most characteristic peak of crystalline silicon dioxide $\mathbf { ( S i O _ { 2 } ) }$ . The d-value corresponding to this diffraction peak is $3 . 3 5 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ (0 1 1), which corresponds to the d-spacing of quartz [37,38]. The peaks at $3 3 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ , $3 5 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ and $4 6 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ are characteristic of srebrodolskite $\mathbf { ( C a _ { 2 } F e _ { 2 } O _ { 5 } ) }$ , magnetite $\left( \mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } \right)$ and akermanite $\mathrm { ( C a _ { 2 } M g S i _ { 2 } O _ { 7 } ) }$ , respectively. It should be noted that these compounds exhibit a great number of overlapped peaks. Nevertheless, the most intense peaks for $\mathrm { C a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 5 }$ , at $3 3 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ (1 4 1), $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } ,$ at $3 5 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ (2 1 1) and $\mathrm { C a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { M g } \mathrm { S i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 7 }$ , at $3 1 . 2 ^ { \circ }$ (2 1 1) can also be distinguished, proving their presence in sample S1 [39,40].

Fig. 1e depicts the XRD pattern of sample S5, which is very similar to that of the sample S4. The differences found were as follows: i) a broad peak was observed at $2 4 . 1 ^ { \circ } \ ( 0 1 2 )$ indicating the presence of hematite (JCPDS no. 33-0664) as minority phase [44], ii) another characteristic peak of goethite at $6 8 . 2 ^ { \circ }$ (1 2 2) was identified and iii) $\mathrm { C a F e _ { 3 } S i _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ( O H ) }$ was not detected.

In both S4 and S5 samples, the diffraction lines exhibited little intensity, this being lower in sample S5. Besides, broad peaks were observed. This suggested that the use of very strong acidic media affected or degraded the crystalline structure of the steel slag significantly, thus forming poorly ordered materials with very fine particles.

# 3.2. Chemical composition

The elemental composition of the steel slag-derived catalysts are shown in Table 2. It should be noted that the content of Fe, Ca, Mg and Al of samples S1, S2 and S3 is higher than in samples S4 and S5, which can be attributed to the harshest conditions employed in the synthesis of the latter and the subsequent increase in the percentage of the abovementioned elements that is leached from them. While S1, S2 and S3 were subjected to digestion with $\mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 }$ for $2 4 { - } 4 8 \mathrm { h }$ , S4 and S5 were in contact with a stronger oxidizing $\mathrm { ( H C l / H N O _ { 3 } ) }$ for a longer time, namely $^ { 7 2 \mathrm { h } }$ . Besides, carbon was also determined in all samples, thus indicating the existence of carbonation as it was confirmed by FTIR analysis. The extent to which carbonation occurs is dependent on the calcium content of the catalysts, as this phenomenon is due to the reaction between calcium and atmospheric carbon dioxide, yielding calcium carbonate [45]. Since S4 and S5 underwent more severe leaching of their components than the other three catalysts, their calcium content will also be lower, thus limiting the degree of carbonation, which accounts for the lower content of C on these substrates.

# 3.4. Textural characterization

Fig. 3 depicts the $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption-desorption isotherms of the different catalysts S1–S5. On viewing the results, such isotherms can be categorized as type IV according to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemist (IUPAC) classification [53]. Besides, a hysteresis loop was observed in all cases, linked to the filling and emptying of mesopores by capillary condensation and evaporation, respectively. These hysteresis loops can be classified as type H3, which is characteristic of solids containing slit-shaped pores and aggregates of platy particles [54]. It should be noted that macroporosity was also present in the catalysts due to the continuous nitrogen uptake observed at relative pressures close to 1.

The textural parameters of the catalysts can be found in Table 3. Specific surface areas were determined using the Brunauer-EmmettTeller (BET) method. The adsorption and desorption branches of the $\Nu _ { 2 }$ isotherms allowed us to obtain the mesoporosity characteristics of the catalysts using Barrett et al. method [55]. In view of the results, the procedure used in the synthesis had a marked effect on the textural properties. In this sense, the catalysts obtained in strong (S3) and very strong acidic media (S4 and S5) showed higher values of BET surface area and smaller mesopores diameters than those obtained in acidic media. In this sense, catalyst S4 exhibited the highest specific surface area $( 4 4 0 . 8 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ and pore volume $( 0 . 7 6 8 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } / \mathrm { g } )$ followed by catalyst S5 with a BET surface area and pore volume values $\sim 1 4 \%$ and $\sim 5 0 \%$ lower than that of S4, respectively (Table 3). The specific surface areas of samples S1 and S2 (57.1 and $7 0 . 5 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ were $8 7 \%$ and $8 4 \%$ lower than that of S4, respectively. The increase in the concentration of acid and/or the digesting time of the steel slag led to a higher solubilization of the metals contained in the slag. This caused a greater percentage of pores inside the particles, which were previously occupied by such metals.

# 3.5. Microstructure: SEM and TEM microscopy

Based on SEM images of samples S1–S5 (Fig. 4a), differences in the structure of the synthesized solids from a heterogeneous mixture of particle aggregates with different sizes and shapes (samples S1, S2 and S3) to essentially layered particle aggregates (samples S4 and S5), can be observed. Such layered particle aggregates are mainly composed of silicon dioxide due to the leaching of alkali and alkaline earth metals and heavy metals present in the steel slag in very strong acidic media.

This can be proved by HAADF-STEM images of samples S1 and S5 and their corresponding elemental mapping (Fig. 5). Thus, Si and O were the most abundant elements in sample S5, whereas in sample S1, a mixture of particle aggregates of akermanite $\mathrm { ( C a _ { 2 } M g S i _ { 2 } O _ { 7 } ) }$ , srebrodolskite $\mathbf { ( C a _ { 2 } F e _ { 2 } O _ { 5 } ) }$ and silicon dioxide can be distinguished. It should be noted that Si and O are distributed homogeneously and Fe is present at higher concentration on darker particles in sample S5. Such results were further corroborated by means of EDX analysis, which was applied to several particles on both S1 and S5 samples. The corresponding results are summarized in Section 2 of the Supplementary Material. Moreover, inter particle cavities were observed in all solids, which are probably responsible for macroporosity (Fig. 4a). TEM images allowed us to obtain an in-depth knowledge of the microstructure of the materials. Thus, a porous structure with slit-shaped pores was identified in all cases (Fig. 4b), which is in agreement with the formation of the type H3 hysteresis loop.

# 3.6. Screening of catalytic activity and activation mechanism

The relative catalytic activity of samples S1–S5 was tested for the degradation of $0 . 4 \mathrm { m g / L }$ PP with ${ } ^ { 1 } { \ g } / \mathrm { L }$ SPS and the results are summarized in Fig. 6. Experiments with samples S1–S3 lead to nearly $9 0 \%$ PP degradation in $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , while runs with S4 and S5 give only $5 0 \mathrm { - } 5 5 \%$ ; notably, these values are almost identical to the extent of degradation that can be achieved by the direct oxidative action of SPS in the absence of catalyst, as demonstrated by the dashed line in Fig. 6 (see also Fig. 7a). Samples S1–S3 have higher iron content than S4 and S5 (Table 2) although their surface area is lower (Table 3).

These findings can be explained on the basis of the effects of the preparation methods on the slag. On one hand, the selection of stronger conditions during the synthesis of the modified slags involved a higher leaching of either iron or alkali and alkaline earth metals, as it was previously explained. Several authors have reported that iron-based heterogeneous catalysts can effectively activate persulfate and generate sulfate radicals [56]. In this sense, the overall reactions involved during the generation of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - * }$ due to the activation of persulfate by iron are described in Eqs. (1)–(6) [57–60].

$$

$$
\begin{array} { l } { { S O _ { 4 } ^ { - * } + F e ^ { 2 + }  F e ^ { 3 + } + S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } } } \\ { { \ } } \\ { { S O _ { 4 } ^ { - * } + S O _ { 4 } ^ { - * }  2 S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } } } \\ { { \ } } \\ { { S O _ { 4 } ^ { - * } + H O ^ { - }  H S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } } } \end{array}

Therefore, once activated by iron, two types of radicals can be produced by SPS: hydroxyl radicals $( H O ^ { \bullet } )$ and sulfate radicals $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } \cdot )$ . In order to identify these radical species in the media, two alcohols (methanol and tert-butanol) were used as quenching agents. Methanol can rapidly react with both $H O ^ { \bullet }$ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ ∙, the rate constants being $6 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m o l } { \cdot } s$ and $1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 7 } \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m o l } { \cdot } s$ at $2 9 8 \mathrm { K } ,$ , respectively [61–63]. Nevertheless, tert-butanol reacts much more quickly with $H O ^ { \bullet }$ (rate constant at $2 9 8 \mathrm { K } = 5 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m o l } { \cdot } s$ than $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - \cdot }$ (rate constant at $2 9 8 \mathrm { K } = 4 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m o l } { \cdot } s )$ [59,60,64]. Results related to the experiments with quenching agents are depicted in Fig. 9. Without these agents, $9 2 . 5 \%$ of PP was removed in $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , the value of $\bf k _ { a p p }$ being $2 7 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } }$ . Nevertheless, the addition of $5 g / \mathrm { L }$ of methanol and tert-butanol reduced in approximately one order of magnitude the kapp values, thus these being 2.3 × 10−3 min−1 and 3.3 × 10−3 min−1 , respectively. Therefore, the decrease in $\bf k _ { a p p }$ was more significant for methanol than for tert-butanol, which suggested that the main radical species generated during the activation of SPS by iron contained in the steel-slag derived catalysts were sulfate radicals. These was also reported by Tan et al. [65], during the degradation of acetaminophen in presence of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles. # 3.7. Factors affecting degradation

The effect of S1 and SPS concentrations on PP degradation is depicted in Fig. 7. Similarly to Fenton and alike systems, the rate increases with increasing catalyst and oxidant concentrations at the conditions under consideration. To elucidate reaction rates at a simple level, PP degradation is considered to follow pseudo-first order kinetics, i.e.:

$$
- \frac { d C } { d t } = k _ { a p p } C \Rightarrow \ln \left( \frac { C _ { 0 } } { C } \right) = k _ { a p p } t
$$

Plotting the concentration-time profiles of Fig. 7 in the form of Eq. (7), apparent rate constants $( \bf k _ { a p p } )$ can be computed from the slopes of the resulting straight lines. The rate constant becomes $6 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ $( 9 7 . 1 \% )$ , $1 5 . 1 \times { { 1 0 } ^ { - 3 } }$ $( 9 9 . 4 \% )$ and $2 7 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } }$ $( 9 8 . 7 \% )$ at 0, 25 and $5 0 \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ S1, respectively, while it takes values of $7 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ $( 9 6 . 1 \% )$ , $2 7 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } ( 9 8 . 7 \% )$ and $6 3 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } }$ $( 9 9 . 2 \% )$ at 0.5, 1 and ${ 2 g / \mathrm { L } }$ SPS, respectively. The numbers in parentheses show the coefficients of linear regression, $\mathrm { r } ^ { 2 }$ . Adsorption of PP on the S1 surface (i.e. without SPS) is negligible.

# 3.8. Identification of TPs and tentative decomposition pathways

An extra experiment was performed with $5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ PP, $2 0 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ S1 and $2 . 2 { \ g } / \mathrm { L }$ SPS in an attempt to identify TPs during a 4-h long experiment. Three TPs of PP were successfully identified based on high resolution accurate mass measurements (UPLC-MS/MS-LTQ-Orbitrap) in negative ionization mode (Fig. 10 and Table 4). The parent compound presents a molecular ion peak $[ \mathbf { M } - \mathbf { H } ] ^ { - }$ at $m / z \ 1 7 9 . 0 7 1 1$ , a fragment at $m / z \ 1 3 6 . 0 1 6 9$ is assigned to the loss of the propyl group, while the fragments $m / z \ 2 1 0 . 0 2 2 4$ and 93.0352 correspond to the phenolic moiety. Three isomeric TPs (TP1, TP2 and TP3) with molecular ions $[ \mathbf { M } - \mathbf { H } ] ^ { - }$ at $m / z$ 195.0657–195.0661 that differ about 16 amu from PP $( m / z$ 179) have been detected; this indicates that monohydroxylation onto the phenolic ring or the propyl ester chain may occur. The profiles of the detected hydroxyl-TPs, as well as of PP are shown in Fig. 11. The reaction of sulfate radicals with electron-rich aromatic compounds takes place preferentially via an electron transfer from the aromatic moiety to the sulfate radical and the resulted radical cation reacts rapidly with water to form hydroxycyclohexadienyl radical, similarly to % OH attack onto aromatic rings. Thus, the phenolic moiety can be considered as the major potential site of hydroxylation (TP2, TP3). The hydroxylation in ortho-position (TP2, 2-hydroxypropyl paraben) was more probable taking into account that the phenolic hydroxyl group $\left( - \mathrm { O H } \right)$ preferentially activates the -ortho and -para positions, due to the electron-donating behavior and considering the steric hindrance effect on the para position. This attack is also consistent with the orientation effect of ester group (meta-directing). The formation of a hydroxylated product in meta position to hydroxyl group (TP2, 3-hydroxypropyl paraben) is less favorable.

As a result, the hydroxylation of the aromatic ring of PP mainly takes place in ortho position relative to phenolic OH group, i.e. the meta position relative to the ester group. This is in agreement with previous data obtained in other advanced oxidation processes [56,74]. For parabens with longer alkyl groups such as propyl or butyl parabens, the alkyl-H abstraction route is also a major pathway [56].

H-abstraction by sulfate and/or hydroxyl radicals can result in carbon-centered radical, which further reacts with dissolved oxygen in the aqueous solution to form a peroxy radical; this, in turn, forms primarily a tetroxide that is finally decomposed into hydroxyl and carbonyl groups on the alkyl chain through the Russell or Bennett reaction mechanism [75]. # 3.9. Leaching tests and stability of the steel-slag derived catalysts

Iron-containing catalysts may suffer partial dissolution which usually is favored at acidic conditions. This will eventually compromise catalyst stability, as well as breach legislation regarding the permissible limits of metal ions in water courses. For example, the upper limit of dissolved iron i) in surface waters intended for the abstraction of drinking water is $2 \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ (Directive 77/440/EEC), while ii) in irrigation water is $5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ (guidelines set by the US Environmental Protection Agency). In this view, leaching tests were performed to evaluate the stability of S1 catalyst at three pH values of 6, 4.5 and 3; this pH range was chosen to cover the initial and final solution pH values of the experiments performed in this work (see also Section 2.3). As seen in Table 5, the degree of leaching is practically negligible in the pH range 4.5–6 and very low at $\mathrm { p H } 3$ .

Additionally, the digestion procedure is a way to stabilise the slag due to the associated reduction in alkalinity and metal and heavy concentrations (Al and Mn). As a result, the synthesized catalysts are safer than the original slag, because in case of leaching $\left( \mathrm { p H } < 3 \right)$ only Fe (low toxicity) and Ca (not toxic) will be released into the water bodies.

# 4. Conclusions

The treatment of BOF slag using oxidative digestion in acid media is a suitable procedure for the synthesis of iron-containing materials that can be used as heterogeneous catalysts to activate SPS for the degradation of PP in water. In this sense, S1, S2 and S3 catalysts, with magnetite in its composition, effectively activated SPS, whereas the ones with goethite and/or hematite (S4 and S5) were not adequate for this purpose.

The oxidation kinetics of PP followed a pseudo-first-order reaction model $( \mathbf { r } ^ { 2 } > 0 . 9 6 )$ under all the experimental conditions studied. Approximately $9 0 \%$ removal of PP (initial concentration of $0 . 4 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ was achieved in $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at a concentration of ${ } ^ { 1 } { \ g } / \mathrm { L }$ of SPS and $5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ of S1 catalyst. The catalyst exhibited great stability throughout the degradation process, iron leaching being negligible $( 3 2 \mu \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ measured in the media at the completion of the reaction).

XPS measurements showed that the presence of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } { - \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } }$ on the catalyst surface was responsible for the generation of radicals. Both hydroxyl $( H O ^ { \bullet } )$ and sulfate $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } \cdot )$ radicals were indirectly identified using scavenging agents (tert-butanol and methanol).

The transformation products identified from the oxidation of PP were used for the proposal of a degradation pathway based on the monohydroxylation onto the phenolic ring or the propyl ester chain of PP.
