# Nitrogen and sulfur dual doped porous carbon as metal‑free catalyst for oxidative degradation of 4‑nitrophenol by persulfate activation

Yasamin Bide\* & Niloofar Naseri Jahromi

The replacement of metals in catalytic processes is highly demanded to improve sustainability and economic growth. Poor stability and metal leaching are the main drawbacks of metal-based catalytic reactions. This work represented the use of nitrogen and sulfur-co-doped mesoporous carbon material ((N, S)-MPC) as a metal-free catalyst for the degradation of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) as a priority pollutant announced by the Environmental Protection Agency through the persulfate based advanced oxidation process. A low amount of (N, S)-MPC catalyst $( 0 . 3 \mathsf { g } / \mathsf { L } )$ exhibited superior performance for the degradation of 4-NP within $3 h$ at room temperature and unadjusted pH. The COD removal was calculated to be $76 \%$ using (N, S)-MPC catalyst. Interestingly, the degradations kinetics of 4-NP followed the zero-order kinetics with the rate constant of $\pmb { 0 . 5 0 5 \mathrm { \ m i n ^ { - 1 } } }$ . The radical quenching experiment was accomplished to investigate the activation pathway of degradation. A real sample from an oil and gas company was treated with the (N, S)-MPC catalyst, which showed excellent total decontamination of $6 1 \%$ . The recyclability and stability of the catalyst have been evaluated for three runs. Owing to the obvious benefts such as high efciency, metal-free nature, and recyclability, the presented catalyst can improve pollutant removal from aqueous media and practical environmental remediation.

4-nitrophenol (PNP) is a toxic and persistent contaminant of industrial efuents, soil, and groundwater that is widely used in the synthesis of pesticides, herbicides, pharmaceuticals, and dyes1,2 . It has been considered the priority pollutant in several countries resulting in potential risk to humans and the ecosystem even at low concentrations3,4 . Tus, it is highly demanded to develop an efcient, cost-efective, and rapid technique to improve PNP degradation in aqueous environments. An advanced oxidation process (AOP) is considered a feasible approach to PNP removal5–7 . Compared to the traditional AOP based on hydrogen peroxide, persulfate represents higher selectivity, lower reaction requirements, and milder conditions8 .

Persulfate-based AOP establishes a signifcant development in wastewater treatment and environmental remediation9 . Owing to the low oxidative potential, the persulfate alone shows a relatively slow reaction with pollutants10. Terefore, various activators such as thermal, ultrasound, radiation, alkaline, and metal activators have been employed to produce reactive radicals and achieve efcient pollutant removal11. Te high energy consumption, high pH dependency, disposal of precipitates, metal agglomeration and leaching, resource scarcity, and toxicity of some metals are some drawbacks of common AOP activators. To overcome these problems, particularly secondary contamination, metal-free catalysts have recently emerged as one of the fastest-growing research felds12,13. In 2015, Duan et al.14 investigated the various dimension of carbon materials as metal-free catalysts for persulfate activation. According to their results, porous carbon material showed high activity due to the defects and pore structure. On the other hand, the introduction of heteroatoms is an operative approach to improving the performance of metal-free carbonaceous materials for persulfate activation15,16. Very recently, Liu et al. applied sulfur-doped ordered mesoporous carbons for persulfate-based PNP removal which showed improved activity due to the introduction of more strain and defects17. On the other hand, nitrogen doping can afect the charge distribution of neighboring carbon atoms, inducing Lewis acid character to graphitic carbons, structural defects, bond disorders, and lattice distortion in a conjugated carbon network18. Te nitrogen and sulfur co-doping exhibits a synergistic efect and enhanced electronic properties resulting in improved catalytic activity in various processes19,20. Terefore, in this work, nitrogen and sulfur doped mesoporous carbon material has been utilized as metal-free catalyst for persulfate-based AOP. Because of the synergistic efect of dual doping and the 3D mesoporous structure, (N, S)-MPC was expected to represent high efciency.

# Experimental

Materials. Sodium hypochlorite solution $5 \%$ active chlorine) (NaOCl) was purchased from Sehat Industrial and Commercial Co. Acetic acid (glacial) $1 0 0 \%$ , 2-aminothiazole (2-AT), sodium peroxydisulfate, 4-nitrophenol (4-NP), sodium hydroxide, and potassium hydroxide were prepared from Merck Co. and used as received. Te rest of the materials and solvents were obtained from Merck Co.

Instruments and characterization. To investigate the functional groups of the samples, FTIR spectroscopy was accomplished using a Philips PU 9600 Serie. Te reduction of 4-NP and disappearance of the yellow color were monitored by UV–Vis spectroscopy (SP8-400 UV/Vis Spectrophotometer). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was performed by a feld emission microscope (Zeiss SIGMA VP, Oxford Instrument, $2 0 \mathrm { k V }$ ) equipped with energy dispersive $\mathrm { X }$ -ray spectroscopy (EDX) and a mapping detector (Oxford Instruments). To designate the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and pore size distribution, $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption–desorption isotherms were obtained using a BElSORP Mini apparatus (Microtrac Bel Corp). Te samples were degassed in a vacuum at $2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ before $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption at $- 1 9 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Raman pattern of (N, S)-MPC was provided using a Raman microscope (XploRA Plus, HORIBA) with a $\mathrm { H e } / \mathrm { N e }$ Laser excitation at $5 3 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . Te chemical composition of the N, S-MPC was determined by using a CHNS elemental analyzer (LECO CHNS-932). Te 4-NP concentration was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, USA) with an ODZ-3 MZ column and UV detector. Te mobile phase included acetonitrile $( 6 0 \% )$ and water $( 4 0 \% )$ . Te column temperature in HPLC analysis was $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Te injection volume of the sample was $2 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { l }$ and the fow rate was $0 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m l / m i n }$ . Te GC analysis was performed on the real sample before and afer $2 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ of AOP reaction using a PHILIPS gas chromatography device (PU4500 model) equipped with an FID detector. Te samples were extracted three times with dichloromethane, dried with magnesium sulfate, and concentrate to a determined amount. Before the injection into the GC, the samples were fltered with a $0 . 4 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ flter. Te $2 \mu \mathrm { L }$ of the sample was injected into the GC. Te initial column temperature was adjusted at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ followed by increasing to $2 4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at a rate of $5 ~ { ^ \circ } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { m i n }$ , and kept for $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Te inlet temperature and detector temperature were set at 300 and $3 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ respectively. Te sample was injected into the GC column in the split-mode at a split ratio of 1:4.

Synthesis of nitrogen and sulfur‑co‑doped mesoporous carbon material. To prepare N and S-codoped mesoporous carbon material, the hydrothermal carbonization of poly(2-aminothiazole) (P(2-AT)) was conducted by a procedure described previously21, with some modifcations. Te polymerization of 2AT was simply performed using sodium hypochlorite as the oxidant and acetic acid as the dopant. Te $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ aqueous solution of $0 . 2 6 ~ \mathrm { M }$ 2AT was mixed with $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of $0 . 2 6 ~ \mathrm { M }$ acetic acid solution. Te solution was rapidly mixed with $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of $0 . 1 3 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ sodium hypochlorite solution and then kept at $5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ without shaking. Before mixing, two aqueous solutions should be reached at $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Afer $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ , the brown powder was collected, centrifuged, and washed with 0.1 M KOH aqueous solution and DI water to remove unreacted monomers and oligomers and the excess reactants. Ten, a specifed concentration of P(2-AT) aqueous solution was prepared and moved to a Tefon-lined stainless-steel autoclave for hydrothermal carbonization at $1 8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 2 \mathrm { h }$ . Te dark brown powder was washed with DI water and dried in an oven at $4 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 2 \mathrm { h }$ .

Persulfate‑based advanced oxidation process of 4‑nitrophenol using (N, S)‑MPC. Te persulfate-based AOP of 4-NP was examined using as-prepared (N, S)-MPC as the activating agent of sodium peroxydisulfate. In a typical procedure, $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of $1 5 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ aqueous solution of 4-NP, $0 . 0 1 5 { \mathrm { ~ g ~ } }$ sodium peroxydisulfate, and $0 . 0 0 3 { \mathrm { g } }$ (N, S)-MPC were mixed in a round-bottom fask equipped with a magnetic stirrer. Te mixture was centrifuged for $3 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ to disperse the catalyst in the solution and then allowed to react for $^ { 3 \mathrm { { h } } }$ at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at unadjusted $\mathrm { p H }$ $\mathrm { ( p H } = 6 )$ . Afer the completion of the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged to isolate the catalyst. Te catalyst was washed with DI water and then dried for recyclability tests. Te amount of $5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { l }$ of $0 . 1 \mathrm { ~ M ~ N a O H }$ solution was added to $4 \mathrm { m L }$ of the remaining solution and then employed for HPLC or UV–Vis measurements. Various tests were conducted to investigate the efect of diferent parameters such as temperature, catalyst content, and sodium peroxydisulfate content.

# Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of metal‑free catalyst. Te P(2-AT) has been synthesized by the chemical oxidation method using various oxidants and dopants as well as diferent procedures22,23. In this work, our attempt to synthesize P2AT with copper chloride and ferric chloride as the oxidant led to metal trapped in the polymer structure. Owing to the nitrogen and sulfur heteroatoms in the polymer structure, the complete removal of these metals seems impossible. As Cifci et al. reported the existence of the Fe element in the polymer matrix is based on the elemental analysis afer the polymerization of $2 \mathrm { A T } ^ { 2 4 }$ . To ensure the metal-free nature of the as-prepared catalyst, among the various synthetic procedures, we chose the rapid mixing polymerization of 2AT using acetic acid as the dopant and sodium hypochlorite as the oxidant described previously with some modifcations25. To enhance the polymerization yield, the reaction time was extended to $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ , but to obtain the desired morphology, the polymerization was carried out at $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ .

To study the chemical structure and functional groups of as-synthesized P(2-AT) and (N, S)-MPC, FT-IR analysis has been carried out. FT-IR spectra of P(2-AT) were given in Fig. 1A. Te peak at 1620 and $1 5 1 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ were related to the stretching vibrations of $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { N }$ and ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } { \mathrm { C } }$ in the polymer chain, respectively. Te band at $3 1 1 5 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ was attributed to the stretching vibration of secondary $\mathrm { - N H - }$ groups demonstrating the formation of secondary –NH– linkages in P(2-AT) which is in good agreement with the literature24. Te bands at 611 and $1 2 2 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ corresponded to ${ \mathrm { C } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { S } }$ and $\mathrm { C - N }$ stretching vibrations, respectively. In the FT-IR spectrum of (N, S)- MPC (Fig. 1A), the peaks at 3115, 1620, and $1 5 1 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ showed an increase in their signals. Moreover, the C-N peak was shifed from 1220 to $1 2 3 6 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , probably due to the aromatization afer hydrothermal treatment of P(2-AT).

Te nitrogen and sulfur contents of N, S-MPC were determined by CHNS analysis as 9.4 and $2 3 . 6 6 \%$ , respectively. To investigate the structural and electronic properties of (N, S)-MPC, Raman spectroscopy analysis was carried out. As observed in Fig. 1B, the sample showed a G band at $1 5 2 5 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and a D band at $1 3 8 0  c \mathrm { m } ^ { - \mathrm { i } }$ related to the graphitic carbons26, as well as a peak at $2 9 4 0 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ ascribed to the 2D band. Te G band is ascribed to the graphitic carbon with $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ confguration and the D band indicates the defects in the carbon structure27. Te heteroatom doping considerably enhances the defect density, which causes the strong D band and high intensity between the D and G bands (valley region). Ayiania et al. suggested that nitrogen functional groups signifcantly afect the vibrational modes in the valley region28. Terefore, the existence of the valley region also confrmed the heteroatom doping in the as-obtained porous carbon material.

Figure 2 exhibited the UV–vis spectra of P2AT and (N, S)-MPC. Both of the spectra showed the peak at $2 4 8 \mathrm { n m }$ ascribed to the $\pi \to \pi ^ { * }$ transition of the conjugated $\pi$ -system29. Te lower absorbance afer hydrothermal carbonization can be due to variations in the surface chemistry of the two types of nanoparticles.

Te FESEM images explicitly designate that P(2-AT) possesses relatively uniform nanosphere morphology with an average particle size of $< 1 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. 3A and B). Te hydrothermal treatment of as-synthesized P(2-AT) led to a porous material, as clearly seen in the SEM images of (N, S)-MPC. Te EDX mapping analysis revealed the presence of carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen in the (N, S)-MPC (Fig. 4). Te matched spatial distributions of S, N, and C indicate the uniform distribution of sulfur and nitrogen within the carbon matrix.

Te architecture of the porous structure of as-synthesized (N, S)-MPC was investigated by nitrogen adsorption and desorption measurements. Te isotherms curve of (N, S)-MPC and BET plot (inset) are presented in Fig. 5A. Te (N, S)-MPC indicated Type IV isotherm based on the IUPAC classifcation of isotherms which is related to the mesoporous materials30. Various mesoporous industrial adsorbents showed this isotherm, of which the initial monolayer-multilayer adsorption on the mesopore walls and pore condensation. Te pore morphology can be identifed by the type of hysteresis loop. Te (N, S)-MPC showed an H1 hysteresis loop in which the vertical and parallel branches over a wide range of gas uptake are observed and abruptly increased at high pressures. Te H1 hysteresis can be attributed to porous materials, including a narrow range of mesopores where the neck of the pores is only slightly narrowed31. Tis result is in good agreement with the SEM images (Fig. 3C and D), where the pores morphology can be detected. Te pore size distribution and BJH cumulative pore volume (inset) have been given in Fig. 5B. Te pore size distribution curve of (N, S)-MPC establishes that the pore size is distributed between 3.2 and $8 5 . 6 \mathrm { n m }$ and mostly between 3.2 and $1 9 . 4 \mathrm { n m }$ . Te BET surface area equals $7 3 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , the total pore volume equals to $0 . 5 0 \mathsf { c m } ^ { 3 } \mathsf { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , and the average pore diameter equals $1 0 . 9 \mathrm { n m }$ .

Catalytic activity for persulfate‑based AOP. To evaluate the possible activity of as-synthesized P(2- AT) for 4-NP removal either with absorption or AOP mechanisms, various experiments have been accomplished and 4-NP removal has been monitored by UV–vis analysis. Te UV–vis spectra of 4-NP and P(2-AT) have been given in Fig. 6A and B, respectively, as the control spectra. Te AOP of 4-NP in the absence of catalyst was examined, indicating no conversion yield afer $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ (Fig. 6C). Moreover, the absorption of 4-NP by P(2-AT) was investigated, which showed $6 \%$ of 4-NP absorption afer $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ (Fig. 6D). Te catalytic activity of P(2-AT) for persulfatebased AOP of 4-NP was tested that exhibited a low conversion yield $( 1 0 \% )$ with $0 . 0 1 \dot { 5 } \ : \mathrm { g }$ sodium peroxydisulfate (Fig. 6E). Based on the results, P(2-AT) did not show efcient absorption or AOP-based removal of 4-NP.

Te catalytic activity of (N, S)-MPC as the metal-free catalyst for persulfate-based AOP of 4-NP was optimized using various amounts of catalyst and sodium peroxydisulfate. Te results were summarized in Table 1. Increasing the catalyst amount from 0.001 to 0.003 considerably afected the efciency of 4-NP removal. But, more increments from 0.003 to 0.006 led to a decrease in catalytic activity with a low slope. Since the best results have been obtained by 0.002 and $0 . 0 0 3 { \mathrm { g } }$ catalyst, the sodium peroxydisulfate content has been optimized using these amounts. For $_ { 0 . 0 0 2 \mathrm { ~ g ~ } }$ catalyst, employing a $_ { 0 . 0 2 \mathrm { ~ g ~ } }$ sodium peroxydisulfate gave a $9 4 \%$ conversion yield (Table 1, entry 4). In the case of a $0 . 0 0 3 \mathrm { g }$ catalyst, the reaction proceeded to $9 8 \%$ using $_ { 0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { g } }$ sodium peroxydisulfate (Table 1, entry 7).

In addition, the efect of reaction temperature on the removal efciency of the as-prepared catalyst was studied. Chemical oxygen demand (COD) specifes the degree of organic contamination, which is an important indicator of water treatment32. Te efciency of (N, S)-MPC catalyst for COD removal was also determined. Te initial COD of $1 5 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ 4-NP was measured to be $3 7 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ . Due to the interference of persulfate for COD measurements and the overestimation, the corrected values were determined33. Te COD of the reaction mixture without 4-NP $_ { \mathrm { 0 . 0 0 3 \mathrm { g } } }$ catalyst, $_ { 0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { g } }$ sodium peroxydisulfate, $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ water) as the blank sample was obtained to be $1 2 2 \mathrm { m g / L }$ . On the other hand, the COD of the reaction mixture containing 10 mL $1 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ 4-NP, $0 . 0 0 3 \ \mathrm { g }$ catalyst, and $_ { 0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { \ g } }$ sodium peroxydisulfate afer $^ { 3 \mathrm { { h } } }$ was determined as $1 3 1 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ . So, the corrected value of COD to eliminate the interference of persulfate was calculated as $1 2 \mathrm { m g / L }$ . Comparing the initial COD and the corrected COD of the reaction mixture afer $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ , the COD removal equals $7 6 \%$ .

Several previously metal-free carbon-based materials for persulfate activation have been compared with this work in terms of synthetic procedure, catalyst amount, persulfate concentration, and removal efciency. As given in Table 2, most of the reported heteroatom-doped carbon materials used as the metal-free carbon-based material for persulfate activation have been synthesized through heating at high temperatures under an inert atmosphere. Using a straightforward synthetic procedure to produce N, S-doped mesoporous carbon material for efcient pollutant removal is an outstanding advantage of this work.

Mechanistic study. To investigate whether the absorption mechanism is efective in the experiments, a test without persulfate was also accomplished, which indicated less than $1 0 \%$ 4-NP removal afer $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ . Terefore, two possible mechanisms have been considered, including the radical pathway and the non-radical pathway. Te hydroxyl radicals are the main active species in the traditional AOP, while sulfate and hydroxyl radicals as well as singlet oxygen as nonradical species, can be dominant in persulfate-based $\mathrm { A O P ^ { 4 4 } }$ . To determine the reactive oxidative species, three experiments were done using t-butanol, methanol, and benzoquinone as radical scavengers. Methanol efciently quenches both $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ and · OH , while t-butanol can efciently scavenge · OH $( 3 . 8 \mathrm { - } 7 . 6 \times \mathrm { i } 0 ^ { 8 } )$ . Te persulfate-based AOP of 4-NP accomplished in the presence of $2 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ methanol showed a conversion yield of $6 . 6 \%$ afer 1, 2, and $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ reaction time. In the case of t-butanol, the conversion yield of $6 . 9 \%$ was obtained afer $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ , which remained constant. Very low conversions of 4-NP in the presence of two radical scavengers established the possibility of a radical mechanism. Te comparable inhibition by the two scavengers indicated that · OH may be the main radical responsible for the 4-NP degradation in our system. Moreover, the benzoquinone was used as superoxide radicals $( \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \cdot \cdot } )$ scavenger, which is diminishes the 4-NP removal to $8 9 \%$ afer $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ , suggesting that $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \cdot \mathrm { ~ - ~ } }$ is slightly involved in activation.

Several factors can be afected the efcient degradation of 4-NP using (N, S)-MPC as the persulfate activator (Fig. 7): (i) Te nitrogen atoms with lone-pair electrons in the (N, S)-MPC can act as Lewis basic sites and induce the enhancement in the redox process; (ii) Te higher electronegativity of nitrogen atoms causes the change of electronic structure of adjacent carbon atoms and enhancement of the electron transfer and consequently enabling sites for the surface reactions; (iii) Sulfur atoms with similar electronegativity to carbon but larger atomic size led to structural defects of the carbon network and consequently more active sites45; (iv) Sulfur atoms owing the lone pair can render electrons in the persulfate activation; (v) the carbon structure can act as electron bridge to simplify the electron-transfer; (vi) the carbon network can interact and activate the benzene ring of organic pollutants via $\pi - \pi$ interaction which is benefcial for ring-opening process and synergistically enhance the oxidation by the reactive radicals.

Real sample application. Te catalytic activity of the as-synthesized metal-free catalyst has been examined for the AOP process and decontamination of real sample to indicate the applicability of the catalyst for complex mixtures. Te produced water from an oil and gas company has been employed as the real sample whose GC analysis was given in Fig. 9A. Te amount of $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ real sample undergoes the AOP process using $\phantom { - } 0 . 0 5 4 \ \mathrm { g }$ (N, S)-MPC catalyst and $_ { 0 . 2 7 \mathrm { g } }$ sodium peroxydisulfate at $2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . Te GC analysis afer the reaction time has been provided in Fig. 9B. As observed in Fig. 9, some peaks of the organic matter have a certain degree of decline, and some peaks even disappear. Te total decontamination of $6 1 \%$ was calculated according to the sum of peak integrals in the whole region. We also divided the GC spectrum to three regions containing the retention time of $4 { - } 1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , $1 5 { - } 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ , and $2 5 { - } 4 2 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ , which the removal was determined to be 96, 56, and $2 7 \%$ , respectively. Interestingly, a high yield of decontamination was obtained for the compounds to pass through the column in the frst $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , which are probably lower molecular weights. As the retention time increases, the removal efciency was decreased, which can be related to the larger molecular weight and lower efciency of the catalyst for these compounds.

Recyclability is one of the advantages and features of heterogeneous catalysts. To evaluate the recyclability of (N, S)-MPC, the experiment was performed for three runs. Afer each run, the catalyst was removed, washed with DI water, and dried to use for the subsequent run. Te recyclability tests of (N, S)-MPC were exhibited in Fig. 10A. Te as-prepared (N, S)-MPC showed $9 8 \%$ removal afer $1 8 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . In the 2nd and 3rd run, (N, S)-MPC provided $9 3 \%$ and $7 9 \%$ removal, respectively.

FT-IR analysis was performed for (N, S)-MPC afer three runs to study the possible infuence of the oxidation reactions on the functionality of as-prepared (N, S)-MPC (Fig. 10B). As observed, the bands remained unchanged which indicated the stability of the catalyst afer three runs and its heterogeneity nature. For the next runs, the catalytic efciency dramatically decreased probably because of the coverage of adsorbed intermediates and the strong oxidation process which have also been observed in previously reported works on the metal-free catalysts for AOP reaction14,46. Te reactivation process, including thermal treatment and ultrasonication, can be done to remove the adsorbed intermediates and retain the catalytic activity.

# Conclusion

Poor stability and metal leaching are the main drawbacks of metal-based catalytic reactions. In this work, we introduced nitrogen and sulfur co-doped mesoporous carbon material as a highly efcient metal-free catalyst for persulfate-based AOP. Te degradation of 4-NP was accomplished as a model reaction to explore the catalytic activity, efciency, and recyclability. Te COD removal was calculated to be $7 6 \%$ using (N, S)-MPC catalyst. Interestingly, the degradations kinetics of 4-NP followed the zero-order kinetics with the rate constant of $0 . { \dot { 5 } } 0 5 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Te radical quenching experiment was accomplished to investigate the activation pathway of degradation. Various factors might be responsible for the high activity of (N, S)-MPC, including the porous carbon structure indicating the carbon bridge and electron transfer as well as interaction with the benzene ring, the presence of heteroatoms enhancing the electron transfer, and structural defect. Among the metal-free carbon-based materials for persulfate activation, the straightforward and low-cost synthetic procedure, and low catalyst amount make (N, S)-MPC an excellent choice for practical applications. In addition, the presented catalyst opened a promising research feld for biomedical applications in which the absence of metals is highly demanded due to the lack of toxicity and higher biocompatibility including theragnostic nanomedicine.
