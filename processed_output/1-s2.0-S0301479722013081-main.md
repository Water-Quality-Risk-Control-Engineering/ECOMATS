# . Introduction

Pharmaceuticals, both in their unchanged forms or as metabolites or conjugates, can enter aquatic environments both through human and animal excretions, as well as through the disposal of unused drugs or released from manufacturing facilities. They often end up in the sewer system, which remains the major route of entry into the municipal sewage systems. These bioactive compounds are mainly removed from the aquatic environment through microbial degradation, sorption to organic matter, hydrolysis and photolysis (Ying et al., 2013). Therefore, the photolysis studies allow us to determine the presence and persistence of pharmaceuticals in the environment, as well as to understand the fate of these contaminants. These data together with the determination of their toxicity is an important health and environmental issue. On the other hand, UVVis range of the electromagnetic radiation is often used in the systems designed for water decontamination. Due to frequently insufficient effectiveness of photolytic degradation, Uv-Vis irradiation treatment is usually enhanced in various ways. Naturally occurring dissolved organic matter (DOM) possesses ability to absorb sunlight and act as a photosensitizer, accelerating decomposition of the pollutants, such as residuals of pharmaceuticals. Nevertheless, even such indirect photolytic degradation is rarely sufficiently effective. Therefore, other methods, relying mainly on formation of the reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as peroxide-mediated degradation or homogeneous and heterogeneous photocatalysis, are extensively investigated (Trawiski and Skibiski, 2017). The latter method is based on the light-induced creation of the electron-hole pairs on the catalyst particle surface. Such pair is able to interact not only with contaminants, but also with surrounding water molecules, resulting in formation of highly active ROS (Di Paola et al., 2012). In recent years a great variety of novel materials for the heterogeneous catalysis process were developed in order to obtain e.g. extended radiation absorption, higher surface area or other properties allowing to increase the photocatalytic activity. Such materials include previously unreported catalysts conjugations, various dopped metal oxides or catalysts deposited on different natural and synthetic polymers. Higher efficiency can be also achieved by optimization of the photocatalysts synthesis processes, as well as by application of unconventional radiation sources, such as LEDs (Baaloudj et al., 2021; Zeghioud et al., 2018, 2019). The peroxide-mediated methods rely on activity of ROS formed in photo-induced decomposition of species such as hydrogen peroxide, peroxymonosulfate (PMS) or peroxydisulfate (PDS). Interest in application of two latter peroxides increases rapidly in recent years, mainly because of the combined formation of the hydroxyl and long-living sulfate radicals, which can synergistically take part in the degradation processes (Giannakis et al., 2021). Interestingly, coupling heterogeneous photocatalysis with PMS and PDS significantly increases formation rate of the sulfate radicals, notably affecting the decontamination processes (Wang et al., 2021).

According to the studies, more than 2 million people is infected annually by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (Gary and Weiner, 2020). In 2019 approximately 38 million people worldwide lived with this infection, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa (Ochodo et al., 2022). The most common and pathogenic subtype of the virus  HIV-1 enters target cells firstly by binding the envelope glycoprotein $\mathtt { g p 1 2 0 }$ to CD4 receptors and then to co-receptors, especially CC chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5), C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), or both, and undergo conformational changes within the gp41 subunit that lead to the fusion of the two membranes (Esté and Telenti, 2007; Wilen et al., 2012). CCR5-tropic viruses predominate at the beginning of infection, whereas CxCR4-tropic viruses are present in significant proportions in the later stages of the disease (Philpott, 2003; Westby et al., 2006; Wilen et al., 2012). Maraviroc (4,4-difluoro-N-[(1 S)-3-[(1R, 5S)-3-(3-methyl-5-propan-2-yl-1,2,4-triazol-4-y1)-8-azabicyclo[3.2.1] octan-8-yl]-1-phenylpropyl]cyclohexane-1-carboxamide) secured approval from the FDA in 2007 for the treatment of HIV infection (Wasmuth et al., 2012). The drug binds in the transmembrane pocket of CCR5 on the surface of $\mathrm { C D } 4 ^ { + }$ cells and macrophages. By preventing the interaction between the V3 loop of HIV envelope gp120 and the CCR5 co-receptor it inhibits viral entry. CxCR4-tropic viruses exist in an appreciable proportion in chronic infections and because maraviroc is only effective against CCR5-tropic viruses, its usage as a salvage therapy may decrease simply because of increased resistance by pre-selection of CXCR4 strains (Connor et al., 1997; Westby et al., 2007). A maraviroc biotransformation study in male subjects showed that the absolute bioavailability of $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m g } }$ of orally administered maraviroc was ${ } ^ { 2 3 \% }$ . The unchanged form was the major circulating component in plasma and the main form excreted from the body (Abel et al., 2008).

Despite being a relatively new drug, maraviroc was identified as an environmental contaminant. Abafe et al. reported presence of antiretroviral drugs, including maraviroc, in influents from three wastewater treatment plants in South Africa at concentrations ranging from 82 to $3 2 0 ~ \mathrm { { n g ~ L } } ^ { - 1 }$ . Very low concentrations (below the limit-of-detection) in effluents from two WWTPs indicate high efficiency of maraviroc elimination. On the other hand, treatment in the third WWTP resulted in only twofold decrease of the drug initial concentration (Abafe et al., 2018). Taking into account high prevalence of HIV infections in the sub-Saharan Africa as well as commonness of the antiretroviral therapy in this region, presence of maraviroc in the aquatic environments should be expected also in the future years. Moreover this drug is always used in the combined therapy with other anti-HIV pharmaceuticals, which may additionally increase risks of its release to the aquatic environments (Nannou et al., 2020).

To the best of our knowledge no papers tracing the environmental fate of maraviroc have been published so far. Also, there are still no comprehensive studies on maraviroc degradation processes. However, a few studies reported that the drug is sensitive to specific agents, such as HCl and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ (Kalyana Chakravarthy and Gowri Sankar, 2012; Chilukuri et al., 2014; Geetha et al., 2013). Although in those studies maraviroc was subjected to UVVis irradiation, no significant changes in its stability were observed, neither were any structures of the photodegradation products presented.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the phototransformation of maraviroc under simulated Uv-Vis light, using both ultrapure and natural river water as a matrix. The performance of photocatalytic oxidation using semiconductive photocatalyst $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ anatase was also examined. Due to unusually high photostability of the studied drug, enhancement of the heterogeneous photocatalysis by addition of PMS was also studied. The quantitative and qualitative analysis of the products formed during the photolytic and photocatalytic processes was carried out using RP-UHPLC-DAD coupled with ESI-Q-TOF mass spectrometer.

# 2.2. Sample preparation

Working solutions were prepared by diluting the acetonitrile stock solution $( 2 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 } )$ in ultrapure water (UW) and RW to obtain a $1 0 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g }$ $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ concentration of maraviroc (initial, pre-irradiation concentration). The suspensions containing working solutions and catalysts were prepared by weighing $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and/or PMS $\mathrm { 1 0 ~ m M }$ concentration of OxONE as a whole, corresponding to $4 . 9 5 ~ \mathrm { \ m M }$ final concentration of potassium peroxymonosulfate), and subsequently mixing them with the working solutions.

# 2.3. Photolytic and photocatalytic experiments

Phototransformation experiments were performed in quartz-capped cells $\left( 3 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m L } \right)$ with the use of a xenon lamp and D65 filter (consistent with the ISO 10977:1993 standard), simulating the full solar UVVis spectrum $( 2 9 0 - 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ set to $7 0 0 \mathrm { W } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ of total irradiation (Suntest $\mathrm { C P S + , }$ Atlas Linsengericht, Germany). The temperature in the chamber was kept below $3 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ The photocatalytic samples (heterogeneous mixtures) were stirred at $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ throughout the experiment with the use of a microstirrer (MINI Stirrer, Cimarel: Telemodul, Thermo Electron LED GmbH, Germany) and a PTFE-covered bar $\mathrm { ( l = 6 } \mathrm { m m }$ . In the case of direct and indirect photolysis experiments, $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ aliquots were collected after 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 (UW) or 1, 2, 4 and 6 (RW) hours of irradiation. In the case of photocatalytic experiments, samples were collected after 5, 15, 30 and 60 $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } )$ or 1, 2, 3 and 4 (PMS and $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ PMS) min of irradiation. All of the samples containing the suspended catalyst were centrifuged at $1 5 0 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ for 5 min before being analyzed.

# 2.4.Qualitative and quantitative analytical procedure

UHPLC-ESI-QTOF analysis was performed with the use of Agilent high resolution Q-TOF system series 6520 and UHPLC system series 1290 and Zorbax Eclipse-C18 HD $( 2 . 1 \times 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ , $\mathrm { d p } = 1 . 8 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ column (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). In order to subject the studied processes to both qualitative and quantitative analyses, the MS detector was tuned in a positive mode in extended dynamic range $( 2 ~ \mathrm { G H z } )$ . To ensure accurate mass measurements, a reference mass correction was used and masses 121.050873 and 922.009798 were used as lock masses. Quantification of maraviroc was performed and validated with the use of extracted ion current chromatogram (EIC) of the doublycharged ion (m/z 257.6712, Table S2).

# 3.Results and discussion

# 3.1.Quantitative study of the phototransformation process

In the present study a Suntest $\mathrm { C P S + }$ chamber, equipped with a xenon lamp, was chosen as a phototransformation apparatus. Application of Xe burner-generated light is widely considered as environmentallyrelevant, since such source accurately imitates the UV-Vis sunlight spectrum on the ground level (Trawiski and Skibiáski, 2017). Based on preliminary studies, it was determined that convenient conditions for photodegradation in the Suntest chamber would be to set $7 0 0 \mathrm { W } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ of total irradiation in the $2 9 0 { - } 8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ range, which corresponds to an energy dose of 2520 kJ m−2 h−1.

In order to assess the influence of interaction between the catalyst and maraviroc on its removal process, the adsorption studies were performed. As shown in Fig. 1, addition of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ (at concentration of 100 $\mathbf { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } } )$ to the ultrapure water solution of maraviroc resulted in its immediate adsorption on the catalyst surface, decreasing concentration of the studied drug to about $9 5 \%$ in comparison to the initial value. Then equilibration of the adsorption-desorption process was observed. In the case of river water solution, adsorption was more pronounced, and approximately ${ \bf 1 0 \% }$ decrease of maraviroc concentration was observed. Such phenomenon may be explained by lower pH of RW than UW (6.76 vs 7.0), which promotes adsorption of basic organic compounds of the metal oxides surface (Kim et al., 2020). After $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of experiment progression of adsorption process led to further maraviroc concentration decline to about $8 5 \%$ of the initial value. However after $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , concentration returned to around $9 0 \%$ , suggesting that the adsorption-desorption equilibrium was achieved.

# 3.1.1. Ultrapure and river water phototransformation

Maraviroc phototransformation studies using the ultrapure water (UW) have shown that maraviroc is a distinctly photostable molecule and its photodecomposition half-life appeared to be $2 5 8 { \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ (Table 1). This suggest that this drug may have a high potential to accumulate in the environment and therefore more extensive studies on its fate are necessary. As it was mentioned, DOM present in the natural water matrices usually alters the organic pollutants degradation rates. On the one hand, it possess an ability to be excited by solar radiation, then transfer the absorbed energy, acting as a photosensitizer, and accelerate the decomposition of the co-existing organic pollutants. However, DOM may also act as a degradation retardant via quenching the ROS or scavenging the radiation (Lin and Reinhard, 2005; Trawiski and Skibiáski, 2017). Therefore, photostability of this drug was tested also with river water (RW) matrix to mimic the effect of the natural environment on drug stability. As expected, in the presence of the RW matrix, maraviroc degradation accelerated significantly and its half-life decreased to $8 . 4 9 \mathrm { h }$ . Despite the significant improvement, the degradation time still remains far too long to be considered as an effective method to eliminate the drug from the environment and therefore we have also investigated the potential of heterogeneous photocatalyst applications in performing efficient maraviroc degradation.

# 3.1.2. Photocatalytic transformation

In this study, we tested the effectiveness of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ ,widely applied semiconductive oxide. Despite its several drawbacks, such as lack of visible light absorption, $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ often serves as a model photocatalyst (Di Paola et al., 2012). As it was expected, application of the heterogeneous photocatalysis substantially accelerated the maraviroc degradation process (Fig. 2), and its half-life decreased over 600 times. Substitution of UW with RW further increased the decomposition rate, reducing half-life to about $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ (Table 1). Additionally, it should be noted that although the adsorption process visibly alters maraviroc concentration in the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ -containing systems, its contribution to the drug removal is rather minor in comparison to the photocatalytic degradation.

Noteworthy, even under the photocatalytic conditions, maraviroc is exceptionally resistant to the UVVis-driven degradation  much more than other photostable pharmaceutical contaminants, such as clozapine or fentanyl (Trawiáski et al., 2021; Trawiáski and Skibiáski, 2019). Therefore, enhancement of the photocatalytic process by coupling it with peroxymonosulfate (PMS) was tested. Although PMS possesses high oxidizing potential, it usually grants low degradation rates when used alone (Wang and Wang, 2018). This problem may be solved by application of various activation methods, which induce transformation of PMS into reactive species, mainly sulfate $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , SR) and hydroxyl radicals (OH'•, HR), however it largely depends on the specific activation method and reaction medium. The main advantage of SR is longer lifetime and lower reaction rate with DOM, when compared with HR (Giannakis et al., 2021). Activation of PMS by UV irradiation relies on fission of the OO bond and subsequent formation of SR and HR (Wang and Wang, 2018). High activity of the UV-Vis-PMS system was confirmed also in this study, reducing maraviroc half-life to less than 7 min. Further coupling of UVVis-PMS with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ photocatalysis resulted in rapid decomposition of the studied drug. Recorded half-life was estimated to $0 . 4 7 \mathrm { m i n }$ , which corresponds to over $6 7 ~ 0 0 0$ faster degradation rate than in the case of direct photolysis. After $4 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ of irradiation maraviroc was almost completely decomposed. Such result can be explained by additional PMS activation by the electrons promoted to the conduction band of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ by UV radiation (Chen et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2021). Furthermore, in order to examine how the presence of the natural water matrix influences the PMS-mediated degradation of maraviroc, both experiments were replicated using RW. Consistently with the previous studies, also in the case of maraviroc water matrix slowed down the degradation, increasing its half-life over twofold to $1 6 . 9 3 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ . Even more pronounced suppression effect was observed in the case of UV-Vis-PMS- $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ system, and the half-life increased to $1 . 8 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ .Such inhibiting properties may be explained by various interactions between excited and non-excited DOM, SR, as well as other ROS. Firstly, non-excited DOM particles possess ability to quench the $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , which is the most important species in the studied process. Moreover, dissolved matter can also reduce the oxidized intermediates, which also contributes to decrease the degradation rate. On the other hand, the excited DOM may take part in the oxidative decomposition of the organic pollutants, but generally the organic matrix is rather considered as inhibitor of SR-based systems (Canonica and Schönenberger, 2019) Nevertheless, the fact is that combination of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and PMS in the presence of RW achieved high catalytic efficiency, which allows to consider this method as potentially effective for the removal of maraviroc in water treatment.

The analyzed phototransformation processes mostly followed pseudo-first-order kinetics (l $\mathtt { n } c = - \mathtt { k t } + \mathtt { l n } c _ { 0 } )$ , which is one of the most often observed in such processes (Ghasemi et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2020; Trawiski and Skibiáski, 2017). Only in the case of direct photodegradation processes were the pseudo-second-order reactions, and the reactions with PMS in both UW and RW fitted the pseudo-zero-order reactions.

# 3.2. Identification of the TPs

Structures of the TPs formed in the studied phototransformation processes were elucidated using high resolution mass spectrometry with positive mode electrospray ionization. The analyses were performed using auto MS/MS mode and various collision induced dissociation (CID) energy, in order to obtain maximum information about the phototransformation products structure. Generally, the structures were suggested on the basis of the accurate masses, with accepted errors not greater than $7 . 0 \ \mathsf { p p m }$ , however in some cases higher values (up to approximately $1 0 \mathrm { p p m }$ were also taken into account (TP18 and TP19), but only if confirmed by the consistent double bond equivalent (DBE) parameter. Nevertheless the key criterion was the MS/MS fragmentation pattern of the identified TPs.

In general, twenty-two phototransformation products of maraviroc were detected, and their structures were elucidated (Table 2). Importantly, due to high number of nucleophilic nitrogen atoms, maraviroc as well as majority of its TPs tend to form multiply  mainly doubly charged ions in the ESI source. Sometimes the doubly-charged species were manyfold more abundant than the single-charged ions (which were even nearly undetectable in case of several products). Therefore, in such cases the evolution profiles were constructed taking into account abundance of the doubly-charged ions. The remaining TPs, which lose 'the excess' of the nucleophilic nitrogen atoms as a consequence of the phototransformation process, were quantified using single-charged ions.

The TPs were divided into the following groups: products of the parent molecule fragmentation (TP  TP4), products of mono-oxidation (TP5  TP11), products of poly-oxidation (TP12  TP16), products of oxidation and dehydrogenation (TP17), and products of degradation followed by rearrangement or oxidation (TP18  TP22). TP1 and TP4 were formed as a consequence of detachment of difluorocyclohexyl (DFCHX) amide fragment. TP1 consists of azabicyclooctane (ABO) and alkyltriazole (ATZ) ring systems, while TP4 represents solely the ATZ moiety. Both TP2 and TP3 were identified as the aldehyde derivatives, however originated from different fragments of the parent molecule. Mono-oxidated products form the largest group of the identified compounds, consisting mostly of the hydroxylated maraviroc derivatives. In the case of TP5 and TP6 hydroxylation took place in the ABO moiety. Similar situation was observed in TP11, however this compound was identified as a maraviroc $N \mathrm { . }$ -oxide. Noteworthy, this compound does not possess a tendency to form doubly-charged ion, which indicates that the ABO nitrogen was blocked by oxygen. TP7, TP8 and TP9 are the products of the phenyl ring hydroxylation, while TP10  the DFCHX hydroxylation. In the case of TP12  TP16 attachment of multiple oxygen atoms was observed. TP12 was identified as a double $N \cdot$ oxide, however in this case the oxidation took place in the ATZ moiety, enabling formation of doubly-charged ions. Triple hydroxylation led to formation of TP13 and TP14, while quadruple gave TP15. TP16 is a product of quintuple hydroxylation and double hydrogenation as well. TP17 is a doubly hydroxylated and dehydrogenated derivative. Determination of accurate location of the additional oxygen atoms present in the TP13

TP17 was not possible on the basis of the collected mass spectrometry data, nevertheless the ABO-ATZ moiety was definitely excluded in all these cases. TP18 and TP19 are twin products, being result of the ATZ oxidative degradation, followed by a recyclization. Whole process probably resulted in formation of an unsaturated 1,3-oxazole ring, possessing a double bond in two different locations. TP20  TP22 represent products of the phenyl ring degradation, followed by oxidation. In the case of TP21 and TP22 whole aromatic ring was eliminated. On the other hand, TP20 possesses one spared methyl group in its structure. Location of the additional oxygen atoms was restricted to the aliphatic chain linking ABO and DFCHX amide moieties. In this case group of the major products was formed by a mixture of mono- and poly oxidized derivatives, as well as phenyl-degraded TPs (Fig. S15).

# 3.3.In silico assessment of aquatic toxicity

Evaluation of toxicity of the identified TPs towards the aquatic organisms was performed using the in silico approach. Totally sixteen prediction models, provided by ECOSAR, T.E.S.T. and Vega software, were applied, five of which estimated toxicity towards algae, next five towards daphnids, and six towards fish (including two models predicting toxicity towards F. minnow). It should be noted that those models use various toxicity units, such as median lethal concentration $\left( \mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 } \right)$ , median effective concentration $\mathrm { ( E C _ { 5 0 } ) }$ or no observed effect concentration (NOEC), thus the obtained toxicity values should be compared in the context of the general trends.

In order to facilitate graphical interpretation of the predicted toxicity data, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. As it was shown in Fig. 4, significant correlations between the applied models occurred, resulting in formation of two groups of variables (Fig. 4). The first one consisted of all the ECOSAR and $D$ magna $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ DEMETRA models, while the second grouped the remaining variables (with the exception of $D .$ magna Chronic NOEC IRFMN). Those two groups of models were rather weakly correlated (angle between vectors is closer to $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ than to $0 ^ { \circ }$ ), which indicates presence of discrepancies in the predicted toxicity trends. Nevertheless, the PCA plot clearly shows that the majority of the TPs possess toxic properties relatively similar to the parent compound. Only three products can be undoubtedly classified as the outliers: TP3, TP4 and TP16. TP4 was definitely the least toxic according to the second group of models, and the second least harmful compound according to the first group. Opposite situation was observed in the case of TP16. Extreme outlying of those TPs indicate that high polarity of the compounds (low molecular mass and high number of nitrogen atoms in case of TP4, and very high hydroxylation rate in case of TP16) implicates significant decrease of the aquatic toxicity. TP3 was also less toxic than the remaining compounds, however in its case the differences were substantially lower, which may be attributed to its lower polarity, when compared to TP4 and TP16. The rest of TPs formed quite well-defined cluster containing also the parent molecule, whose location suggests that its toxicity is generally the highest among all the studied compounds, however the differences are probably insignificant in some cases. Some compounds grouped in the cluster may be classified as slightly outlying. For example TP1 possessed lower toxicity especially according to the D. magna Chronic NOEC IRFMN and the second group of models. Such behavior may be result of relatively high polarity. On the other hand, TP20 and TP21 were less toxic to some extent according to the first group of models. In this case such properties probably result from the degradation of the phenyl ring.

Raw data concerning the in silico estimation of toxicity was presented in the Supplementary material (Table S3).

# 4. Conclusions

In this study photochemical transformation process of maraviroc under the simulated sunlight was presented. The drug turned out to be extraordinarily resistant to UVVis irradiation, which may indicate its significant environmental persistence. Experiment using real river water as a matrix showed that naturally occurring species possess ability to significantly improve the degradation rate. Unusually high photostability of maraviroc was also reflected in the process of its photocatalytic degradation  although application of model titania catalyst significantly increased the process, the observed half-life was still relatively high, even in the presence of river water. However, the use of peroxymonosulfate granted much better results. Even alone, lightactivated PMS was able to halve the concentration of maraviroc after 7 min of irradiation, while in combination with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ recorded half-life was decreased to 0.47 min  over 67 000 times less than in the case of direct photolysis. Although natural matrix slows down all PMS-driven processes, the degradation efficiencies remain satisfactory.

All studied processes resulted in formation of numerous maraviroc phototransformation products. Majority of them was formed as a consequence of parent molecule oxidation and partial decomposition or structural rearrangement. Importantly, in most of the experiments concentrations of those products increased up to termination of the irradiation process  the most advanced level of the TPs decomposition was observed in the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ -PMS system.

The results show that UV-PMS systems, with particular reference to UV-PMS-heterogeneous catalysis, may greatly facilitate degradation process of remarkably photostable contaminants  even taking into account suppressing role of the natural water constituents  simultaneously leading to significant degradation of the formed products.

In silico estimation of aquatic toxicity showed that majority of the formed TPs possess rather similar toxic potential to the parent compounds. Only few compounds were identified as significantly less toxic, and their properties probably arose from higher polarity, being result of low molecular mass coupled with high content of nitrogen (TP4 and, to a less extent, TP3) or high hydroxylation rate (TP16). Much lower decrease of toxicity was a consequence of the phenyl ring degradation (TP20 and TP21). Such findings clearly indicate that degradation of maraviroc itself is insufficient, and in order to achieve significant decline of toxicity the TPs should be also decomposed. Such conditions were fulfilled to the greatest extent using the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ PMS system, which makes it the most suitable for toxicity abatement of maraviroccontaining water.
