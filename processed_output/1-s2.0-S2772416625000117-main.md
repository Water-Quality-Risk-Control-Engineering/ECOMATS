Keywords:

Acetaminophen (ACT), a widely used analgesic and antipyretic, frequently appears in various aquatic environments due to its extensive use and incomplete removal by conventional wastewater treatment processes. The persistence and potential adverse effects of ACT necessitate the development of effective treatment technologies. Sulfate radical based-advanced oxidation processes (SR-AOPs), which generate sulfate radicals with high oxidative potential, are examined for their ability to degrade complex organic molecules such as ACT. To increase sulfate radical generation and promote degradation efficiency, a variety of catalytic systems have been reviewed in this study, including carbon-based materials, transition metal oxides, and composite catalysts. Key factors such as pH, temperature, and the presence of co-contaminants are analyzed for their impact on the degradation process. The study also addresses the stability and leaching of catalysts, which are critical for the sustainability and environmental safety of SR-AOPs. Furthermore, the formation of by-products during ACT degradation is discussed, with a focus on the identification and toxicity of these intermediates. The review underscores the importance of monitoring both the formation and breakdown of intermediates to fully understand the toxicity dynamics and ensure the safety of the treated effluents. By integrating recent advancements and case studies, this review highlights the strengths and limitations of SR-AOPs and identifies areas for future research. The work offers to optimize SR-AOPs for large-scale water treatment applications, thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of their potential to treat water contaminated with pharmaceutically active compounds.

# 1. Introduction

Pharmaceutically active compounds (PhACs) have emerged as ubiquitous environmental contaminants due to their widespread use and incomplete removal by conventional wastewater treatment processes (Dos Santos et al., 2022). Among these, ACT, a widely used analgesic and antipyretic, is frequently detected in various aquatic environments (Park and Oh, 2020). The persistence and potential adverse effects of ACT on human health and aquatic ecosystems necessitate the development of effective and sustainable treatment technologies (Igwegbe et al., 2021). Traditional treatment methods often fall short in completely eliminating ACT, leading to a growing interest in advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) that can degrade such recalcitrant compounds more effectively (Shokouhi et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023; Movahedyan et al., 2009; Shahmahdi et al., 2020).

Sulfate radical-based advanced oxidation processes (SR-AOPs) have gained significant attention as a promising approach for the degradation of recalcitrant organic pollutants, including PhACs (Li et al., 2024). SR-AOPs can generate sulfate radicals $( \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - } )$ ) along with other reactive radicals, which have a high oxidative potential and can effectively break down complex organic molecules into smaller, less harmful compounds (Lalas et al., 2022; Naseri et al., 2023). The versatility of SR-AOPs, which can be activated using various methods such as heat, UV light, and transition metal catalysts, makes them suitable for a wide range of environmental conditions and applications (Giannakis et al., 2021). This flexibility, combined with the strong oxidative power of $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ , makes SR-AOPs a highly effective technology for the treatment of contaminated water.

The degradation of ACT using SR-AOPs involves a complex interplay of mechanisms, including the generation of reactive species, the activation of the persulfate, and the optimization of operational parameters. Different catalytic systems, such as transition metal oxides, carbonbased materials, and composite catalysts, have been explored to enhance the efficiency of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ generation and to improve the degradation rates of ACT (Sun et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2017). The role of these catalysts, along with the influence of factors such as pH, temperature, and the presence of co-contaminants, is critical in determining the overall performance of the SR-AOPs. Understanding these interactions is essential for optimizing the processes and achieving maximum degradation efficiency.

Another crucial aspect of SR-AOPs is the stability and leaching of catalysts, which can significantly impact the long-term sustainability and environmental safety of the treatment processes (Honarmandrad et al., 2023). Catalyst stability ensures consistent performance over multiple cycles, while minimal leaching reduces the risk of secondary pollution. Studies have shown varying degrees of catalyst stability and leaching behavior, depending on the catalyst composition and operational conditions. For instance, some catalysts demonstrate excellent stability with negligible leaching, while others may require periodic regeneration or replacement. Addressing these challenges is vital for the practical application of SR-AOPs in real-world scenarios.

It should be noted that SR-AOP for water/wastewater treatment has been the subject of numerous recent publications (Zhou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Uluçtan et al., 2025; García et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2025). However, this review offers a comprehensive analysis of degradation pathways, identifying key intermediate products and clarifying the complete degradation mechanism of ACT as a representative pharmaceutical pollutant. It combines findings on the influence of operational parameters such as pH, temperature, and oxidant concentration, providing a comprehensive understanding of their effects on degradation efficiency. Additionally, the review evaluates the impact of co-existing components in real wastewater matrices, offering a realistic assessment of SR-AOPs in complex environments. Meanwhile, lots of research has either examined a large number of pollutants, which does not offer a thorough perspective for industrial applications, or some studies have only looked at a particular subset of these processes, which in any case emphasizes the need for more research of this kind.

By emphasizing the importance of long-term stability and reusability of catalysts, the study facilitates more accurate comparisons and enhances the reliability of conclusions drawn from multiple studies. Furthermore, it underscores the necessity of comprehensive environmental and toxicological assessments to ensure the formation of nontoxic byproducts. Through these advancements, the review significantly enhances the current knowledge base and provides valuable insights for future research and practical applications in SR-AOPs. By integrating recent advancements and case studies, this review aims to highlight the strengths and limitations of SR-AOPs, identify areas for future research, and offer insights into optimizing these processes for large-scale water treatment applications. The degradation of ACT serves not only as a case study for understanding the dynamics of SR-AOPs but also as a benchmark for exploring their broader applicability in addressing the challenges posed by emerging contaminants in aquatic environments.

# 2. Acetaminophen

Among the recalcitrant organic contaminants of anthropogenic origin, ACT (N-acetyl-p-aminophenol) is the most popular antipyretic pharmaceutical, widely used around the world even without any medical prescription (Vo et al., 2019). Therefore, the annual worldwide intake of this pharmaceutical agent—a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, or NSAID—is almost 1.45 tons/year (Noorisepehr et al., 2020). ACT solubility at ${ } ^ { 2 5 } \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ has been reported as $3 0 5 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ , indicating a relatively slight solubility in water (Table 1) (Liu et al., 2023). With a Log $K _ { \mathrm { o w } }$ value of 0.46, ACT is considered a hydrophilic molecule. After oral administration, about $9 0 ~ \%$ of ACT is mainly excreted via urine as unchanged and conjugated forms (Kalantzi et al., 2006; Forrest et al., 1982). In addition to the animal and human urine excretion to wastewater, ACT can enter the aquatic/natural environment through the release of pharmaceutical industries, and the discarding of expired or unused drugs (Fig. 1) (Vo et al., 2019). Due to its extensive clinical application, ACT is frequently detected in a variety of environments, such as wastewater, natural surface water, groundwater, and streams, with concentrations ranging from ng/L to mg/L (Vo et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2018). The direct release of untreated or partially treated wastewater, a significant source of ACT, can have severe ecotoxic effects on aquatic species and carcinogenic and mutagenic effects on humans (Vieira et al., 2024). It is, therefore, imperative to properly remove ACT and its degradation products from water before discharge into the environment to preserve environmental quality and mitigate its detrimental effects on both ecosystems and human health.

Conventional wastewater treatment techniques often partially degrade these recalcitrant compounds because of their inherent design limitations to tackle such complex compounds with high hydrophilicity (Wang et al., 2019). With such a Log $K _ { \mathrm { o w } }$ value, ACT shows a low tendency for adsorption to solid organic matter. Also, its non-volatile nature, indicated by low saturated pressure, implies inefficient removal through volatilization in municipal WWTPs. As a result, extensive research is currently underway all over the world to identify alternative methods for removing PhACs from wastewater, among which SR-AOPs warrant special attention.

# 3. SR-AOPS

Research developments in the middle of the 2000s gave rise to a subclass of AOPs called SR-AOPs, in which the production of ${ \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ is encouraged, either independently or in conjunction with hydroxyl radicals (OH⋅ ) (Anipsitakis and Dionysiou, 2003).

It takes the activation of an appropriate oxidant to produce $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ . Consequently, peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and peroxydisulfate (PS), two of the strongest oxidants utilized in environmental applications (redox potential: 1.82 V vs. NHE for PSS and 2.01 V vs. NHE for PS) have been utilized extensively (Eqs. (1) and (2)) (Hou et al., 2021).

The most widely used activation techniques are alkali, heat, photocatalytic activation $( \mathrm { h } ^ { + } / \mathrm { e } ^ { - } )$ , microwaves, ultrasounds, UV or visible light, and metal or non-metal catalysts (Hou et al., 2021). Different transition metals (Co, Fe, Mn, Cu, Ag, etc.) can activate PMS and PS (Zhu et al., 2021). Their higher valance can produce low reactivity $S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \cdot - }$ and $S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { - }$ and can catalyze the disruption of the $\mathrm { o - } \mathrm { o }$ bonds of persulfate anions, leading to the production of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ (Eqs. (3) and (4)). For PS activation via Fe(III), Eq. (7) was also proposed, which involves Fe(III) oxidation to higher valence iron (Ike et al., 2017). In fact, Fe(IV) might aid in the breakdown of organic contaminants (Wang et al., 2020a). According to Eq. (8), surplus transition metals can also scavenge $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ . High energy inputs, such as heat, UV, US, and microwaves, convert one mole of PS or PMS into two moles of radicals (Eqs. (9), (10)). PMS and PS can be broken down into free radicals by photo-induced electrons $( { \mathsf { e } } ^ { - } )$ and photo-induced holes $( \mathrm { h } ^ { + } )$ produced by stimulating photocatalysts (Eqs. ((11)–(14)). Apart from the application of PMS and PS, sulfate ions have also been utilized in the production of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ (Gągol et al., 2020).

Any of the aforementioned activation methods that PS or PMS are subjected to cleaves the peroxide bond, producing $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ for PS or OH⋅ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ for PMS, respectively (Luo et al., 2015). Depending on the method of activation, PMS or PS can produce sulfate or sulfate and hydroxyl radicals. Fig. 2 (Wacławek et al., 2017) provides an overview of how these species typically change based on the matrix’s pH and activation mode, which spreads radical reactions. Because they may affect the process’s overall effectiveness, the operational pH and the components in the matrix should be taken into account when selecting an oxidant. The resulting radicals have half-lifetimes of $3 0 { - } 4 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { s } }$ for $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ and ${ 2 0 } \mathtt { n s }$ for OH⋅ (Oh et al., 2016). Although they are both highly oxidative (2.5–3.1 and $1 . 8 { - } 2 . 7 \ : \mathrm { V }$ vs. NHE), their selectivity to electronrich moieties is what distinguishes them from one another (Wang et al., 2017a).

Other radicals, such as the superoxide/hydroperoxyl radical, may also be produced depending on the catalyst’s nature. Lately, the induction of non-radical pathways, which include the generation of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathsf { e } ^ { - }$ , has come under scrutiny (Duan et al., 2020). The homogeneous or heterogeneous application mode for persulfate activation depends on the type of catalyst used. While homogeneous AOPs undoubtedly have higher efficacy, their lower efficiency over a wide pH range led to a wide range of heterogeneous applications (Ahmed et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2009). The benefits of heterogeneous AOPs have since been widely utilized, including the catalyst’s easy separation from the media, durability in adverse circumstances, and potential for future reuse (Wang et al., 2017b; Gong et al., 2018).

# 4. Factors influencing SR-AOPs efficiency

# 4.1. Effect of pH

The influence of pH on the degradation efficiency of ACT in SR-AOPs is substantial, as it affects the generation, stability, and reactivity of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ (Liang et al., 2022). The provided data in Table 2 indicates that SR-AOPs achieve high degradation efficiencies across a range of pH levels. However, the most frequently observed optimal pH range tends to be in neutral to slightly acidic conditions. This pH range is particularly important because it aligns with many natural and wastewater conditions, making it practical for real-scale applications. For example, the CoAl-CLDH/PMS system reached $1 0 0 \%$ degradation efficiency at pH 6 within $1 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , indicating that neutral pH conditions can be optimal for certain catalysts (Zhu et al., 2019). Additionally, systems such as $\mathbf { S } – \mathbf { M g O / P M S }$ and SCl-CN/PMS/Vis showed excellent degradation efficiencies of $1 0 0 ~ \%$ at $\mathrm { p H } 7$ (Fanaei et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2024). These results suggest that while neutral pH may not always provide the highest rate of radical production, it can still facilitate effective degradation through other mechanisms, such as direct oxidation by the activated oxidants.

In acidic environments $\mathrm { ( p H < 4 ) }$ , PS and PMS are typically more efficient, leading to high degradation rates of ACT. For instance, the $\mathrm { F e ^ { 2 + } / M o S _ { 2 } / P M S }$ system achieved a $1 0 0 \%$ degradation efficiency of ACT at a pH of 3 within $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , with a rate constant of $0 . 1 0 9 9 \mathrm { { m i n } } ^ { - 1 }$ (Zhang et al., 2020). This enhanced performance is attributed to the increased formation of highly reactive ${ \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ under acidic conditions. Similarly, the $\mathrm { F e ( I I I ) / C u ( 0 ) / P M S }$ system showed an $8 8 . 8 \%$ degradation at $\mathrm { p H } 3$ in 12 min, demonstrating the favorable effect of low pH on the catalytic activity and radical generation (Liu et al., 2021).

In alkaline environments $\mathrm { ( p H > 8 ) }$ , the degradation efficiency of SRAOPs can decline due to the reduced formation of ${ \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ and increased formation of less reactive species (Liang et al., 2022). However, certain systems have shown promising results by enhancing the generation of other reactive oxygen species. For instance, the NaOH-enhanced heat/PMS system achieved $1 0 0 ~ \%$ degradation at a highly alkaline pH of 10.43 within $1 5 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ , indicating that tailored approaches can maintain high degradation efficiencies even at elevated pH levels (Cai et al., 2022a). This is often achieved through the promotion of OH⋅ and other reactive intermediates that contribute to the degradation process.

Several studies have explored the degradation of ACT over a wide pH range, providing insights into the adaptability and robustness of SRAOPs. For example, the SA-Co CNP/PMS system effectively degraded ACT across a $\mathsf { p H }$ range of 3 to 9, achieving $9 2 \%$ degradation within 10 min, indicating the system’s efficiency across varying $\mathsf { p H }$ conditions (Liao et al., 2022). Another notable example is the PB-Mn-X/PMS system, which achieved $1 0 0 ~ \%$ degradation efficiency within $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ over a pH range of 3 to 11, demonstrating consistent performance across a broad spectrum of pH levels (Zhu et al., 2023).

It should be mentioned that pH plays a critical role in determining the degradation efficiency of ACT in SR-AOPs. While acidic conditions generally favor the generation and activity of $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ , neutral and slightly alkaline conditions can also yield high efficiencies with the appropriate catalytic systems. Optimizing the reaction conditions for specific pH ranges can enhance the degradation performance, making SR-AOPs a versatile approach for pollutant removal in various aqueous environments.

# 4.2. Effect of initial PS/PMS dosage

To optimize the degradation of pollutants, the impact of PS or PMS concentration on the degradation removal in SR-AOPs is critical. For $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ to be generated in sufficient amounts without causing scavenging effects or secondary pollution, the right dosage is needed (Zhou et al., 2018). Up to a certain point, the degradation rate is usually accelerated by higher PS/PMS concentrations. Because of the overabundance of $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ , which may recombine to form persulfate and thereby reduce the availability of reactive radicals for the degradation process, concentration increases beyond this optimal level may not improve and may even reduce the efficiency of the degradation process (Zou et al., 2021).

With $1 0 0 ~ \%$ removal efficiency in just two minutes, the PMS/amorphous $\mathrm { C o ( O H ) _ { 2 } }$ process used the least amount of radical precursor among the studies that used PMS for radical production $( 5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { M } )$ (Qi et al., 2021). Therefore, in regard to precursor consumption and retention time, it can be concluded that this technique is appealing. A noteworthy aspect of this treatment technique is its high reaction rate $( k =$ $3 . 6 8 \mathrm { \ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ). The PS/bicarbonate process consumes the least PS overall $( 1 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { M } )$ , but even with its $6 8 ~ \%$ removal efficiency in $4 2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , the outcome is not very impressive (Jiang et al., 2017). Table 2 illustrate that both PS and PMS concentrations in the range of $0 . 5 \mathrm { m M }$ to $2 \mathrm { m M }$ are commonly effective for achieving high degradation efficiencies.

# 4.3. Effect of temperature

Temperature generally enhances the degradation efficiency by accelerating the reaction rates and increasing the generation of reactive radicals (Li et al., 2018). For instance, an increase in temperature to 80 $^ \circ \mathrm { C }$ in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } / \mathrm { P S }$ system resulted in a degradation efficiency of $1 0 0 ~ \%$ with a significantly higher rate constant of $1 0 . 4 1 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ compared to all other studies (Deng et al., 2014). This indicates that higher temperatures facilitate more rapid decomposition of PS or PMS, leading to the production of more $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ , which are highly reactive and efficient in degrading organic pollutants such as ACT.

Conducted at a temperature range of $2 0 { - } 2 7 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , Sunlight/PS/Fe²⁺ system achieved a degradation efficiency of $9 4 . 1 \%$ with a rate constant of $0 . 5 7 6 \mathrm { ~ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ (Ramos et al., 2023). The use of sunlight in conjunction with PS activation at ambient temperatures shows effective degradation, demonstrating the potential for energy-efficient and environmentally friendly processes. At an elevated temperature of $9 0 \quad ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$ , NaOH-Enhanced Heat/PMS system reached a $1 0 0 ~ \%$ degradation efficiency with a robust rate constant, highlighting the synergistic effect of heat and alkaline conditions in enhancing PMS activation and subsequent pollutant degradation (Cai et al., 2022a). In this investigation, when the reaction temperature rose from 55 to $9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } ,$ the $K _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ value of ACT degradation increased from 0.0921 to $0 . 4 8 7 4 \mathrm { ~ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ .

These observations underscore the importance of temperature as a parameter in optimizing SR-AOPs for wastewater treatment and environmental remediation. Future studies should aim to balance temperature with energy consumption to develop sustainable and cost-effective degradation processes.

# 4.4. Effect of co-existing components

Anions commonly present in natural waters, such as sulfate $( S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } )$ nitrate $\mathrm { ( N O _ { 3 } \mathrm { ^ { - } ) } }$ , carbonate $( \mathrm { C O _ { 3 } ^ { 2 - } } )$ , bicarbonate $\mathrm { ( H C O _ { 3 } ^ { - } ) }$ , and chloride (Cl⁻), can significantly impact the efficiency of SR-AOPs in the removal of ACT.

Generally, the presence of sulfate ions does not significantly hinder the degradation efficiency of ACT (Table 3). For example, in the $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } { \big / }$ $\mathrm { Z n O @ N i F e _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } / P M S / U V A }$ system, sulfate ions at concentrations of 50 $\mathrm { { \ m g / L } }$ resulted in a removal efficiency reduction to $6 4 \%$ from the control of $1 0 0 \%$ (Kohantorabi et al., 2021). However, in other systems, such as NaOH-enhanced heat/PMS and ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { C O } } _ { 3 }$ /thermal/PMS, the removal efficiency remained at $1 0 0 ~ \%$ even with $\mathrm { 5 ~ m M }$ sulfate ions present, indicating that sulfate ions might have variable effects depending on the specific SR-AOP system used (Cai et al., 2022a, 2022b).

The impact of nitrate ions is relatively moderate. For instance, in the LED/(Cu, $\mathrm { F e } @ 8 \mathrm { - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } ) / \mathrm { P S }$ system, the presence of $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ nitrate resulted in a slight reduction in removal efficiency to $8 9 \%$ from a control of $100 \%$ (Alvandi et al., 2023). In systems like $\mathbf { A g } / \mathbf { Z n O } @ \mathbf { N i } .$ - Fe2O4/PMS/UVA, $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ nitrate reduced the efficiency to $5 9 \%$ from the control of $1 0 0 ~ \%$ , showing that higher nitrate concentrations can inhibit the degradation process significantly (Kohantorabi et al., 2021).

Carbonate and bicarbonate ions tend to have a more pronounced inhibitory effect on ACT removal. In the $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { M n } @ \mathbf { C A s } / \mathbf { P M S } } \end{array}$ system, $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ carbonate resulted in a removal efficiency of $5 0 \%$ , while bicarbonate at the same concentration showed a removal efficiency of $4 6 ~ \%$ (Zhang et al., 2024). These ions likely scavenge $\mathrm { 8 O } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ , thereby reducing the available radicals for ACT degradation.

The presence of chloride ions can both inhibit and promote ACT degradation, depending on the concentration and the SR-AOP system. For example, in the PMS/chlorine system, $^ { 1 \ m M }$ chloride increased the removal efficiency to $9 6 ~ \%$ compared to a control of $8 8 ~ \%$ (Ding et al., 2021). In contrast, in the $\mathsf { A g / Z n O @ N i F e _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } } /$ PMS/UVA system, $5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ chloride resulted in a significant reduction in removal efficiency to $5 0 \%$ from a control of $1 0 0 ~ \%$ (Kohantorabi et al., 2021).

Humic acid, a complex mixture of partially decomposed organic matter, often presents a significant challenge in AOPs due to its ability to act as a radical scavenger (Pan et al., 2024). At low to moderate concentrations, humic acid can moderately inhibit ACT removal. For instance, in the $\mathrm { F e ( I I I ) / C u ( 0 ) / P M S }$ system, $0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ HA resulted in a removal efficiency of $7 9 \%$ compared to a control of $8 8 . 8 ~ \%$ (Liu et al., 2021). Similarly, in the SA-Co CNP/PMS system, 1 mM HA reduced the removal efficiency to $7 0 \%$ from a control of $9 2 \%$ (Liao et al., 2022). At higher concentrations, the inhibitory effect of humic acid becomes more pronounced. In the Mn@NBC-800/PMS system, humic acid concentrations ranging from 2 to $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ resulted in $2 2 \%$ degradation, indicating $1 0 0 ~ \%$ removal efficiency in its absence, suggesting a severe negative impact at high HA levels (Sun et al., 2024). Similarly, in the SFBC800/PMS system, humic acid concentrations from 1 to $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ caused a reduction in removal efficiency from $9 6 \ \%$ to $3 8 . 4 \ \%$ , demonstrating a substantial inhibitory effect with increasing HA concentrations (Bae et al., 2024). It’s interesting to note that, even in the presence of this organic substance, two Biochar/PMS and DEMAS200/PMS systems were able to achieve complete removal, negating the influence of the humic acid (Li et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2024).

The efficiency SR-AOPs in removing ACT is significantly influenced by the presence of anions and humic acid in the water matrix. Anions such as sulfate, nitrate, carbonate, bicarbonate, and chloride have varied impacts, with carbonate and bicarbonate showing more consistent inhibitory effects (Fig. 3). Humic acid, acting as a radical scavenger, generally reduces the degradation efficiency of ACT, especially at higher concentrations. Numerous studies have also looked into the impact of various phosphorus compounds on the effectiveness of SR-AOPs. These substances were shown to have an inhibitory effect on the elimination of ACT in the majority of cases. These findings underscore the importance of considering the specific water matrix composition when applying SRAOPs for pharmaceutical contaminant removal in real-world scenarios.

# 5. Mineralization of ACT in different SR-AOPs

The mineralization efficiency of SR-AOPs, often evaluated by total organic carbon (TOC) removal, is a critical parameter indicating the extent to which organic contaminants are fully degraded to inorganic compounds such as $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ . The TOC removal efficiency across different SR-AOPs varies widely based on the catalyst used, operating conditions, and the nature of the target contaminant (Rodríguez-Chueca et al., 2017). The data from various studies presented in Table 2 provide a comprehensive overview of the mineralization performance of these processes.

Several SR-AOPs exhibit high TOC removal efficiencies, reflecting their robust capability to achieve extensive mineralization of ACT. For instance, the system utilizing PB-Mn-X/PMS at a concentration of $^ { 1 \mathrm { m M } }$ PMS achieved a remarkable $8 6 . 4 ~ \%$ TOC removal, indicating almost complete mineralization of the target compound (Zhu et al., 2023). This highlights the potential of the technique in achieving substantial TOC reduction without the need for high energy input. Similarly, the UVA/BiOCl/Na2S2O8 system, operated at $\mathrm { p H } ~ 5 . 4$ with $1 ~ \mathrm { \ m M }$ PS, demonstrated an impressive TOC removal efficiency of $8 5 \ \%$ , highlighting the effective activation of PS under UV irradiation (Wang et al., 2020b). The SCl-CN/PMS/Vis system demonstrated a $6 1 . 3 \ \%$ TOC removal efficiency in the middle range when exposed to visible light (Liu et al., 2024). This suggests that even though the system works well, there might be some restrictions because of incomplete mineralization or the development of intermediate byproducts. Another example is the CF/MCN/PMS system, which at pH 7 with $1 . 5 \mathrm { m M }$ PMS achieved a TOC removal efficiency of $6 5 ~ \%$ (Hassani et al., 2020). Although significant degradation occurs, these moderate efficiencies imply that catalyst design and process conditions can be optimized to achieve higher mineralization rates.

Some systems exhibit lower TOC removal efficiencies, highlighting the challenges in achieving complete mineralization under certain conditions. The $\mathrm { N a _ { 2 } C O _ { 3 } } ,$ /thermal/PMS system, for instance, achieved a TOC removal efficiency of $1 0 ~ \%$ at $\mathrm { p H } 6 . 5 2 $ with $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ PMS (Cai et al., 2022b). This indicates that despite the presence of efficient oxidant generation, the process might be hindered by factors such as radical scavengers or the formation of recalcitrant intermediates. Similarly, the $\mathbf { M n } @ \mathbf { C A s } / \mathbf { P M S }$ system showed a lower TOC removal efficiency of $1 0 ~ \%$ despite achieving complete degradation of the parent compound, suggesting that mineralization was not fully realized (Zhang et al., 2024).

These varying levels of TOC removal highlight the importance of optimizing both the catalytic materials and operational conditions to maximize mineralization efficiency. Future research should focus on enhancing catalyst design, fine-tuning operational parameters, and conducting comprehensive evaluations of byproduct formation and removal. By addressing these areas, the overall performance of SR-AOPs can be significantly improved, making them more viable for practical applications in water and wastewater treatment.

In the context of SR-AOPs, several studies have investigated PS/PMS activation techniques where the $\mathrm { 8 O } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ played either a minimal or no role in the degradation of ACT. Notably, techniques such as $\mathrm { U V C } / \mathrm { S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - }$ , PMS/chlorine, PS/bicarbonate, Mn-doped $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } } / \mathrm { P M S }$ , Fe-BC/PMS, Biochar/PMS, SFBC800/PMS, $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { M n } @ \mathbf { C A s } / \mathbf { P M S } } \end{array}$ , and Mn@NBC-800/PMS have demonstrated this phenomenon (Sun et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2021; Bae et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2019; Zeng et al., 2023; Moussavi et al., 2017). These methods, although based on the activation of PS or PMS, often rely on other reactive species or mechanisms for the degradation process. This observation highlights the complexity of the degradation mechanisms and the potential for various intermediates and catalysts to influence the effectiveness of ACT removal. Understanding these nuances is crucial for optimizing SR-AOPs and ensuring efficient pollutant degradation.

# 6. Feasibility of practical applications

SR-AOPs have demonstrated significant potential for the degradation of ACT in various real water matrices, including wastewater, groundwater, surface water, and tap water (Table 4). These applications highlight the versatility and efficiency of SR-AOP systems under different environmental conditions, making them promising for largescale implementation.

Wastewater treatment is a critical application area for SR-AOPs due to the high levels of pharmaceutical contaminants typically present. For instance, the COCN/PMS system achieved a $6 4 \%$ removal rate of ACT in wastewater within just $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , demonstrating rapid degradation capabilities (Nguyen et al., 2024). Similarly, the DEMAS200/PMS system reported an $8 9 . 9 8 ~ \%$ removal efficiency in sequencing batch reactor (SBR) effluent and a $6 7 . 7 6 \%$ removal rate in domestic sewage within 15 min, indicating robust performance across different types of wastewater (Wan et al., 2024). These high removal rates demonstrate the effectiveness of SR-AOPs in mitigating pharmaceutical pollutants in complex wastewater matrices, thus reducing environmental and health risks associated with untreated effluent discharge.

SR-AOPs also exhibit substantial efficacy in treating groundwater and surface water, where the presence of pharmaceuticals like ACT can pose significant environmental threats. For example, the $\mathrm { F e ( I I I ) / C u ( 0 ) / }$ PMS system achieved $8 2 . 5 ~ \%$ ACT removal in groundwater within 12 min, indicating rapid and efficient degradation in subsurface environments (Liu et al., 2021). The SFBC800/PMS system showed a removal efficiency ranging from $9 8 . 7 \%$ to $2 2 . 1 9 \%$ in groundwater over $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , depending on the specific conditions and ingredient concentrations (Bae et al., 2024). In surface water, the SA-Co CNP/PMS and NS-CMK-3/PMS systems reported removal efficiencies of $8 8 ~ \%$ and $6 5 ~ \%$ , respectively, within $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , highlighting their effectiveness in surface water bodies (Liao et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2021). These results underscore the adaptability of SR-AOPs in various natural water matrices, making them suitable for applications in diverse geographic and environmental settings.

The application of SR-AOPs in tap water treatment focuses on ensuring the removal of residual pharmaceuticals to meet stringent drinking water standards. Several SR-AOP systems have shown exceptional performance in this context. For instance, the $\mathbf { S } – \mathbf { M } \mathbf { g } \mathbf { O } / \mathbf { P } \mathbf { M } S$ system achieved $9 9 \%$ ACT removal in tap water within $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , demonstrating their high efficiency in cleaner matrices (Fanaei et al., 2019). Additionally, the Co-SnP/PMS system exhibited complete removal $( 1 0 0 \% )$ of ACT in tap water within $2 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , emphasizing the potential for achieving non-detectable levels of contaminants in drinking water supplies (Deng et al., 2024). The $\mathrm { A g / Z n O @ N i F e _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } / P M S / U V }$ VA and PB-Mn-X/PMS systems also reported high removal efficiencies of $9 3 \ \%$ and $1 0 0 ~ \%$ , respectively, indicating their applicability in advanced drinking water treatment processes (Zhu et al., 2023; Kohantorabi et al., 2021).

# 7. Formation of by-products

The degradation of ACT through SR-AOPs generates a variety of intermediate compounds, which play a critical role in understanding the degradation pathways and ensuring the completeness of the pollutant removal process (Table 5 and Fig. 4). Among the most common intermediates identified are hydroquinone and its related compounds, such as p-benzoquinone and benzene-1,4-diol, which are observed in systems like PB-Mn-X/PMS (Zhu et al., 2023). The frequent formation of hydroquinone and related quinonoid structures suggests a predominant oxidative attack on the aromatic ring of ACT, leading to ring cleavage and subsequent hydroxylation (Vogna et al., 2002). In the study involving the SA-Co CNP/PMS and Mn-doped $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } } / \mathrm { P M S }$ systems, hydroquinone was identified as the sole intermediate (Liao et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2019). This indicates that the degradation pathway of ACT in this system likely involves the direct oxidative cleavage of the aromatic ring, leading to the formation of hydroquinone without subsequent transformation into other by-products. Additionally, phenolic compounds such as phenol and p-hydroxylphenol are notable intermediates in systems like mCS-Fe/PS and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { P S }$ (Wang et al., 2019; Dong et al., 2018). These intermediates indicate successive oxidation reactions that further break down the ACT structure, transforming it into smaller, more reactive species.

Amides and carboxylic acids are also significant intermediates produced during the degradation of ACT. Compounds such as acetamide, N(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl) formamide, and various carboxylic acids like acetic acid and formic acid are identified in systems including $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } /$ $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 } / \mathbf { P M S }$ and $\mathrm { F e ^ { 2 + } / C u O / P S }$ (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2018). These intermediates reflect the extensive oxidation and fragmentation of the ACT molecule into more oxidized forms. Additionally, complex organic molecules such as $\mathrm { C _ { 8 } H _ { 9 } N O _ { 3 } }$ , $\mathrm { C _ { 8 } H _ { 1 0 } N O _ { 3 } } ,$ and $\mathrm { C _ { 1 6 } H _ { 1 6 } N _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } }$ appear in processes like $\mathbf { S } – \mathbf { M g O / P M S }$ and PMS/amorphous ${ \mathrm { C o } } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ , demonstrating the formation of conjugated systems and multifunctional intermediates (Fanaei et al., 2019; Qi et al., 2021). Furthermore, chlorinated intermediates, including dichloroacetic acid and trichloroacetic acid, emerge in systems using chlorinated oxidants like PMS/chlorine, highlighting the incorporation of chlorine atoms into the degradation pathway (Ding et al., 2021). These diverse intermediates underscore the complexity of the degradation mechanisms and the need for comprehensive analysis to optimize SR-AOPs.

Some intermediates may possess toxicity or persistence, necessitating further treatment to mitigate their environmental or health impacts. By focusing on the intermediates, researchers can refine reaction conditions and catalyst designs to enhance degradation efficiency while minimizing the formation of harmful by-products. Ultimately, the comprehensive study of intermediates not only advances the fundamental understanding of SR-AOPs but also aids in developing more effective and safer treatment technologies.

# 8. The toxicity of ACT and its by-products

The degradation of ACT using SR-AOPs generally leads to a dynamic shift in toxicity levels, influenced by the formation and subsequent breakdown of various intermediates. Initial stages of the degradation often exhibit increased toxicity due to the formation of transient byproducts, as observed in multiple studies (Table 6). The toxicity of the PMS/CBNS/CAHM and NaOH-enhanced heat/PMS systems, for example, showed an initial spike, followed by a peak in inhibition percentages and ratios, which eventually declined as the intermediates continued to degrade (Cai et al., 2022a; Soltani et al., 2020). This trend underscores the importance of monitoring both the formation and degradation phases to fully understand the impact of these processes on toxicity levels.

As degradation progresses, the acute and chronic toxicity of the byproducts often decreases, highlighting the efficacy of SR-AOPs in detoxifying ACT-contaminated solutions. Studies using S-MgO/PMS, $\mathrm { N a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 } ,$ /thermal/PMS, and Fe-BC/PMS systems reported substantial reductions in toxicity over time (Fanaei et al., 2019; Cai et al., 2022b; Zeng et al., 2023). For example, the $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ values for Daphnia magna increased significantly post-treatment, indicating lower toxicity of the final effluents. Similarly, systems like the CoS/S(IV) and PMS/amorphous ${ \mathrm { C o } } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ demonstrated variable toxicity profiles, where some intermediates exhibited higher toxicity compared to ACT, but the overall trend showed a reduction in mutagenicity and toxicity of the final degradation products (Qi et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022).

In broader environmental and biological contexts, the overall reduction in toxicity following complete degradation is promising. Systems such as COCN/PMS and DEMAS200/PMS revealed that the final byproducts were less toxic or even non-toxic compared to the parent compound, ACT (Wan et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2024). For instance, the minimal impact on mung bean germination rates further supports the environmental safety of these treated effluents. These findings collectively illustrate that while initial byproducts may pose increased risks, the advanced oxidation processes ultimately lead to safer, less toxic final products, underscoring the potential of SR-AOPs in mitigating the environmental impact of pharmaceutical contaminants.

# 9. The catalyst stability and reusability

The stability and leaching behavior of catalysts SR-AOPs are critical factors influencing the efficiency and environmental impact of ACT degradation. Various studies have evaluated these aspects to determine the long-term viability and safety of different catalytic systems (Table 7). In the context of SR-AOPs for ACT removal, several catalysts have demonstrated remarkable stability over multiple cycles of use. For instance, PB-Mn-X/PMS maintained $1 0 0 ~ \%$ degradation efficiency of ACT even after five consecutive cycles, indicating excellent structural integrity and catalytic performance (Zhu et al., 2023). Similarly, DEMAS200/PMS showed sustained activity after five cycles, confirming its robustness in repeated applications (Wan et al., 2024). Other catalysts such as Mn-doped $g { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } } / \mathrm { P M S }$ and NS-CMK-3/PMS also showed commendable stability, with the former maintaining $9 7 ~ \%$ of its initial activity after four cycles and the latter retaining $9 8 ~ \%$ after five cycles (Fan et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021). This high stability suggests that these catalysts can be effective over long-term applications, making them suitable candidates for long-term applications and thereby lowering operational costs.

Leaching, or the release of catalyst components into the treated solution, is a significant concern as it can lead to secondary contamination and reduce the catalyst’s active lifetime. The extent of leaching varies among different catalysts used in SR-AOPs. For example, Fe/TNTs activated PMS exhibited minimal iron leaching (not detectable), suggesting its potential for safe and environmentally friendly application in water treatment processes (Ma et al., 2021). In contrast, some catalysts such as CoM/TNTs/PMS showed higher levels of leaching, raising concerns about their environmental impact and necessitating additional steps to mitigate secondary contamination (Chen et al., 2021). Notably, the design of the catalyst plays a crucial role in minimizing leaching. Catalysts such as $\mathrm { A g / Z n O @ N i F e _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } }$ and DEMAS200 are engineered to anchor the active metal sites firmly, thereby reducing the tendency for leaching and enhancing overall stability (Kohantorabi et al., 2021; Wan et al., 2024). For instance, CoIB exhibited negligible cobalt leaching, underscoring the effectiveness of the biochar support in stabilizing the cobalt species (Yang et al., 2019). This indicates that the structural design and choice of support materials are pivotal in developing leaching-resistant catalysts for SR-AOPs.

The sustainable application of SR-AOPs requires the use of catalysts that balance high degradation efficiency with minimal environmental risks. Additionally, the use of recyclable and robust catalysts reduces the demand for raw materials and lowers the environmental footprint of the process. # 10. Challenges and future directions for practical application of SR-AOPs

SR-AOPs have demonstrated significant potential in the degradation of persistent pharmaceutical contaminants such as ACT. The effectiveness of SR-AOPs in generating highly reactive $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet _ { - } }$ capable of breaking down complex organic pollutants positions them as a promising technology for future water treatment applications. However, several challenges must be addressed to enable large-scale practical applications (Fig. 5), and future research should focus on overcoming these obstacles to optimize SR-AOP systems.

One of the most critical challenges is ensuring the long-term stability and reusability of catalysts. Many catalysts experience degradation or structural changes after multiple cycles, reducing their effectiveness. Developing robust catalysts that maintain high degradation efficiency over extended use is essential for reducing operational costs and ensuring practical feasibility. Furthermore, catalyst leaching poses a significant concern, as the release of active components into treated water can lead to secondary contamination and diminish the catalyst’s active lifespan. Engineering catalysts with anchored active sites or stable support materials can mitigate leaching, enhancing both environmental safety and operational longevity.

Another major challenge lies in the high energy and operational costs associated with SR-AOPs. Current systems often rely on energy-intensive activation methods, such as UV irradiation or heat, which increase the overall cost. However, since the cost of a system depends on a number of variables, including the type of catalysts or activators, necessary equipment, involved mechanisms (i.e., radical and non-radical), water matrix, and the pollutant’s properties (i.e., ionization potential), it might not be possible to make a general statement regarding the costefficiency of SR-AOPs. As a result, the cost of a system can differ for each application, and to the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive cost analysis of an AOP has been reported (Nguyen et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2023; Fernandes et al., 2019; Ike et al., 2018). By all means, identifying low-energy activation strategies, such as solar or visible light-driven systems, or leveraging ambient-temperature activation methods, could significantly improve economic viability and sustainability. Similarly, the formation of toxic by-products during pollutant degradation requires careful attention. Comprehensive studies are needed to identify, monitor, and mitigate harmful intermediates to ensure that treated water is safe for discharge or reuse.

The complexity of real-world water matrices further complicates the practical application of SR-AOPs. Components such as natural organic matter, bicarbonates, and chloride ions can inhibit sulfate radical generation by scavenging radicals or forming less reactive species. Tailoring SR-AOP systems to specific wastewater types—such as industrial effluents, pharmaceutical wastewater, and municipal sewage—is critical for maximizing efficiency under such conditions. Research should explore optimal operational parameters, including pH, temperature, and oxidant dosage, to improve performance across diverse water matrices.

Scaling up SR-AOP systems from laboratory to full-scale applications also presents significant challenges. Ensuring uniform catalyst distribution, addressing mass transfer limitations, and maintaining consistent reaction conditions across larger volumes are essential for reliable scaling. Pilot-scale studies should focus on designing reactors that address these issues, evaluating long-term operational performance under practical conditions. Moreover, LCAs and cost-benefit analyses are necessary to evaluate the environmental and economic impact of SRAOPs, guiding the development of sustainable and cost-effective systems.

To further advance SR-AOPs, research should prioritize the development of more efficient and economical catalysts. Innovations in catalyst design, such as nanostructuring, doping, and the use of biocharbased composites or natural clays, offer promising opportunities for improving catalytic performance while reducing costs. Catalysts with high surface areas and tailored active sites can enhance activity, stability, and reusability, addressing key challenges in practical applications. Additionally, integrating SR-AOPs with other treatment technologies—such as biological treatment, adsorption, or membrane filtration—can broaden their applicability and improve overall efficiency. For instance, SR-AOPs can serve as pre-treatment to enhance the biodegradability of recalcitrant compounds, facilitating subsequent biological treatments.

Finally, the scope of SR-AOPs should be expanded to target a broader range of emerging contaminants, such as endocrine-disrupting chemicals, microplastics, and persistent organic pollutants. Investigating the degradation pathways and mechanisms for diverse pollutants will establish SR-AOPs as a versatile and adaptable technology. Research into energy-efficient activation methods, including hybrid systems that combine ultrasonic or electrochemical activation, can also provide synergistic enhancements in pollutant degradation.

By addressing these challenges and focusing on these research directions, future studies can optimize SR-AOPs for widespread adoption. These advancements will ensure that SR-AOPs evolve into a sustainable and effective solution for global water pollution challenges, facilitating their implementation in real-world water and wastewater treatment systems.

# 11. Conclusion

This study underscores the significant potential of processes SR-AOPs for the degradation of pharmaceutically active compounds, exemplified by the extensive research on ACT degradation. The high reactivity of $\mathrm { { s o } _ { 4 } ^ { - } }$ , generated through the activation of persulfates (PMS and PS), facilitates the effective breakdown of complex organic pollutants such as ACT. Various catalytic systems, including transition metal oxides, carbon-based materials, and composites, have demonstrated varying degrees of success in enhancing ${ \mathrm { 8 O } } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ production and degradation efficiency.

The formation of by-products during ACT degradation necessitates careful monitoring to ensure the safety and effectiveness of SR-AOPs. Identifying and understanding the toxicity of these intermediates is crucial for refining reaction conditions and catalyst designs to minimize harmful by-product formation.

Future research should focus on developing more efficient and stable catalysts, optimizing operational parameters, and integrating SR-AOPs with other water treatment technologies to enhance overall efficiency. Addressing environmental and economic aspects through LCA and costeffectiveness analyses will be vital for the sustainable deployment of SRAOPs.
