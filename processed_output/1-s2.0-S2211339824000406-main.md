# Review Photothermal-mediated advanced oxidation processes for wastewater purification

Fengting He1 , Haijun Chen2 , Jing ${ \mathsf { L } } ^ { 3 }$ , Chaocheng Zhao1 , Jinqiang Zhang4 and Shaobin Wang4

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) stand out as promising solutions for wastewater purification. An integration of a photothermal effect in AOPs will bring about a transformative impact on the processes. This innovative approach will boost treatment efficiency and overcome the limitations of the conventional ones. In this review, we thoroughly investigate the progress of applying a photothermal effect on various AOPs technologies for robust wastewater treatment. Our discussion encompasses the origin of the photothermal effect, elucidation of its working principles, and a summary of strategies for engineering the photothermal effect to catalytically remove contaminants. Furthermore, we delve into the diverse applications of photothermal effect in wastewater treatment technologies, spanning photocatalysis, peroxymonosulfate activation, and the Fenton reaction. Finally, we identify some challenges and outline future prospectives for the implementation of photothermal wastewater treatment. This review is anticipated to rejuvenate conventional AOPs, offering more clean, sustainable, and efficient technologies for wastewater treatment.

# Introduction

The relentless escalation of industrialization and urbanization has precipitated a substantial global energy crisis and environmental quandaries. Water pollution, in particular, poses a severe threat to the sustainable development of human society, necessitating urgent solutions to advanced and sustainable wastewater purification for clean water supply. While advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have proven to be effective technologies for treating industrial wastewater, their stand-alone use exhibits drawbacks such as considerable energy consumption, incomplete mineralization, and potential production of toxic intermediates [1,2]. Despite significant strides in the past decades, the inadequacy of single AOPs to meet industrial demands persists.

Previous studies have indicated that integrating advanced oxidation technology with other energy sources, such as electricity, heat, or light, can augment degradation efficiency and mitigate ecological hazards [3,4]. However, the incorporation of electricity or heat often results in increased energy costs and other pollution. Leveraging solar energy, a renewable and environmentally friendly source, presents a promising avenue for intensifying AOPs at minimal energy expenditure. Since the groundbreaking discovery by Fujishima and Honda in 1972 [5], photocatalysis has garnered significant interdisciplinary attention in the academic community owing to its potential in energy conservation and environmental protection [6]. Thus, combining photocatalytic technology with various AOPs, known as photocatalytic-mediated AOPs, enhances the degradation and mineralization rates of refractory organic compounds in wastewater. Despite recent advancements, the state-of-the-art systems still suffer from low conversion efficiency of solar energy and pollutant degradation efficiency, largely attributed to the inadequate utilization of full-spectrum sunlight, sluggish charge dynamics, and substantial Gibbs free energy associated with chemical reactions [7,8]. Notably, the current photosystem can only response to ultraviolet light $( 5 \% )$ and a small portion of visible light, making it challenging to utilize infrared light $( 5 3 \% )$ [9,10].

Alternatively, a photothermal effect has been proposed to promote solar energy capture, garnering significant attention for curtailing the energy crisis and alleviating environmental issues in the past decade [11,12]. This approach aims to address the limitations of traditional high-energy thermocatalysis, which poses challenges for achieving green transformations aligned with the global goal of carbon neutrality [13]. The integration of the photothermal effect into various catalytic degradation technologies has emerged as a groundbreaking approach for efficient contaminant elimination in wastewater treatment [14,15]. For instance, introducing the photothermal effect into photocatalytic degradation technology has proven to be an effective strategy for utilizing full-spectrum sunlight in wastewater purification [16,17]. While photothermal-mediated AOPs are widely regarded as one of the most promising technologies for practical application in wastewater treatment, a systematic summary is still lacking for its further development. As such, this minireview comprehensively explores the concept of photothermal effect and its application in diverse AOPs systems for wastewater purification, delving into the recent developments, underlying mechanisms, and its potential as a key solution for sustainable water management.

# Photothermal effect

The core of the photothermal effect lies in the generation of heat induced by photons. In this process, the catalyst transforms a portion of the absorbed photons into high-energy hot electrons, releasing heat through nonradiative pathways such as electron–phonon coupling and phonon–phonon coupling. Moreover, the absorbed photons can be directly converted into thermal energy. Photothermal conversion represents a green and pollution-free method, directly transforming solar energy into heat. This conversion enhances charge dynamics in photocatalysis and lowers energy barriers of chemical reactions. Consequently, it has demonstrated the potential to achieve unparalleled performance in various environmental applications.

# Strategies for engineering photothermal effect

Various efforts have been dedicated to enhancing the photothermal effect on reactions, focusing on a diverse range of photothermal materials, including plasmonic metals, semiconductors, and carbon-based materials [18]. Photo-to-thermal conversion primarily relies on the nonradiative relaxation of energetic hot carriers and the absorption of infrared radiation. In fortifying the photothermal effect, several key factors must be considered in the design of catalytic materials for chemical reactions.

# Optimization of light absorption

Photon absorption serves as the initial and crucial step in initiating photo-to-thermal conversion. Full-spectrum light absorption can eventually enhance the excitation of hot electrons and increase nonradiative relaxation. As such, harnessing near-infrared/infrared light for heat conversion while suppressing radiation relaxation is essential. Currently, the light-responsive capability of catalysts is adjustable through the implementation of band control, geometric optimization, and morphological engineering techniques. For instance, constructing ordered–disordered $\mathrm { H N b } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 }$ junction structures realized high solar energy utilization, leading to efficient photoredox and photothermal activation in water purification applications (Figure 1a) [19]. Furthermore, it was discovered that the upconversion of luminescent materials also enhances photon absorption and heat release, facilitating the decomposition of methylene blue (Figure 1b) [20]. Nevertheless, achieving full-spectrum sunlight absorption remains a challenge. Therefore, multiple-stage modification strategies, including morphology engineering, defect modulation, and heterojunction fabrication, seem to be an intriguing direction to realize the absorption of full-spectrum sunlight.

# Enhancement of charge kinetics

Effective photoexcitation of hot carriers not only promotes photo-oxidation and reduction but also converts hot electrons excited by low-energy visible or infrared light into heat. Therefore, improving charge kinetics can effectively increase nonradiative transitions and infrared light absorption for heat generation. Creating heterojunctions is a widely adopted and straightforward approach to improving charge dynamics. For example, Schottky heterojunctions with metal/semiconductor, bimetallic structures, and II/S/Z-scheme heterojunctions constructed by photothermal materials/photocatalytic semiconductors can promote charge carrier separation and increase heat generation (Figure 1c) [21]. It is worth noting that type II heterojunctions facilitate the release of thermal energy through nonradiative pathways while reducing their photoredox ability.

# Thermal storage

Effectively managing heat loss is imperative for enhancing the efficiency of photothermal effect in wastewater treatment. Utilizing photothermal films represents a highly effective approach to minimize diffuse reflection and enhance light absorption. Researchers have demonstrated the construction of photothermal membranes by loading graphene oxide (GO) and multiwalled carbon nanotubes onto a polyvinylidene fluoride substrate, thereby reducing diffuse reflectance of light and improving light absorption, ultimately intensifying photothermal conversion efficiency (Figure 1d) [22]. Additionally, the utilization of 3D graphene-based foam and aerogel has been extensively explored to enhance light–material interaction and medium transportation, leveraging their porous structure to alleviate thermal dissipation in wastewater purification. For instance, the encapsulation of phase-change thermal storage materials into metal-organic frameworks exhibits high photothermal conversion efficiency and water purification efficiency (Figure 1e) [23]. This design is versatile and can be extended to various wastewater treatment applications.

# Photothermal effect in various advanced oxidation process

With the great advancements in designing various photothermal catalytic systems, photothermal effect has exhibited appealing potential in wastewater purification by AOPs. In the following section, we overview and highlight the applications of the photothermal effect in several typical areas of photocatalysis, peroxymonosulfate (PMS) oxidation, and Fenton reaction.

# Photothermal coupling in photocatalysis (photothermal catalysis)

Photothermal catalysis exploits incident light irradiation to concurrently propel both photocatalysis and thermal catalysis. This involves utilizing UV–visible light for photochemical processes and harnessing visible and near-infrared light energy for thermocatalytic processes, thereby maximizing the efficiency of tapping into fullspectrum solar energy and significantly enhancing catalytic activity and selectivity [24,25]. When the oscillation frequency of incident light aligns with that of electrons on the surface of metal nanoparticles or matches the band energy of semiconductors, photons can be absorbed, generating hot carriers (electrons and holes) and releasing thermal energy via radiation and nonradiative transitions, respectively. Till now, photothermal catalysis can be categorized into three types based on the dominant driving forces: Photo-assisted thermal catalysis, thermal-assisted photocatalysis, and photothermal synergistic catalysis (Figure 2a) [26].

Photo-assisted thermal catalysis is primarily powered by thermal energy generated by photoexcited hot carriers. These hot carriers, lacking photocatalytic activity, elevate the localized temperature of the catalyst and necessitate materials with excellent photothermal conversion performance (Figure 2b) [27]. In photothermal coupling catalysis, contributions from photochemical and thermocatalytic processes to photothermal catalytic throughputs co-exist, and the total energy not only facilitates photoexcitation to generate heat carriers but also allows the energy released during the photothermal catalysis to overcome thermodynamic reaction barriers in chemical processes. Ultimately, the collective effects of hot carriers, thermal energy, and their interactions serve as the driving force for the catalytic reaction, surpassing the combined impact of photocatalysis and thermal catalysis (Figure 2c) [10]. Additionally, thermal-assisted photocatalysis is dominated by photoexcited hot carriers, with the heat energy derived from external heating or photothermal conversion, effectively reducing the activation energy of photocatalysis and enhancing the mass transfer rate of photocarriers (Figure 2d,e) [28,29].

Integrating photothermal effects with photocatalysis for the simultaneous conversion of sunlight into both chemical and thermal energy has proven to be an effective strategy for enhancing solar utilization and swiftly eliminating contaminants in sewage water [30,31]. In the absence of external heating, the heat generated by the photothermal effect in the photocatalytic degradation of wastewater is often insufficient to directly activate reactants. Instead, in thermal-assisted photocatalysis, the primary reliance is on enriched energetic carriers generated by direct photoexcitation, with heat primarily contributing to the mass transfer process of charge carriers. A common approach to heighten photothermal effects and enhance photocatalytic degradation involves combining photocatalysts with other photothermal materials to create composite materials or utilizing infrared photocatalysts with exceptional photothermal conversion abilities.

Recently, carbon-based materials (such as activated carbon [AC] and graphene family), transition metal sulfides (e.g. $\mathrm { M o S } _ { 2 }$ ), and composites modified with plasmonic metal nanoparticles have gained widespread attention. These materials generate heat through molecular thermal vibration, nonradiative relaxation, and plasma local heating, intensifying the photothermal effect and improving the photocatalytic degradation of organic compounds in wastewater. For example, Gan et al. [32] designed AC/graphitic carbon nitride $( \mathrm { g } { \mathrm { - C } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } )$ composites to achieve efficient photothermal-assisted degradation of sulfamerazine, attributed to the excellent photothermal effect of AC on the catalyst’s surface and facilitating the transfer of photo-generated carriers. Additionally, Gan et al. [33] prepared a core-shell structure Z-scheme tandem heterostructure of ${ \mathrm { M o S } } _ { 2 }$ with $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 }$ -x to effectively photodegrade 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4- DCP), with the strong photothermal effect induced by ${ \mathrm { M o S } } _ { 2 }$ to improve photocatalytic performance. Furthermore, Gan et al. [34] demonstrated the incorporation of plasmonic silver nanoparticles $( \mathrm { A g } \ \mathrm { N P s } )$ and $\mathrm { A g } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ nanolayers onto $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ for the effective decontamination of organic and antibiotic effluents. The surface plasmon resonance phenomenon exhibited by $\mathrm { A g / A g _ { 2 } O }$ generates highly energetic charge carriers, and the resulting photothermal energy, through nonradiative relaxation pathways, enhances the thermodynamics and kinetics of the reactions. Zhu et al. [28] reported a novel sillenite photocatalyst, $\mathrm { B i } _ { 1 2 } \mathrm { C o O } _ { 2 0 }$ , demonstrating a remarkable response in the near-infrared (NIR) region $( 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ . This photocatalyst exhibits exceptional photothermal activity for the degradation of phenols, primarily attributed to the photothermal effect enhanced by the internal electric field, thereby improving charge separation and transfer. Recently, Ma et al. [35] presented a covalent organic framework complexed with $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ ions in significantly enhanced the photothermal effect for selective degradation of $\beta .$ -lactam antibiotics in wastewater.

While applying the photothermal effect in photocatalytic degradation can address the intrinsic limitation of poor photocatalytic efficiency, the reflective properties of carbon materials and the characteristics of plasmonic metal nanoparticles may influence the absorbability of composite systems, resulting in lower photo-to-thermal efficiency. Therefore, constructing a confined space and reducing the size of nanoparticles can improve light capture efficiency. Thus, integrating photocatalysts with optimized photothermal materials is expected to further enhance photothermal conversion efficiency. Additionally, preparation of high-performance photocatalysts with a full spectral response remains a significant challenge. Designing dual-functional catalysts with both photothermal conversion and catalytic performance is the key to achieving stable and efficient photothermal performance.

# Photothermal catalysis in persulfate activation

Persulfate-based AOPs have found diverse applications in the deep treatment of organic pollutants in water. PMS/peroxydisulfate (PMS/PDS) can be activated through physical means, such as ultraviolet light, thermal energy, or chemical activation with catalysts to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) for pollutant degradation. However, physical activation methods often involve high energy consumption and costs. In contrast, the use of solar-driven photocatalysis for activating PMS/PDS has emerged as a green and efficient alternative, making significant strides in pollutant degradation. It is noteworthy that the photothermal effect induced by nearinfrared light in reaction systems is often overlooked. This heat generation enhances the efficiency of photocatalytic reactions and PMS/PDS activation, playing a crucial role in wastewater treatment.

To date, a series of hybrid nanomaterials have been developed to achieve a synergistic effect between photothermal and photocatalytic activation of PMS, optimizing light utilization, tuning ROS, and promoting reaction rates [36]. For example, GO exhibits a photothermal effect, harnessing near-infrared light to enhance charge separation and molecular collision. Designed GO coupled with $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ composites intensifies the efficiency of photocatalytic PMS activation, expediting the degradation of pollutants such as bisphenol A [37]. Additionally, materials with strong photothermal conversion ability, such as molybdenum disulfide $( \mathrm { M o S } _ { 2 } )$ , can directly serve as activators for heat-activating PDS. ${ \mathrm { M o S } } _ { 2 }$ efficiently converts near-infrared light to heat energy for PDS activation, presenting a light-to-heat-induced strategy for degrading organic contaminants in environmental remediation [38]. To address limitations in heat transfer efficiency, loading ${ \mathrm { M o S } } _ { 2 }$ onto aerosols creates a three-dimensional tunnel structure, significantly improving heat transfer rates and insulating effects. This approach substantially enhances the efficiency of photocatalytic and photothermal activation of PMS, facilitating the removal of 2,4-DCP.

# Photothermal Fenton reaction

Photothermal enhancement in the heterogeneous Fenton reaction holds significant promise for organic wastewater treatment, attributed to its simple operation and mild reaction conditions. However, challenges such as low activation efficiency and excessive $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ consumption have hindered its widespread practical application [39]. The integration of the photothermal effect into Fenton/Fenton-like reactions proves to be a transformative approach, addressing these limitations and enhancing the overall efficacy of wastewater treatment [18,40].

For example, Li et al. employed NIR light–driven bimetallic PtCu alloy nanocages to propel photothermaldriven heterogeneous Fenton for the degradation of dyeing pollutants [27]. The infrared-driven photothermal effect of PtCu-NCs not only augmented the generation rate of •OH but also reduced the reaction energy barrier. Moreover, other positive outcomes of photothermal effects have been observed in modified Fenton-like systems using various techniques, such as light, electricity, and ultrasound [41]. The application of the photothermal effect has been demonstrated to enhance the photo-Fenton degradation of dye- and antibiotic-containing wastewater, diminishing the reaction energy barrier, facilitating the separation and transport of photo-generated electrons, and augmenting the production of stimulated $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and $\bullet \mathrm { O H }$ radicals [42,43]. Furthermore, the photothermal effect plays a crucial role in promoting the electro-Fenton degradation of pollutants by boosting the production of $\bullet \mathrm { O H }$ radicals and accelerating the associated reaction kinetics [44].

# Conclusion and future prospects

The incorporation of the photothermal effect into catalytic systems has revolutionized wastewater purification, offering a sustainable, efficient, and environmentally friendly approach to address water pollution challenges. As the research progresses and technology evolves, photothermal catalysis is poised to play a pivotal role in shaping the future of wastewater treatment, ensuring cleaner and safer water resources for generations to come. Nevertheless, endeavors are still needed to realize the industrialization of photothermal-mediated AOP technologies, and more investigations are suggested below.

a. Catalyst design: The simultaneous achievement of efficient high heat conversion and catalytic degradation poses a critical challenge in photothermal-assisted AOPs for wastewater treatment. Innovative strategies, such as encapsulation and surface modification, are under exploration to enhance their overall efficiency. In addition, machine learning is a powerful technique for catalyst design and screening, which can be widely employed in future studies for catalyst development.

b. Heat loss optimization: Effective catalyst design and reaction optimization, including the use of thermal insulator substrates, photothermal films, and materials or coatings for reflecting or absorbing infrared radiation, are essential for maximizing the efficiency of photothermal catalytic systems in real-world applications.

c. Thermal storage: Incorporating heat storage materials, which combine phase-change materials with photothermal catalysts, address the issue of intermittent sunlight. This approach is anticipated to successfully translate laboratory observations into practical applications within wastewater treatment plants.
