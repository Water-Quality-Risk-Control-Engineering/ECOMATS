Intensive research has been applied to photoelectrocatalysis (PEC) for water/wastewater treatment; however, the scale-up & automation of these systems hasn't been examined. This is a critical step if PEC is to be a viable solution for water/wastewater treatment. In this work, a PEC system has been scaled up, holding a treating volume of $0 . 8 5 \mathrm { L }$ in automated batch runs. Expanded titanium mesh was anodised to form titania nanotubes on the surface and was used as the photoanode with a length of $9 5 0 \mathrm { m m }$ , with 3 meshes in concentric packing. The largest diameter was $2 8 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ giving an estimated geometric surface area of $9 2 9 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ . This work is a proof-ofprinciple using the charge (coulombs) passed to solution at a fixed potential to establish the required time for treatment. To ensure effective treatment, a minimum threshold current $\mathrm { ( > 1 . 5 ~ m A ) }$ ) is used for calculating the total charge, this prevents any dark current from being summed should the irradiation or power turn off, and a 1.5 factor-of-safety is also used. When the system was operated in an automated mode with the addition of 0.3 mM of peroxymonosulfate, it achieved a ${ > } 5$ log reduction in antibiotic resistance $E$ . coli in synthetic wastewater.

# 1. Introduction

Effective water and wastewater treatment is essential for human health and environmental protection. However, the emergence of new pollutants, as well as the increase in microorganism resistance to traditional treatment methods, results increased challenges [1,2]. To address these challenges, new water treatment technologies have been investigated; in particular, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs). AOPs include a wide range of technologies, for example: ozone-based [3]; electrochemical [4]; Fenton-like process [5]; combined UVC with peroxide/ozone [3,6]; photocatalysis [7]; and photoelectrocatalysis (PEC) [8,9]. Regardless of the AOP technology that is used/examined, the objective is to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS); primarily hydroxyl radicals (•OH); a highly oxidative radical $( 2 . 8 \mathrm { e V } )$ second only to fluorine at $( 2 . 8 7 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ [10]. However, some processes may produce other ROS that are capable of attacking and degrading a range of contaminants; such as: superoxide radical anion $\left( \mathbf { \cdot } _ { \mathbf { 0 } _ { 2 } ^ { - } } \right)$ [11]; hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ [4]. Also, sulfate radicals may be generated in the presence of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) or peroxydisulfate (PDS) [12].

Photoelectrocatalysis (PEC) generates ROS by using at least one semiconducting electrode (commonly an n-type photoanode), which can absorb irradiation, provided the energy of the photon is greater than the band gap of the semiconductor [11]. When this occurs, an electron-hole pair is produced. The hole can directly oxidise the contaminant or indirectly via oxidation of water to produce • OH. An externally applied electrical bias between the electrodes helps prevent recombination of the electron-hole pair and drives photogenerated electrons to the counter electrode, where they can directly electrochemically reduce the contaminant or indirectly via the reduction of molecular oxygen to $\cdot 0 _ { 2 } ^ { - }$ and/or $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ [8]. PEC has the advantage of being effective against a wide range of contaminants, including microorganisms, and organic & inorganic compounds. However, most studies are based on small labscale experiments [13] with only a few PEC systems reported to have been scaled up [8,14,15], and none have been reported to be automated. For deployment in real scenarios, automated control is necessary to ensure effective treatment. This is challenging as if solar driven the irradiation intensity will vary over time, there is the potential for electrode fouling, and there may be fluctuations in the composition of the water matrix when operated in a continuous mode.

This work provides a proof-of-principle on the automation and feedback control based on irradiation intensity for a PEC reactor. The feedback control is implemented using the electrical current at a fixed cell potential as a means of establishing the required treatment time for the inactivation of antibiotic-resistant E. coli in synthetic wastewater.

Monitoring and control of the treatment process is an important step in making PEC a viable water treatment technology in the future.

# 2.2. Reactor configuration and automation

The reactor configuration (Fig. 1) is a scaled-up (1 m) and modified version of our previously reported PEC reactor [14]. The photoanode is a self-ordered array of titanium dioxide nanotubes (TiNT) produced via anodisation of expanded titanium mesh (method described in Section 2.3). The expanded meshes were rolled into cylinders (before anodisation) with a length of $9 5 0 \mathrm { m m }$ and diameters of $2 8 \mathrm { m m }$ , 22 mm & 15 mm and combined to maximise photon absorption. A stainless steel 316 rod $\operatorname { 5 } \operatorname { m m }$ diameter) was placed in the centre of the reactor and used as the cathode. A compound parabolic collector (CPC) was used to increase the reflection of irradiation from the lamp around the circumference of the tube. The reactor was constantly aerated to enable mixing within the reactor and after initial testing, the reactor was posited to $9 0 ^ { \mathrm { o } }$ to enable better mixing. The total volume of the reactor was $8 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ and it was irradiated with a fluorescent UVA lamp (Schiefer UVA 4 ft T8 36W) with an average intensity of $1 6 . 8 \mathrm { W } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ . The intensity is lower compared to other reports some of which are as high as $4 4 8 \mathrm { W } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 }$ [16].

The intensity of the lamp was measured using a spectrometer (OceanOptics) at equally spaced locations over the area of the lamp, the intensity of the lamp was then calculated at each point via trapezium integration using Matlab. The individual values at each point were then used to generate a map of the irradiation with cubic interpolation to give a more precise estimate of the average intensity (Fig. 2).

The PEC reactor was powered by a benchtop power supply (RS3005D) and the PEC control system was automated using an Arduino Uno microcontroller connected to a computer, to power the microcontroller and record the data via the open-source serial console, “PuTTY”. The pump and solenoid valve were powered by a main to DC $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ adaptor and switched on when required via a relay switch. A process diagram for the control system is shown in Fig. 3a and a diagram of the control box in Fig. 3b (photograph shown in Fig. S2). The code, components and wiring required for the control system are available in the supplementary information (Fig. S1).

The current was measured using an INA219 module and recorded each second (provided the current was $> 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { m A }$ to prevent any dark current being measured) and summed to calculate the total charge passed to the solution i.e coulombs. To establish the required total charge passed or coulomb value for automation, batch tests were performed for the inactivation of antibiotic resistance E. coli in synthetic wastewater (SWW), as stated in Section 2.4. The inclusion and activation of potassium peroxymonosulfate (Oxone) was also examined (1.0, 0.5, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m M } )$ . The required charge under the optimal condition was 66.2C. A safety factor 1.5 was used (resulting in a target charge of 99.3C) during the automated runs to ensure treatment was effective. The system was operated in a sequential batch mode. Once the required total charge was passed for one run, a solenoid valve was opened to remove the treated water and then closed again. A pump was then operated to fill the reactor, and the sum of the current measurement was then re-set.

# 2.3. Anodisation of titanium mesh

The anodisation was conducted as per previous work [14]; to summarise, expanded titanium mesh was first cut to size and rolled into cylinders with a length of $9 5 0 \mathrm { m m }$ and diameters of $2 8 \mathrm { m m }$ , $2 2 \mathrm { m m } \& 1 5$ mm. The expanded titanium mesh was then anodised in a 2-electrode set-up, in which titanium mesh was used as both the anode (the working electrode where the nanotube formation takes places) and a larger mesh around the working electrode as the cathode. The applied potential was $+ 3 0 . 0 \mathrm { V }$ for $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ in a solution $_ { ( \sim 9 \mathrm { L } ) }$ of ethylene glycol $( 9 7 \% \nu /$ v), ultra pure water $( 3 \% \mathrm { v / v } )$ and $\mathrm { I H } _ { 4 } ( 0 . 3 \% w / \nu )$ which was conducted in a PVC tube with a diameter of $1 1 0 \ \mathrm { m m }$ and length of $1 0 0 0 \mathrm { m m }$ . The samples were then annealed at $4 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ with a ramping up/down rate of $2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } { \cdot } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . A scanning electron microscope (Hitachi SU5000 FESEM) was used to confirm and analyse the self-ordered array of TiNT.

The following was also added but due to the small qualities required, a concentrated stock solution was produced and the required dilution made to ensure that the final SWW matrix contained: KCl $( 4 \ \mathrm { m g { \cdot } L } ^ { - 1 } )$ ; beef extract $( 1 . 8 \ \mathrm { m g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ ); peptone (2.7 $\mathbf { m } \mathbf { g } { \cdot } \mathbf { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ ; humic acid $( 4 . 2 \ \mathrm { m g { \cdot } L ^ { - 1 } } )$ ; tannic acid $( 4 . 2 \ \mathrm { m g { \cdot } L ^ { - 1 } } )$ ; lignosulfonic acid sodium salt $( 2 . 4 ~ \mathrm { \ m g { \cdot } L ^ { - 1 } } )$ ; sodium lauryl sulfate (0.9 $\mathbf { m } \mathbf { g } { \cdot } \mathbf { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ ; gum arabic from acacia tree $( 4 . 7 ~ \mathrm { m g { \cdot L } ^ { - 1 } } )$ ; and $\mathrm { K _ { 2 } H P O _ { 4 } }$ (7.0 $\mathbf { m } \mathbf { g } { \cdot } \mathbf { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ .

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Nanotube formation

The nanotube formation was examined at the bottom, middle and top of the mesh; however, regardless of location, nanotube formation occurred with similar lengths $( 2 . 5 \mathrm { - } 3 . 5 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ and diameters $( 8 2 . 3 \pm 9 . 4 $ nm); as shown in Fig. 4. The length and diameter are comparable to other reports under similar conditions [11,14,19], though these reports are at smaller scales. Reports on the scaled-up TiNT electrodes are limited [20], and more commonly evaluated on planar (foil) geometry with sizes up to $6 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ being reported [21]. Cylindrical geometry electrodes are less commonly reported; however, there have been some reports up to $1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { m m }$ and a diameter of $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ $( \sim 3 1 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ [22]. In this work, the largest electrode had an estimated geometric surface area was ${ \sim } 9 2 9 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ , calculated based on the opening area and thickness of the mesh.

Although there were nanotubes at all points in the mesh, it was not completely uniform as shown in Fig. 5. The lack of uniform tubes at all locations via anodisation of titanium has also been reported by Szkoda et al. [21]. In their work, the highest geometric surface area anodised was $6 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ and lacked an ordered tube structure at all points. It is therefore not surprising that with a geometric surface area of ${ \sim } 9 2 9 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ that there are areas with unordered tubes. It is also important to highlight the danger of cherry-picking micrographs in literature [23] and thus some reporting on the anodisation of titanium nanotubes may not provide a full representation of morphology and neglect variability across the surface. It is, of course, not feasible to map full the nanostructure morphology across the full macro-scale substrate; however, when scanning different areas, it was observed that the morphology is variable, with three distinct regions: areas with tubes, a transition region with tubes and broken tubes and areas of mostly collapsed tubes. This could be due to the geometric shape of the mesh and the direction of the local electric field when anodising results in disorganised tube growth in the gaps of the mesh [24]. Also, there is the possible heating due to the Joule effect during the anodisation, which may affect the tube growth; as such, future large-scale anodisation of titanium would benefit from recirculating the electrolyte with an external reservoir tank to reduce heating [20] and the monitoring of the fluoride concentration and replenishment to ensure optimal fluoride for tube formation. It would be near impossible to design a counter electrode to conform to the geometric shape of the mesh.

# 3.2. Current response

The current response of the system was first examined via linear swept voltammetry using a potentiostat at a rate of $5 \mathrm { m } { \cdot } \mathrm { V } { \cdot } s ^ { - 1 }$ . This was performed in $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } S { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ (Fig. 6a) and SWW (Fig. 6b), though there was a small $0 . 6 7 \ \mathrm { m A }$ difference in the photocurrent generated at $+ 2 . 0$ $\mathrm { \Delta V _ { c e l l } }$ . The inclusion of the CPC resulted in a doubling of the current at $+ 2 . 0 \mathrm { V _ { c e l l } , }$ due to the increased irradiation directed into the reactor and at higher biases, there is reduced recombination. The current response was also examined using the current sensor (INA219) and a fixed potential applied using a benchtop power supply $( + 2 . 0 \ \mathrm { V _ { c e l l } }$ , $+ 2 . 5 \ \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { c e l l } } ,$ $+ 3 . 0 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { c e l l } } )$ , the electrolyte was SWW (Fig. 6c); as expected the increased voltage resulted in higher current; however, the higher voltage/current did not relate to better performance (see Section 3.3). The current and the summed data (total charge/coulombs) from the Arduino at fixed potential are plotted in Fig. 6d, showing that the sum function programmed on the Arduino is working accordingly. A close-up at the start of the measurement (Fig. 6e) shows that when the potential is applied, there is a spike in the current due to capacitance charging at the interface, but it starts to decay to a steady state [25]. When the UV lamp is turned on, the anodic current increases due to photogeneration of electron-hole pairs. The current continues to rise as the fluorescent lamp heats up until it reaches its maximum photon flux. There is a small decrease in the photocurrent over extended time. When the UV lamp is turned off, the anodic current drops to under $2 \mathtt { m A }$ (Fig. 6f). When the applied bias is removed, there is a sharp negative current as electrons and holes recombine and the fermi energy levels of the electrodes reach equilibrium at short circuit [26].

# 3.3. Initial optimisation experiments

The system was first tested in batch runs to establish the optimal conditions, with Oxone to improve the reactors' performance due to the low irradiation of the lamp. As Oxone is an oxidant, it is capable of inactivating the ABR E. coli; as shown in the Fig. 7a under dark conditions. This was performed as a control experiment to examine if the PEC system improved the rate of inactivation. Fig. 7b displays the inactivation of ABR E. coli in SWW for the different concentrations of Oxone while under irradiation and with an applied bias of $+ 2 . 0 \mathrm { V _ { c e l l } }$ . Due to the low irradiation, there is a limited reduction in the bacteria with no Oxone; however, with Oxone there is an increase in the reductions compared to the dark control experiments.

A concentration of $0 . 3 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ was selected for further control experiments (Fig. 8a), as it displayed a good improvement with PEC compared to the dark and one wants to limit the addition of consumable chemicals. The control experiments demonstrated that photocatalysis had a 2.18 log reduction which is an improvement compared to the dark $\left( 1 . 4 \log \right.$ reduction), the reduction is lower than expected due to the low irradiation; as well, unirradiated areas caused by the mesh allows for the bacteria to be shielded for UV [27] and TiNT does not perform well as a photocatalyst, compared to P25 but is significantly better for PEC [28]. With the only UV irradiation i.e. no mesh, the reduction is higher than with photocatalysis (2.71 log reduction), further indicating that the mesh is causing some dark areas that enable the bacteria to be protected, resulting in low reduction levels. For electrochemical i.e. bias and no irradiation, the reduction is greater than photocatalysis with a $2 . 6 4 \log$ reduction, which is also within the error of the UV irradiation only result, which is uncommon in PEC [11,28,29]; however, as the electrochemical control experiment was conducted at $+ 2 . 0 \ \mathrm { V _ { c e l l } }$ which is past the breakdown voltage of the material (Fig. 6b), an average dark current of $0 . 5 6 \mathrm { \ m A }$ was observed and thus possible electrochemical activation of PMS. The work Fu et al. [30] examined the electrochemical activation of PMS using a carbon cloth as an anode; while Tanos et al. [31] used a metal oxide anode; and Deng et al. using a stainless-steel cathode [32]. The reduction could also be attributed to electromigration of bacteria [33] and other ROS produced at the stainless-steel cathode.

The best performance was with the PEC achieving a $3 . 6 2 \ 1 0 g$ reduction, when applying different biases with $0 . 3 \mathrm { m M }$ Oxone (Fig. 8b) it was observed that at $+ 2 . 5 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { c e l l } }$ was the optimal bias, achieving a 5.23 log reduction; however, applying a high bias of $+ 3 . 0 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { c e l l } }$ the performance was reduced (2.81 log reduction), this may be attributed to undesirable water splitting yielding oxygen at the anode which also hinders the mass transport of bacteria to the electrode surface due to gas evolution [34]. The enhancement observed when using PEC with the optimal bias for water treatment has been previously examined [11,14] by evaluating the mechanism/radicals generated using TiNT for PEC water treatment and found there was a complex reaction system involving the generation of OH radical, superoxide radical anion and chlorine (in the presence of chloride) at the anode, and hydrogen peroxide at the cathode (carbon electrode). However, in this work the system is much more complicated as synthetic wastewater is used as the electrolyte which contains a complex mix of organic and inorganic compounds, including chloride. Also, Oxone was added to enhance the disinfection efficiency. Others have reported on the electrochemical and photoelectrochemical generation of sulphate radical from PMS [30–32,35] either anodically or cathodically. Lim et al. [35] performed a detailed study of electrochemical activation of PMS on nitrogen doped carbon nanotubes. They reported a complex interplay between $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ generation and PMS activation, as well as a locally increased pH near the electrode due to the oxygen reduction reaction. Overall, this resulted in a $S 0 4 ^ { - } / { \cdot } 0 \mathrm { H }$ mixed oxidation environment that facilitated pollutant degradation. They also confirmed $s 0 4 \cdot ^ { - }$ and •OH generation by in-situ EPR. There are a large number of different reactions which may take place at the anode and cathode to generate reactive species whether it is ROS, reactive chlorine species or sulphate radicals, which will lead to further reactions with the reactive species themselves, the compounds in the synthetic wastewater, and the bacteria. Some of the key oxidation and reduction reactions that may take place in a PEC system with oxygen, chloride and PMS present are given in Table 1. Overall, the generation of reactive oxygen species, including $s 0 4 ^ { \bullet ^ { - } }$ , and chlorine, will lead to effective inactivation of bacteria due to attack on the cell wall and membrane leading to irreversible damage. The mechanism of bacterial inactivation by PEC and sulphate radicals has been reported previously [36,37].

# 3.4. Automated operation

The previous experiments demonstrated that $+ 2 . 5 \mathrm { \ V _ { c e l l } }$ was the optimal potential and required a charge of $6 6 . 2 \textrm { C }$ to reach achieve bacterial inactivation to the detection limit. To add extra safety into the control system, the required charge was set to $9 9 . 3 \mathrm { ~ C ~ }$ i.e. with a 1.5 factor of safety. The automated system was run for 4 batches, with batch $2 \ \& \ 3$ being subjected to additional operational condition tests (irradiation turned off, irradiation varied & power turned off) to stress test the system, and samples were taken at the start and end to calculate the change in the concentration of bacteria. In all runs, the detection limit (DL) of 1 log was reached with a starting concentration of ${ > } 6$ log i.e. ${ > } 5$ log reduction after each discharge, thus provide a high level of protection. The current and total charge from the microcontroller were recorded (Fig. 9). A summary of the conditions for each batch is stated in Table 2. As the required charge is set and operates under a fixed potential, the required energy to power the electrodes is fixed at $2 4 8 . 3 \mathrm { ~ J ~ }$ which equates to $6 . 8 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } { \mathrm { k W h } }$ . However, the 10 W pump requires 1 $\mathrm { k J } / 2 . 7 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { k W h }$ ; while the Lamp (36 W) & and Arduino Uno (1.04 W [38]) energy requirement will vary due to the lamp & Arduino being on for different periods of time per batch, the resulting total energy requirement is stated in Table 2. The highest energy requirement was batch 3 with $0 . 0 8 7 4 ~ \mathrm { k W h }$ , due to the longer time required to treat as additional test condition experiments were conducted the cost of the electrical usage will vary depending on where it is operated due regional difference in electrical energy pricing [39]. Most of the electrical energy usage was the lamp with ${ \sim } 9 7 ~ \%$ , as such more energy efficient irradiation is required to reduce the electrical cost e.g. the use of UV LEDs and/or the utilisation of solar irradiation.

In batches 1 & 4 it was observed that the total charge increased as expected with a stable current. Once the required charge is achieved, the solenoid opens to drain the treated water, and the sum is reset to 0. In batch 2, the irradiation was turned off to examine the impact, it was observed that as the current drops below the threshold $( 1 . 5 \mathrm { m A } )$ the total charge doesn't change and stays stationary (as programmed). When the irradiation is turned on, the current spikes above $1 . 5 \mathrm { m A }$ the total charge sum continues to rise. In batch 3, the irradiation was reduced (Fig. 10) by moving the UV lamp; and as photocurrent is directly proportional to the irradiation at low intensities, the current is reduced proportionally to the UV intensity [11,14], and the current changes accordingly.

When the power is turned off, the current displays a typical n-type photoanode response as previously explained in Section 3.2, again due to the threshold in the code, the sum of the total charge does not change or reduce due to the negative value. Overall, this added extra safety to the system to ensure adequate treatment. However, when the power is switched off while still under irradiation, a negative current $\left( - 1 . 8 6 { \mathrm { m A } } \right)$ is observed which continues to decay towards zero. This negative current is due to the Fermi energy levels of the electrodes equilibrating under short circuit conditions where electrons accumulated at the cathode recombine with holes at the anode (negative flow of electrons). As $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ is n-type semiconductor it should rectify current anodically, even under short circuit conditions; however the NTs are defective meaning critical band bending is large and quite positive potentials are required to maximise the photocurrent response. If the reactor was deployed in the real world a failsafe would be built in to shut down the reactor in case of power supply failure.

# 4. Conclusion

In this work, we have developed an automated and scaled-up photoelectrochemical system for wastewater treatment which was tested for the inactivation of antibiotic resistance $E$ . coli synthetic wastewater. The system achieved a ${ > } 5$ log reduction when operated in the automated mode, with the addition of peroxymonosulfate (Oxone). The automated system worked on calculating the total charge/coulombs that was passed to the solution at a fixed potential $( + 2 . 5 \mathrm { V _ { c e l l } } )$ . To ensure effective treatment, a factor-of-safety was used (1.5), the sum function only summed data that was greater than $> 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { m A }$ , this prevented any dark current/negative current from being included in the total charge during operation. As the study is primarily a proof-of-principle, there are still improvements that can be made to the system, which include higher power LED UV irradiation or solar irradiation, different cathode materials, evaluation of different pollutants, improvement in the anodisation of large scale titanium electrodes, inclusion of other sensors for quality control, and testing with real secondary wastewater. Overall, this work is a step forward towards automated control of photoelectrocatalysis for wastewater treatment.
