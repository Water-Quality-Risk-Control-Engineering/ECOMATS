# Rapid Synthesis and Recycling of Carbonized Wood Catalyst Decorated with Co Nanoparticles for High-Efficiency Degradation of Rhodamine B

Pengyu Xing, Yaoxing Wang, Xueqin Fan, Xueqi Li, Peiru Wang, Quankuo Zhang, Qiuyu Du, Yanjun Xie, Ran Yin,\* and Wentao Gan\*

Developing catalysts for purifying organic pollutants in wastewater is significant for environmental remediation. Herein, a high-temperature (1000 K) thermal shock (HTS) method is employed to rapidly synthesize a self-supporting catalyst consisting of cobalt (Co) nanoparticles (NPs) and carbonized wood (CW) within 2 s. Thanks to the porous structure of wood and uniformly distributed Co NPs, the wood catalyst exhibits excellent catalytic activity, achieving over $99 \%$ removal of $2 5 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ Rhodamine B (RhB) within $1 0 m i n$ using the activated peroxymonosulfate advanced oxidation processes, while the leaching rate of Co ions is less than $1 \ m g L ^ { - 1 }$ . As a proof of concept, a continuous filter is constructed based on the CW catalysts for efficient RhB degradation in flowing water. With a flow rate of $2 0 m L \min ^ { - 1 }$ , it maintains the removal of over $90 \%$ of RhB from wastewater for a duration of $^ { \textrm { \scriptsize 2 h } }$ .

Benefiting from the rapid synthesis of HTS method, a "deactivation-regeneration" strategy is proposed that extends the recycling cycles of wood catalysts to 20 times, surpassing most reported Co-based catalysts. Derived from renewable wood materials, this catalyst offers advantages of high efficiency, low cost, simple synthesis, and durability, showing great potential for application in various fields including water treatment, electrocatalytic conversion, and energy storage.

# 1. Introduction

The pollution of water environments and destruction of aquatic ecosystems, caused by high-concentration emissions of the toxic substances and degradation byproducts in dyes (e.g., rhodamine B, methyl orange, safranine T, and crystal violet), pose severe threats to the safety of the livable planet.[1,2] Efficient water treatments significantly contribute to the sustainable development of human society. A variety of techniques have been developed to adsorb and degrade water contaminants, such as physical adsorption,[3] ion exchanges,[4] membrane filtration,[5] photocatalytic degradation,[6] and advanced oxidation processes (AOPs).[7-9] Among them, the AOPs from peroxymonosulfate (PMS) are considered the effective wastewater treatment methods due to their fast reaction kinetics, strong oxidative potential, operational simplicity, and non-selectivity.[10] Typically, noble metal catalysts, such as Pt, Pd, and Ru, are employed for PMS activation, which can generate highly reactive species (e.g., OH, $0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 } )$ ,and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - 1 }$ during the non-homogeneous catalysis. However, their high cost and scarcity hinder large-scale applications.

Transition metal catalysts, such as Co,[11] Fe,[12] Ni,[13] and Mn,[14] are the substitutable choice in current AOPs due to their abundance and cost-effectiveness. Cobalt (Co) ions exhibit the strong activation effect on PMS, promoting the generation of radicals to accelerate the degradation of organic pollutants.[15] Immobilizing Co catalysts on the carbon materials with large specific surface area can concurrently boost the catalytic activities and resilience against variations in water complexity and environmental conditions. Recently, various cobalt-based catalysts have been prepared through hybridizing carbon substrates and Co nanoparticles (NPs), such as Co-N/carbon fibers,[11] Co-porous N-doped graphene,[6] Co/defective carbon/MOF,[17] which not only effectively prevent the leaching of Co NPs through metal-carrier interactions, but also generate a unique charges distribution to improve the catalytic performance.

As an abundant, renewable, and carbon-rich biomass material, wood possesses the hierarchical porous structure with numerous vertically aligned channels, making it a natural filter that can facilitate the transportation of liquids and the impregnation of metal precursor solutions. Additionally, the wood cell walls contain abundant electronegative hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, which can serve as active anchoring sites for coordinating with metal precursors. Various functionalization and modification methods have been proposed to prepare wood-based materials in the field of water treatment. For instance, a 3D mesoporous wood membrane modified with Pd NPs is used for efficiently catalytic degradation of methylene blue. This method fully utilizes the synergistic effects of Pd NPs and the porous structure of natural wood, thus enabling the high-throughput water filtration.[18] Furthermore, carbonization is another effective approach that enhances the adsorption capacity for solute molecules in water by increasing the specific surface area of wood-based materials.[19] Various active carbonized materials derived from wood powder, wood fiber, and bulk wood are used for the adsorption and catalytic degradation of organic pollutants (i.e., dyes, antibiotics, pesticides) in wastewater.[7,18,20] However, these carbonized wood-based materials often suffer from low atomic utilization and insufficient durability due to the poor interface compatibility between nano-catalysts and carbonaceous substrates, highlighting a gap from catalysts preparation and practical production. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop highly efficient and long-lasting catalysts for continuous water treatment.

Herein, we developed a rapid and energy-efficient hightemperature thermal shock (HTS) method to in-situ nucleate Co NPs on the carbonized wood (CW) surface, which can serve as the efficient, stable, and recyclable catalyst for RhB degradation (Figure 1a). The HTS at $1 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { K }$ provides sufficient kinetic energy for the Co salts to migrate and anchor on the CW. Followed quenching and crystallization results in the formation of uniformly distributed Co NPs. The entire heating process takes approximately 2 s (Figure 1b and Figure S1, Supporting Information). Compared to the conventional pyrolysis method, HTS not only significantly reduces both time and energy consumption, but also addresses issues of nonuniform nanoparticle agglomeration (Figure 1c). As a result, the RhB degradation kinetic constant of $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ is $0 . 5 5 6 3 \mathrm { \ m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , which is 1.79 times higher than that of conventional pyrolysis method $( 0 . 3 1 0 3 \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ . More importantly, the recycling issues caused by the occupation of intermediate products during the RhB degradation can be solved by a re-shocking process. Thanks to the rapid HTS processing, we can revive the catalyst activity by re-shocking the deactivated $\operatorname { C o } \ @ \operatorname { C W }$ at $1 9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { K }$ The degradation intermediates attached on the surface of deactivated $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ are carbonized into graphite carbon layers, facilitating electron transfer during the RhB degradation. Therefore, the Co-based catalysts prepared using HTS technology demonstrate good recyclability, achieving 20 cycles with consistent degradation efficiency exceeding $90 \%$ . Drawing on sustainable wood, our approach may offer a "two birds with one stone" strategy to boost both degradation efficiency and cyclic stability of composite catalysts in the field of water treatment.

# 2.2. RhB Degradation of Co@CW/PMS System

A series of catalytic degradation experiments are performed to evaluate the catalytic performance of as-prepared samples using activated PMS for the RhB degradation. Increasing the catalyst and PMS dosage improves the removal ratio of RhB due to the boosted active species from the enhanced activation of PMS (Figure S7a,b, Supporting Information). Additionally, a blank control experiment is conducted, revealing that $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ alone achieved a RhB adsorption degradation efficiency of $2 . 2 8 \%$ within $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ , while PMS alone achieved $6 . 5 4 \%$ .These results confirm that the degradation process relies on the synergistic effect of the catalyst and PMS. However, increasing the catalyst dosage to $4 \mathrm { g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ does not significantly enhance the removal ratio and even leads to a slight decline. With $1 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { \bf { u } \ P M S }$ , the high concentration of generated free radicals results in their mutual interaction and binding, forming stable molecules or non-free radical substances. This process effectively slows or halts the reaction, causing a slight decrease in degradation efficiency.[29] Therefore, $2 \mathrm { g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ of catalyst dosage and $0 . 5 \mathrm { m m }$ of PMS dosage are chosen for the following tests.

# 2.3. Reactive Species and Activation Mechanism in the Co@CW/PMS System

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and quenching experiments are used to determinate the primary reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsible for RhB degradation via PMS activation by $\mathsf { C o } @ \mathsf { C W } \cdot 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ Using tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) to quench •OH, methanol (MeOH) to quench $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , pbenzoquinone (p-BQ) to quench $0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , and histidine (His) to quench ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ allow for the assessment of ROS contributions to RhB degradation.[11] As shown in Figure 3c, the addition of methanol decreases RhB degradation to $8 2 \%$ within 10 minutes, while the impact of TBA on the overall reaction is negligible. Therefore, •OH contributes minimally to the $\operatorname { C o } @ \operatorname { C W } / \operatorname { P M S }$ system, while $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ plays a supporting role. The subsequent introduction of His and p-BQ reduces RhB degradation efficiency by $9 5 . 2 6 \%$ and $5 2 \%$ within $1 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ , respectively, indicating that both ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ are involved in the $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } . 1 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { K / P M S }$ system for RhB degradation, and ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ plays the key role. # RhB Degradation Pathway

The decrease in degradation efficiency during cyclic degradation testing is mainly attributed to two factors: the accumulation of degradation intermediates on the catalyst surface and the depletion of active metal sites.[17] These factors impede the reaction progress. To gain insight into the potential degradation pathways of RhB, we employed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis to examine the intermediates generated during the reaction process. The LC-MS spectra, chemical structures, and molecular weights of the intermediates are shown in Figures S13S22 (Supporting Information). The four main degradation processes for RhB are N-de-ethylation, chromophore structural cleavage, ring-opening, and mineralization (Scheme 1).[11,17] Various ROS firstly attack RhB molecules in the $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0$ K/PMS system, producing multiple de-ethylated intermediates. These large molecular intermediates then undergo oxidative destruction of the chromophore structure between anthracene and benzene, forming smaller molecules containing aromatic rings. Next, an opening process occurs where these aromatic rings break down into short-chain alkenes and low-molecular weight fatty acids. Finally, these compounds decompose into alcohols, amines, organic acids, and simple molecules like $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ through further mineralization. The final degree of mineralization is evidenced by the total organic carbon (TOC) test results, showing a 1-hour removal efficiency ${ \approx } 6 7 \%$ (Figure S23, Supporting Information). The high TOC removal rate stems from the high oxidation capacity of the PMS-based AOPs system. Our research demonstrates that a significant number of intermediate products generated during the RhB degradation process adsorb onto the catalyst surface, hindering the progress of cyclic reactions. Removing the surface-bound intermediates is an effective way to restore the catalyst activity for subsequent cycles.

# 2.5. Recycling and Regeneration of Co@CW

We proposed a cyclic "deactivation-regeneration" strategy for $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ regeneration and recycling utilization. The reactive intermediates loaded on the deactivated $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ can be thermally degraded into carbon layers when subjected to an HTS process at $1 9 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ for 2 s (Figure 4a). The newly formed carbon layers facilitate enhanced electron transfer between Co NPs and PMS system,[36] thus enabling efficient and convenient reactivation of $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ Notably, regenerating the catalyst at temperatures below 1900 K did not produce regular carbon layers on the surface of Co NPs. High temperature promotes graphitization, while lower temperatures lack the kinetics energy needed for crystalline graphite formation, which renders the $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } .$ $1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ unable to regain its original catalytic abilities.[27,37]

The SEM images in Figure 4b-d clearly depict the morphological changes of Co NPs deposited on CW. Initially, the Co NPs exhibit a smooth spherical structure with exposed active sites. After 10 cycles of use, they changed into larger nano-flower structures with reduced activity due to the adsorption of degradation intermediates. Following high-temperature regeneration at 1900 K, these Co NPs regained their activity and returned to smooth spherical shapes. At the same time, the high temperature provided sufficient kinetic energy for Co NPs to undergo melting, cooling, and recrystallization, leading to the re-anchoring of Co NPs with small particle sizes on the CW surface. Furthermore, the TEM images provided an intuitive insight into the changes of individual nanoparticle, particularly regarding the surface morphology before and after re-activation (Figure 4e—g). The $0 . 3 4 \mathrm { n m }$ layer spacing observed around these Co NPs provided sufficient evidence for the formation of carbon layers (Figure S24, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, XPS spectra showed the $\mathtt { C o 2 p }$ peak of the sample after 10 cycles shifted slightly towards higher binding energies by ${ \approx } 1 . 1 ~ \mathrm { e V } ,$ compared to the pristine sample (Figure S25, Supporting Information), indicating the adsorption of degradation intermediates during the reaction process and surface oxidation of Co NPs. After HTS at $1 9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { K }$ , the regenerated sample exhibited a slight shift towards low binding energies by ${ \approx } 2 . 5 ~ \mathrm { e V } .$ The redistribution of Co NPs on the CW surface and the removal of degradation intermediates after re-thermal shocking treatment caused the binding energies in the regenerated samples to shift towards lower values, aligning with the TEM observations. The XPS spectra also demonstrated the changes in the proportions of $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { C o } ^ { 0 } }$ , $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + }$ , and $\mathsf { C o } ^ { 3 + }$ resulted from oxidation during the re-thermal shocking process. XRD results confirmed that metallic cobalt remained present in the used sample (Figure S26, Supporting Information), indicating no phase changes occurred during the entire "deactivation-regeneration" process.

# 2.6. Continuous Filters Based on Co@CW for Water Treatment

To evaluate its potential for water treatment, we constructed a continuous filter using $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ Wood can be easily processed into plates with the sizes of $1 0 0 \mathrm { m m } \times 5 0 \mathrm { m m } \times 2 \mathrm { m m }$ ,and further carbonized to create disc-shaped filter membranes with a diameter of $2 0 \mathrm { m m }$ (Figure 5a-c). We designed a homemade flow degradation device, where five $\operatorname { C o } \ @ \operatorname { C W }$ catalytic filters are connected in series for continuous catalytic degradation of RhB. The catalytic degradation reactions proceed successfully as the RhB solution flows through the flter, driven by a peristaltic pump, leading to the solution to change from pink to colorless and clear (Figure 5d). Specifically, the RhB mixed with PMS is pumped into the filter to establish the relationship between hydraulic retention time (HRT) and flux rate that can be adjusted by controlling the speed of the peristaltic pump. The HRT represents the average time for wastewater to travel from the top to the bottom of the catalyst layers.

We investigated the influence of RhB concentrations and catalyst quantities on the performance of continuous filters at various flux rates (Figure 5e-g). At certain flux rates, a lower RhB concentration and more $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ filters gradually increased removal efficiency. Even with just one filter at a flux rate of $9 0 \mathrm { m L }$ $\mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , a removal efficiency of $5 1 . 8 1 \%$ was achieved with an RhB concentration of $5 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L ^ { - 1 } }$ . However, as the RhB concentration increased to $1 0 \ \mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ and $2 5 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L ^ { - 1 } }$ , the removal efficiency dropped to $2 6 . 3 7 \%$ and $5 . 2 5 \%$ , respectively. In contrast, using five catalyst filters achieved full removal efficiency for RhB concentrations of $5 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ and $1 0 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ , and a $6 8 . 8 7 \%$ efficiency for $2 5 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L ^ { - 1 } }$ RhB. Increasing to 10 filters significantly enhanced degradation efficiency, achieving an impressive $9 9 . 2 1 \%$ removal efficacy even for $2 5 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ RhB. Meanwhile, a gradual decrease in removal efficiency as the initial concentration of PMS decreased can be obtained under a fixed condition of $2 5 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ RhB concentration and five catalyst filters. When the initial concentration of PMS was $2 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { \mathbf { M } }$ , the degradation efficiency exceeded $90 \%$ across flux rates from 1 to $5 0 \mathrm { m L } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ .Even at a higher flux rate of $9 0 ~ \mathrm { m L } ~ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , the degradation rate was $7 8 . 8 3 \%$ However, as the initial concentration of PMS decreased to 1, 0.5, and $0 . 2 \mathrm { m m }$ (at a flux rate of $9 0 \mathrm { m L } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ), the degradation rates decreased to $7 3 . 4 \%$ $6 8 . 8 7 \%$ , and $4 6 . 9 2 \%$ , respectively, indicating poor degradation efficiency of low PMS concentrations under high flux conditions (Figure S28a, Supporting Information). Using 10 wood filters, continuous operation at a flux rate of $2 0 \mathrm { m L } \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ for 2 hours maintained a relatively high efficiency of over $90 \%$ (Figure S28b, Supporting Information). In addition to the high catalytic activity and durability, $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ demonstrates favorable comprehensive performances compared to commonly used transition metal catalysts, such as Fe SA/NPCs, $\mathrm { C u - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ , Co-HAP, and $\mathrm { C o - N } _ { x }$ wood as the substrate, and the rapid catalyst synthesis via the HTS method (Figure 5h).

# 3. Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully employed a HTS method $( 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K } , 2 \mathrm { s } )$ to prepare a high-performance catalyst with Co NPs uniformly distributed onto carbonized wood, saving significant time and energy. The straightforward wood channels provide numerous active sites for anchoring Co NPs and rapid pathways for liquid transport. The Co NPs on CW substrates activate PMS to generate a large amount of ROS for the RhB degradation. The $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ exhibits excellent PMS activation performance, which can rapidly degrade RhB across a wide pH range of 39 $_ { 2 \mathrm { g } }$ $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ of catalyst, and $0 . 5 \mathrm { m M }$ of PMS), achieving a high degradation kinetic constant of $0 . 5 5 6 3 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . More importantly, we proposed a "deactivation-regeneration" strategy to extend the cycle life of wood catalysts up to 20 times, achieving RhB removal efficiency exceeding $90 \%$ . The deactivated reactive intermediates deposited on $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ surface can be thermally decomposed into a graphite carbon layer around the Co NPs, which accelerates electron transfer, thereby restoring the catalytic activities. As a proof of concept, we built a homemade filter using $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { K }$ for continuous RhB degradation. By connecting 10 filters in series and operating them continuously for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at a flux rate of $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ $\mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , we achieved a RhB removal rate exceeding $90 \%$ , demonstrating the dynamic degradation capacity. Based on abundant and renewable wood, the $\mathrm { C o } @ \mathrm { C W } { \cdot } 1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ catalyst synthesized using energy-efficient HTS method offers obviously advantages in rapid synthesis, effective catalysis, and recyclability, holding great promise for clean water production and environmental remediation.
