# . Introduction

Pharmaceuticals, especially antibiotics, have become concerning pollutants in the past decades because of their intact properties and long-term existence in aqueous environments (Van Doorslaer et al., 2014). Most active ingredient remains unchanged after metabolism, resulting in the excretion of a large portion of antibiotics into water bodies (Lyu et al., 2018). Additionally, the indiscriminative overuse of antibiotics in agriculture and aquaculture also induced the antibacterial resistance. Accordingly, a trace amount (below $1 0 \mu \mathrm { g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ of antibiotics in water have high potential risks for human and animal health through the food chain (Danner et al., 2019). # 2. Fate, transportation, and effects of FQs

The transportation of fluoroquinolones in the environment involves various processes that affect their fate and behavior in natural systems. Fluoroquinolones, which are commonly used antibiotics, can enter the environment through various routes, including wastewater discharges from pharmaceutical manufacturing, agricultural runoff from animal farms, and improper disposal of unused medications. Once in the environment, fluoroquinolones can undergo several biomechanisms of transportation, which can impact their distribution, transformation, and potential ecological risks. Additionally, fluoroquinolones that are sorbed to solid particles can be released through dissolution or desorption, potentially leaching into groundwater or surface water. The transformation and degradation of fluoroquinolones in the environment is another important biomechanism of transportation. Fluoroquinolones can undergo various biotic and abiotic transformation processes, such as metabolism by microorganisms, hydrolysis, photolysis, and oxidation. These processes can result in the degradation or transformation of fluoroquinolones into other compounds, potentially altering their properties and fate in the environment. The transport of fluoroquinolones in water systems is also a significant biomechanism of transportation. Fluoroquinolones can be transported in water systems through processes such as runoff, erosion, and surface water flow. They can also be transported in groundwater through leaching, depending on the properties of the aquifer and the chemical characteristics of the fluoroquinolones. This transport can lead to their distribution in different environmental compartments, potentially affecting aquatic organisms and ecosystems. Bioaccumulation and biomagnification are additional biomechanisms of transportation for fluoroquinolones. Fluoroquinolones can bioaccumulate in living organisms, such as fish and other aquatic organisms, through processes such as bioconcentration and biomagnification. This can result in the potential exposure of higher trophic level organisms to higher concentrations of fluoroquinolones, which can have ecological implications. The presence of fluoroquinolones in the environment can also have ecotoxicological impacts on aquatic and terrestrial organisms. Fluoroquinolones have been shown to exhibit toxicity to a wide range of organisms, including bacteria, algae, invertebrates, and vertebrates. Their potential toxic effects can vary depending on factors such as species, life stage, and exposure duration, and can impact the structure and functioning of ecosystems. The destiny of the FQs in the environment is determined by both their physicochemical qualities, such as its solubility in water, octanol-water partition coefficient $( \mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { o w } } )$ and other environmental factors. High amounts of FQs are found in the aquatic environment as a result of their hydrophilicity, which are taken by humans and cattle and then excreted through faeces and urine. In reality, sorption is the primary process for regulating the movement of FQs. Three main factors influenced the fluoroquinolones sorption onto microplastics in an aquatic environment that were the solution properties (pH, salinity, dissolved organic matter), the properties  the structure of fluoroquinolones (an queous solubility, functional groups), and the microplastic physicochemical properties (types, crystallinity, surface area) (Stapleton et al., 2023). Franklin et al. (2022) showed that the antibiotic sorption effect was into the soils depending on organic content, pH, cation exchange capacity, and soil texture. Some variables have significant impacts on its effectiveness: pH, cation exchange, organic content, clay minerals, surface complexation, and microbial degradation or transformation. It was established that pH has an impact on the whole sorption process because FQs are ionic organic molecules (Bu et al., 2022). Cations like Ca, Al, and Fe have been discovered to complex with FQs and to have a major impact on the ability of FQs to disperse into the environment (Duan et al., 2018).

FQs commonly referred to as "wonder drugs" are chemotherapeutic medicines that inhibit or stop bacterial development. FQs, as antimicrobial agents, exert inhibitory effects on the growth of several pathogenic organisms (Sefton, 2002). However, due to excessive use, some microbes have acquired resistance to some antibiotics. The soil and water source were contaminated by the fluoroquinolones via urine and faeces excretion because of the poorly biodegradable and metabolized in an aquatic environment. Due to the increasing use of FQs in animal feed, there has been an upsurge in the contamination of human food products in recent years (to improve growth and productivity).

The presence of FQs is often determined by how long they have been present in the environment. FQ concentrations in environmental samples typically vary from mg to $\mu \gtreqless$ per liter. However, if there are significant levels of FQ residues, they could pose a serious problem by causing antibacteria resistance, which make treatment more challenging. Along with illnesses, significant allergic reactions in people have also been connected to antibiotic resistance. If the current trend continues, 300 million premature deaths are anticipated to occur globally during the next 35 years. The World Health Organization claims that "antibiotic resistance" represents a serious societal and financial risk to public health. As a result, a number of fluoroquinolones have been identified as potential environmental contaminants. Additionally, these FQ classes have been chosen for their speedy and precise detection across a range of matrices (e.g., food, beverages, and environmental samples). A list of commonly prescribed FQs that can be hazardous to both human and veterinary systems has been reported (Gouvêa et al., 2015).

FQs often stop the synthesis of bacterial cell walls, protein, DNA, RNA, and folic acid in order to combat infections or germs. By deactivating the enzymes responsible for DNA twisting, FQs can break down DNA and stop bacterial development (Liu et al., 2022a). FQs are designed to have a dosage-dependent impact on bacterial and microorganism populations. FQs have detrimental effects that result in microbial colonies disappearing or being eradicated because they have bactericidal and bacteriostatic qualities. However, it is believed that a few numbers of FQs are selected for microbial populations with resistance and genotypic and phenotypic variety. A number of new classes of FQs have been created in recent years to fight disease-causing microbes, but their indiscriminate and excessive use usually resulted in the emergence of bacterial strains that were resistant to them. Antibiotic resistance is a defense mechanism used by the microbial population to avoid the potentially lethal effects of antibiotics. Some of the most typical processes for antibiotics include the creation of hydrolytic enzymes, modifications to the permeability of bacterial cell walls, and alterations to the target locations for antibiotics (Bharadwaj et al., 2022). Eventually, each of these steps will help stop antibiotics from penetrating the microbial cell. In order to lower the prevalence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, dose adjustment and trace-level antibiotic monitoring are strongly advised. Several analytical methods have been created in order to precisely and reliably monitor antibiotic residues in natural resources. When compared to the currently employed analytical methodologies, electrochemical biosensors have proven to be portable, trustworthy, fast response diagnostics for examining antibiotic residues.

Moreover, the effect of fluoroquinolones in the environment is influenced by various biomechanisms, which encompass the processes by which these antibiotics interact with natural systems. Fluoroquinolones, a widely used class of antibiotics, can enter the environment through sources such as pharmaceutical manufacturing discharges, agricultural runoff, and improper medication disposal. Once in the environment, they can exhibit diverse effects on different components of ecosystems, including microorganisms, plants, animals, and humans. One of the key biomechanisms that affects the environmental effect of fluoroquinolones is their interaction with microorganisms. Fluoroquinolones can impact microbial communities in soils, sediments, and water systems, leading to changes in their diversity, abundance, and function. These antibiotics can inhibit the growth of susceptible bacteria, including both pathogenic and beneficial species, leading to disruptions in microbial ecosystems. This can result in shifts in nutrient cycling, altered decomposition rates, and changes in overall ecosystem functioning. Another important biomechanism that influences the effect of fluoroquinolones in the environment is their potential to persist and accumulate in different environmental compartments. Fluoroquinolones are known to have moderate to high persistence in soils, sediments, and water systems, which can lead to their accumulation over time. This accumulation can result in long-term exposure of organisms to these antibiotics, potentially leading to chronic effects on the environment and biota. The potential for fluoroquinolones to induce toxicity in various organisms is also a significant biomechanism that affects their environmental effect. These antibiotics can exhibit toxic effects on different organisms, including aquatic and terrestrial species. For instance, fluoroquinolones can inhibit the growth and reproduction of aquatic organisms, disrupt the activity of enzymes, and affect the behavior and survival of animals. The toxicity of fluoroquinolones can be influenced by factors such as species sensitivity, exposure duration, and concentration, and can have implications for the structure and function of ecosystems. Furthermore, fluoroquinolones can contribute to the development and spread of antibiotic resistance in environmental bacteria, which is a global concern. Exposure to fluoroquinolones in the environment can select for antibiotic-resistant strains of bacteria, leading to the emergence and spread of resistance genes. This can result in the proliferation of antibiotic-resistant bacteria in environmental compartments, potentially posing risks to human and animal health. The potential for fluoroquinolones to interact with other environmental factors, such as pH, temperature, and organic matter content, is another important biomechanism that can affect their environmental effect. These antibiotics can undergo chemical transformations and degradation processes, which can alter their properties and fate in the environment.

# 3. Removal methods

# 3.1. Adsorption

Adsorption is a process in which molecules or atoms are transported from bulk aqueous to a solid surface. The adsorption process can be divided into physical process (Van der Waals force) and chemical process (chemical bond force). Lagergren pseudo-first order, pseudo-second order, Elovich and intra-particle diffusion kinetics models are usually applied to evaluate the adsorption kinetics (Lou et al., 2017). Meanwhile, the Langmuir and Freundlich models are the most famous two models to illustrate the isotherm of adsorption. Some of the kinds of adsorbents are usually used as activated carbon, biochar, graphene, carbon nanotubes, nano metal oxide, resin, and composite. Mechanism of FQs adsorption (Fig. 1) consists of electrostatic attraction, hydrophobic interaction, H-bonding, diffusion pore, $\pi { - } \pi$ interaction, and acid-base interaction (Ashiq et al., 2021; Hu and Xu, 2020; Igwegbe et al., 2021; Khan et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2022c, 2022e; Wang and

Jang, 2022). Moreover, the structural OH units of absobents are crucial to the adsorption process. Since the charges of the adsorbent and adsorbate are the key factors to the adsorption process, increasing pH will increase the hydroxide ions on the adsorbates, which facilitate the adsorption efficiency. Also, the adsorption process relies on the contact time, and temperature of the reaction. It has been reported that the adsorbent's capacity reduced when the treatment heat increase, demonstrating that the formation of the hydrogen bonding in the adsorbent is the primary source of the adsorption (Lou et al., 2015). As shown in Table 1, the adsorption capacity of various absorbents for the FQs was conducted in the laboratory, which has a superior capacity to the FQs removal, suggesting that the adsorption process has a high potential contribution to the FQs removal methods.

# 3.1.1. Effect of pH

The pH of bulk solution is an important parameter of the adsorption process since the electrostatic interaction (attraction and repulsion) between adsorbent and absorbate depends on pH value due to the surface charge of adsorbent and the absorbate ion species. Ma et al. showed that the maximum adsorption ability of CIP by $\mathtt { Z n }$ -SBC absorbent was at $\mathtt { p H } 5$ (Ma et al., 2021). The reason is the CIP species formation $\mathrm { ( C I P ^ { + } }$ at $\mathrm { p H } < 6 . 1$ , $\mathrm { C I P ^ { + - } }$ at $6 . 1 < \mathrm { p H } < 8 . 7$ , $\mathrm { C I P ^ { - } }$ at $\mathrm { p H } > 8 . 7 $ and $\mathrm { \ p H _ { \mathrm { p z c } } }$ of Zn-SBC was 5.89. Hence, the electrostatic attraction at $\mathtt { p H } 5$ was more outstanding than that of electrostatic repulsion. Specific, MOX species were positive ions at $\mathrm { p H } < 6 . 2 5$ and zwitterion  negative ion at $\tt p H >$ 6.25. The $\mathrm { \ p H _ { \mathrm { p z c } } }$ of RH700 adsorbent was 6.30 so the repulsion interaction between RH700 and MOX was formed at $\mathrm { p H } ~ 7$ and $\tt p H 9$ led to reducing MOx degradation capacity (Akhtar et al., 2021).

# 3.2.4. Effect of pH value

The pH solution influenced significantly the efficiency of EO. In most cases, an optimal pH value plays the most important role in pollutants removal. The contaminants and TOC removal in the acidic environment are better than that in the alkaline environment. The reasons of this trend are (i) the electrotransfer obstruction with the electrode surface and the formation of $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ is reduced in the alkaline condition; (ii) the decaying of 'OH into oxygen is inhibited by the $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ ion in the acidic condition (Veciana et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2020). In the study of Yang et al., the rate constant of CIP $\mathrm { C } _ { 0 } = 0 . 0 6 \ \mathrm { m M }$ removal by 3D MP $\mathrm { S n O } _ { 2 } – \mathrm { S b }$ anode increased from $0 . 0 1 1 5 \mathrm { ~ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathrm { ( p H 1 0 ) }$ to $0 . 0 3 1 1 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ (pH 4) due to the hindrance of \*OH decomposition and oxygen evolution at the acidic condition (pH 4) (Yang et al., 2021). Zhao et al. also found a similar result of the high CIP removal in the acidic condition by FeCM cathode and $\mathbf { S n O _ { 2 } } – \mathbf { B i } / \mathrm { T i }$ anode (Zhao et al., 2022). In another study, NOR $( \mathrm { C } _ { 0 } = 0 . 0 6 3 \ : \mathrm { m M } )$ treatment by volcanic rock three  dimensional (3D) partial electrodes was achieved the highest efficiency $( 8 7 . 6 8 \% )$ at $\mathrm { p H } 3$ from pH range of 311 (Song et al., 2021). The reason is that this pH provided higher electrocatalytic ability, more quickly degradation of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to generate reactive species, and a combination between $\mathrm { M n } / \mathrm { C u }$ of volcanic rocks and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to generate reactive species in the acidic condition.

# 3.2.5. Degradation pathways

By enhancing persulfate in the EO system, Zhao et al. illustrated that there are 3 main degradation pathways of CIP (Zhao et al., 2022). They were the dropping ethylene group via the ring cleavage, the piperazinyl ring transforming via the oxygen supplement, and the hydroxylation process via the fluoride replacement by the reactive oxygen species attack. The byproducts of LEV were performed in HPLC/MS-MS, oxazinyl  piperazinyl  quinolyl are decomposed in 14 intermediates with the attacking of •OH radical through demethylation  deoxazinyl decarboxylation  depiperazinyl  defluorination process (Guo et al., 2020). Mineralization of NOR by reactive oxygen species expressed in six by-products by the processes: breaking piperazinyl ring, quinolone collapsing, and defluorination (Liu et al., 2021a).

# 3.4. Photocatalysis

Removal of antibiotics by photocatalytic technology has been paid much of attention because this solution is green, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective (Li et al., 2022). The main mechanism of pollutant photodegradation by photocatalysts can be shortly illustrated in Fig. 4. Under the excitation from a photon flux with the energy higher than the band gap of a semiconductor, the migration of an electron from the valence band (VB) to the conduction bank (CB) occurs, resulting in photogenerated electron-hole pairs on the catalyst. The generated charge carriers then diffuse into catalytically active sites of photocatalysts, and degrade organic compounds by three possible species: (i) holes in VB; (ii) hydroxyl radicals ("OH) from the reaction between holes and $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } O ; }$ . # 3.4.3. Effect of pH

Effect of initial pH on FQs degradation has been studied extensively at different ranges within 211.8 (Khazaee et al., 2021; Narenuch et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2022a; Zhu et al., 2022). pH at mild acidic (e.g., 5 and 5.5) and neutral conditions (e.g., 6.6 and 67) were favorable for the antibiotic treatment (Khazaee et al., 2021; Narenuch et al., 2021; Tien et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2022). Increasing pH to a highly basic condition (e.g., 11 and 11.8) led to the decrease in photoactivity (Khazaee et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022a; Zhu et al., 2022). This result was due to (i) the higher negative surface on the photocatalyst (Khazaee et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022a), (ii) the availability of the anionic form of FQs (Narenuch et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022a) resulting in the repulsion between the heterojunction photocatalyst and antibiotic or the competition between $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ and the anionic form of antibiotic for active sites on photocatalytic surface, and (iii) the inhibition of 'OH radicals generation in an alkaline condition (Yu et al., 2022). A decrease in pH at low levels (e.g., 2 and 3) caused a decrease of photodegradation efficiency which would be attributed to the dissolving of photocatalytic materials under acidic conditions (Narenuch et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022a; Zhu et al., 2022). However, a different trend was found from NOR photocatalytic degradation by $\mathrm { B i _ { 2 } W O _ { 6 } }$ with the presence of TX100 - an amphipathic nonionic surfactant added (Tang et al., 2016) and by $\mathrm { M n } { : } \mathrm { Z n } \mathrm { S }$ quantum dots (Patel et al., 2020), in which the increase in pH from 3.96 to 10.1 and from 2 to 11, respectively led to the increase in NOR degradation efficiency. Tang et al. explained that the higher photocatalytic degradation efficiency achieved from alkaline condition compared to acidic condition was due to the facilitating of the co-adsorption of NOR and TX100 on $\mathrm { B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { W O } _ { 6 }$ surface, and the higher amount of hydroxyl ions in alkaline condition could react with photogenerated hole to generate more hydroxyl radical (Tang et al., 2016).

# 3.4.4. Effect of coexisting ions

Several studies have showed that some inorganic salts presenting in water provided either negligible or negative effects on photocatalytic treatment of FQs. The trends and the significance of the effect found in these studies were different. For anionic ions, Tang et al. reported that $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ and $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ retarded the decay rate of NOR degradation since these ions worked as the scavenger of $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ radicals (Tang et al., 2016). In another study, $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ had a negligible effect while $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ and $\mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ showed inhibitory effect on the photocatalytic degradation of LOM over ZBC-10 (Truong et al., 2022). In addition, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ and $\mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ caused negligible effect on degradation process while the presence of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ caused a slight decrease in photodegradation process of LEV over $\mathrm { Z n F e _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } / N C D }$ . $\mathsf { s } / \mathsf { A g } _ { 2 } \mathsf { C O } _ { 3 }$ (Li et al., 2020a). Regarding to cationic ions, $\mathrm { K } ^ { + }$ , $\mathsf { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ ,and $\mathrm { M g } ^ { 2 + }$ showed a slight inhibition for the degradation of NOR while $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ suppressed significantly this process (Tang et al., 2016). For the degradation of LEV over $\mathrm { Z n F e _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } / N C D s / A g _ { 2 } C O _ { 3 } } ,$ Li et al. found a negligible effect of $\mathrm { N a ~ } ^ { + }$ but a significant inhibition of $\mathsf { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { M g } ^ { 2 + }$ (Li et al., 2020a).

# 3.4.5. Degradation pathways

Although the photocatalytic degradation of antibiotics achieved high efficiencies and in some studies the antibiotics were totally eliminated (Narenuch et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2022), the DOC or TOC removals given in several studies were $6 0 { - } 6 8 \%$ (Gao et al., 2022; Narenuch et al., 2021; Su et al., 2022; Truong et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2018). This fact suggests that the presence of degradation intermediates in treated solution together with inorganic species from the total mineralization of antibiotics (Truong et al., 2022). To propose the pathways of FQs degradation, intermediates have been extensively studied using liquid chromatography method. In general, the treatment of different FQs under photocatalytic processes showed similar degradation pathways (Li et al., 2022), including (i) cleaving the piperazine ring; (ii) cleaving the quinolones core ring; (iii) decarboxylation and (iv) defluorination (Anjali & Shanthakumar, 2019; Dang et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Phoon et al., 2020; Senasu et al., 2022). Depending on the difference in the FQs chemical structure and the reaction conditions of photocatalytic processes, the main pathways proposed by various studies might be partially different. For example, Truong et al. found a total of 16 major intermediates from the treatment of Lomefloxacin (LOM) by $\mathrm { Z n F e _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } / B i V O _ { 4 } / g  – C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ and suggested three biodegradation pathways, which are opening of piperazinyl ring, separating of piperazinyl and quinoline moieties, and cleaving of pyridine ring on the quinoline moieties (Truong et al., 2022). Three pathways was proposed for Norfloxacin (NOR) degradation by $Z _ { \mathrm { { n O } / \mathrm { { B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { { W O } _ { 6 } , } } } }$ ,including the defluorination and de-ethylation to form intermediates, piperazinyl group destruction, and direct photodecomposition of NOR (Narenuch et al., 2021).

# 3.5. Biological methods

As mentioned above, the physicochemical methods bring high efficiency to FQs treatment, however, the essence of the removal mechanism is only to transfer FQs from one phase to another (e.g. liquid phase to solid phase). Biological methods have been considered to employ for antibiotic removal, especially for the fluoroquinolones group.

Once the biological processes have been employed for antibiotics treatment, these antibiotics can inhibit the microbial community in bioreactors, resulting in the deterioration of removal efficiency. In fact, Wang et al. showed that the bioactivity of sludge was slightly restricted when the fluoroquinolones concentration was higher than $5 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { g } ~ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ (Wang et al., 2017b). Despite this drawback, biological methods have been widely employed for FQs treatment due to their good efficiency. According to previous studies, FQs in wastewater could be reduced by biological methods including air-stripping, photo-transformation, adsorption, and biodegradation (Zhang et al., 2008) (Verlicchi et al., 2012) (Fig. 5). Since the air-stripping process relates to air/water partition or known as the Henry coefficient $( \mathrm { K _ { H } } )$ , an antibiotic must have a Henry coefficient higher than $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ for air-stripping occurrence. Unfortunately, most fluoroquinolones possess a low Henry coefficient (Li et al., 2015), consequently, the air-stripping mechanism is negligible under fluoroquinolones treatment. In addition, because most of light sources can be blocked due to the high turbidity and sludge concentration in wastewater, the photo-transformation also accounts for a low proportion of antibiotics removal mechanism.

Overall, fluoroquinolones in bioreactors are mainly removed by biodegradation and adsorption pathways. For the removal mechanism by sorption on biosolids, an antibiotic that has an octanol/water partition coefficient (log $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { o w } } )$ higher than 3.2 tends to adsorb on sludge biomass in bioreactors (Tiwari et al., 2017). Besides, Li et al. indicated that the adsorption ability of antibiotics was dependent on the sludge/water partition coefficient $\mathrm { ( K _ { d } ) }$ , and an antibiotic can be adsorbed to sludge when the $\mathrm { K _ { d } }$ value is higher than $5 0 0 \mathrm { L } \mathrm { k g ^ { - 1 } M L S S }$ (MLSS mixed liquor suspended solids) (Li et al., 2015). In the case of the fluoroquinolones group, NOR, CIP, and OFL have $\mathrm { K _ { d } }$ values of 190, ${ > } 1 5 0 0$ , and 250 L ${ \mathrm { k g } } ^ { - 1 } { \mathrm { M L S S } }$ ,while log $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { o w } }$ values were $- 1 . 0 3 \ - \ 1 . 4 8$ 0.281.32, and $0 . 8 4 { - } 2 . 1 0 $ , respectively (Li et al., 2015; Tiwari et al., 2017). Despite the low $\mathrm { K _ { d } }$ and log $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { o w } }$ values, the strong adsorption of fluoroquinolones into sludge has been demonstrated in some previous studies (Kim et al., 2014; Tran et al., 2016). These studies indicated that the electrostatic interaction between the fluoroquinolones molecules and negative charges of sludge surface could help them absorb into sludge more easily. Moreover, the threshold of $\mathrm { K _ { d } }$ and log $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { o w } }$ as mentioned above were determined under synthesis wastewater with ignoring the effect of ionized species of a compound. Real wastewater contains many complex components so the adsorption ability of FQ antibiotics is related to some other factors.

# 3.5.1. Bacteria-based bioreactors

Compared to conventional bacteria-based bioreactors, membrane bioreactor (MBR) technology has been demonstrated to have the potential for antibiotic elimination. MBR can retain a high biomass concentration, which promotes fluoroquinolones removal via biosorption mechanism. Sipma et al. concluded that sludge retention time (SRT) has a significant effect on the MBR performance since SRT can decide the biodiversity and biomass concentration in MBR (Sipma et al., 2010). Keeping SRT longer than 20 days would gain the appropriate development of bacteria communities. In recent studies, a modified MBR system utilized for wastewater treatment has been defined as Sponge-MBR. Polyethylene cubic sponges, which have been added to MBR to create attached-growth biomass inside sponges, could improve the diversity of microbial communities. Moreover, sponges assist bacteria in having enough time to adapt and transform antibiotics in wastewater. The removal efficiency of antibiotics in hospital wastewater over two sponge-MBRs installed with different membrane modules was researched and compared (flat sheet and hollow fiber). The concentrations of NOR, OFL, and CIP measured in hospital wastewater in Vietnam were 6.343.6, 7.640.3, and $1 . 9 { - } 2 3 . 8 \mu \mathrm { g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. A flat-sheet sponge-MBR could remove $9 3 { - } 9 9 \%$ of NOR, $6 2 \mathrm { - } 8 6 \%$ of OFL, and $7 3 { - } 9 3 \%$ of CIP from hospital wastewater under an operational flux of $1 0 { - } 2 0 \ \mathrm { ~ L ~ m ^ { - 2 } ~ h ^ { - 1 } }$ (LMH) (Arya et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2017). Meanwhile, the removal efficiencies slightly decreased in the hollow-fiber sponge-MBR; the obtained values were 7693, 5470, and $6 0 { - } 9 7 \%$ for NOR, OFL, and CIP removal, respectively. This result was attributed to the higher biomass concentration obtained in the sponge-MBR that installed with flat-sheet membrane modules. Similar results were obtained in the study of Bui et al. (2019); the removal efficiencies were achieved at around $8 6 \%$ for NOR, $6 8 \%$ for OFL, and $7 0 \%$ for CIP by employing a hollow-fiber sponge-MBR for hospital wastewater treatment. Besides, this study suggested a post-treatment unit such as the ozonation process for enhancing antibiotic removal. The results showed that the overall removal efficiency was improved by $1 3 { - } 2 0 \%$ for three fluoroquinolones.

On the other hand, an improved version of the conventional activated sludge process, such as aerobic granular sludge, has been utilized for antibiotic removal. Compared to activated sludge flocs, sludge granules have several properties such as fast settling ability and good contact between biomass and wastewater due to improving the diffusion interface (Wang et al., 2021). Another outstanding feature of the granular formation is that bacteria inside sludge granules extract more extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) to defend themselves from the toxicity of antibiotics (Shi et al., 2013). In specific research by Zhao et al., a sequencing bioreactor was employed to generate aerobic sludge granules for personal care products and pharmaceuticals treatment (Zhao et al., 2015). Among organic compounds elimination, CIP removal efficiency was achieved at above $6 0 \%$ under the initial concentration of $5 0 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ and then increased to approximately $8 0 \%$ since bacteria gradually adapted to pharmaceuticals with time. Moreover, the specific surface area of granular sludge increasing with time leads to the development of adsorption sites for organic compounds (Zhao et al., 2015).

# 3.5.2. Fungi-based bioreactors

Some white-rot fungi possess nonspecific oxidative enzymes such as lignin peroxidases, laccase, versatile peroxidase, manganese peroxidase (extracellular enzyme), and cytochrome P450 (intracellular enzyme) which have the capability to bioremediate pharmaceutical active compounds effectively (Cruz-Morató et al., 2014). Among them, Trametes versicolor has been widely applied in bioreactors for emerging pollutant treatment; for instance, Trametes versicolor was added into MBR (Yang et al., 2013) or fluidized-bed bioreactor (Cruz-Morató et al., 2013) to remove complex organic compounds. As mentioned above, antibiotics in bioreactors have been removed through biosorption and biodegradation. In cases where biosorption prevails, the antibiotics that accumulate in the sludge can become secondary contaminants that complicate the sludge treatment. Adding Trametes versicolor pellets to bioreactors can improve the biodegradation mechanism, thereby antibiotics are converted to non-toxic products (Cruz-Morató et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2013). In the case of fluoroquinolones treatment, a batch fluidized-bed bioreactor operated with Trametes versicolor was conducted to treat hospital wastewater in Spain by Cruz et al. (Cruz-Morató et al., 2014); two antibiotics having highest contents were OFL and CIP with initial concentrations of 31.9 and $1 3 . 0 4 ~ \mu \mathrm { g } ~ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ (average), respectively. The results showed that OFL and CIP were almost completely eliminated. However, the application of white-rot fungi has a major drawback as the pH drop due to the excretion of organic acid by these fungi.

Therefore, it is necessary to find the efficient and affordable techniques to remove antibiotics, not only for saving resources but also for reducing the negative influence of FQs' residues on the ecosystem. Adsorption has some advantages in eliminating FQs instead of producing risky metabolites and the main mechanism of adsorption process includes electrostatic attractions, pore-flling, and $\pi { - } \pi$ interaction by metal oxides, resins, and carbon-based composites. The transition metalbased composites, electro- and photo-catalytic methods are associated with the strong oxidant radicals generation under PMS/PS activation, electric current  photo energy to degrade FQs. Much emphasis has been given to the photocatalytic removal of antibiotics. Antibiotic concentration, photocatalyst dosage, pH, and coexisting ions are some of the variables impacting the treatment efficiency. Although the removal efficiency increases with increasing photocatalyst dosage, it decreases when the initial antibiotic concentration rises. Moreover, the photocatalytic treatment is favorable in mildly acidic and neutral pH conditions but declines in a very basic environment. Depending on the differences in the chemical structures of the FQs and the conditions of the reaction, the degrading pathways for FQs photocatalytic degradation can include the cleaving the piperazine ring, cleaving the quinolones core ring, decarboxylation and defluorination. The biological processes for the degradation of FQs are based on the biosorption and biodegradation of microorganisms on activated sludge. Recently, photocatalytic and electrocatalytic methods are mostly exclusively applied in degrading FQs. The high cost of operation restricts the scalable application of electrochemical catalysis. Activation of oxidizing agents and photocatalysis are the most cost-effective strategies for FQs degradation. The oxidizing agents have been proven to be feasibly applied to treat high concentrated FQs water.

Combining those methods is a powerful strategy to remove FQ antibiotics from the environment, for example, adsorption followed by photocatalysis or biological treatment can induce a better removal efficiency, not only for lab-scale but also for full-scale applications. Hence, to further improve the antibiotic removal in wastewater treatment plants, the combined treatment process including cheap adsorption and catalytic materials would provide the best result for the treatment of effluents containing antibiotics. Practically, the conventional treatment plants can be integrated with advanced physical-chemical treatment technologies. Future work should pay more attention to developing unified models as well as evaluating and controlling the influence of various operational parameters on treatment processes, which will provide a better understanding the fate and transport of these kinds of antibiotics in the environment.
