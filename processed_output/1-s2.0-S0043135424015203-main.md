Conventional heterogeneous Fenton-like systems employing different peroxides have been developed for water/ wastewater remediation. However, a large population of peroxides consumed during various Fenton-like systems with low utilization efficiency and associated secondary contamination have become the bottlenecks for their actual applications. Recent strategies for lowering the peroxide consumptions to develop economic Fenton-like systems are primarily devoted to the effective radical generation and subsequent high-efficiency radical utilization through catalysts/systems engineering, leveraging emerging nonradical oxidation pathways with higher selectivity and longer life of the reactive intermediate, as well as reactor designs for promoting the mass transfer and peroxides decomposition to improve the yield of radicals/nonradicals. However, a comparative review summarizing the mechanisms and pathways of these strategies has not yet been published. In this review, we endeavor to showcase the designated systems achieving the reduction of peroxides while ensuring high catalytic activity from the perspective of the above strategic mechanisms. An in-depth understanding of these aspects will help elucidate the key mechanisms for achieving economic peroxide consumption. Finally, the existing problems of these strategies are put forward, and new ideas and research directions for lowering peroxide consumption are proposed to promote the application of various Fenton-like systems in actual wastewater purification.

# 1. Introduction

At present, water purification faces the problems of overloaded treatment capacity and complicated compositions of emerging pollutants, such as personal care products, pharmaceuticals, pesticides, endocrine disrupting chemicals, which may cause adverse effects on various aquatic organisms and human health and has received increasing attention (Gao et al., 2024c; Guo et al., 2023a; Liang et al., 2023; Preethi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022a). Traditional water treatment technologies could not completely remove these emerging pollutants. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are particularly effective for the removal of these refractory compounds (Gosselin et al., 2013; Ji et al., 2018; Preethi et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2023a). The Fenton-like reaction is one of the most widely studied AOPs, which can produce a series of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via activating versatile peroxides (e.g., hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ , persulfate, peracetic acid (PAA), ozone $\left( \mathbf { O } _ { 3 } \right)$ for attacking pollutants. As a result, Fenton-like systems using various peroxides have become promising techniques for the removal of emerging contaminants in water (Chen et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2024c; Gosselin et al., 2013; Ji et al., 2018; Nie et al., 2022; Preethi et al., 2024; Subramanian and Prakash, 2021; Yin et al., 2023a). However, the intensive peroxide consumption in various Fenton-like systems, coupled with low utilization efficiency, has become one of the biggest bottlenecks for their actual application.

Different peroxides always suffer from different factors for controlling their amounts used in Fenton-like systems (Bai et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2018; Pan et al., 2018; Preethi et al., 2024; Subramanian and Prakash, 2021). As for $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , the large-scale industrial preparation method is mainly based on the ethyl anthraquinone reaction, which is a multi-step process requiring the participation of noble metal catalysts, consuming massive energies and costs with accompanying of the production of organic wastewaters. The direct fabrication of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ from $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ has become an environmentally friendly alternative (Chang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022a; Preethi et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2020;

Zhou et al., 2022a). In addition, the hazards of excessive $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ in Fenton and Fenton-like systems are well recognized, posing significant challenges. Therefore, an optimal dose of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ has been suggested. Persulfates, including peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and peroxydisulfate (PDS), have good oxidation properties and can decompose rapidly in the aqueous solution to release ROS for water remediations (Ji et al., 2018; Ling et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2021c; Nie et al., 2022; Preethi et al., 2024; Thamilselvan et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023a). Persulfate is highly oxidizing and may cause fire or explosion when in contact with organic matters, reducing agents or flammable substances. In addition, a large amount of sulfate $( S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } )$ is also produced in water treatment applications, which causes remarkable salt residue in the water body (Guo et al., 2024a,b; Yin et al., 2024a). PAA is a strong oxidizing agent, and recently it is also widely used in Fenton-like systems (Chen et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2024c; Liu et al., 2021b; Wang et al., 2021b; Wu et al., 2020). However, the primary concern is the extreme instability of PAA. When its concentration exceeds $4 5 \ \%$ , it becomes explosive and can cause an explosion in the presence of high heat, reducing agents, or metal ions (Liu et al., 2021b; Sharma et al., 2023). Basically, the use of excess peroxide in Fenton systems is recognized to lead to peroxide consumption. As such, the dangers of excessive peroxide are well recognized with the major challenge not to minimize use but to find the most economical dose, that is, the amount of peroxide to achieve the specified required removal performances.

The timelines for the development of Fenton-like reactions based on low-peroxide-consumption strategies as well as conventional heterogeneous Fenton-like systems were given in Fig. 1a. Conventional heterogeneous Fenton-like systems have been developed in a variety of aspects, including different peroxides (e.g., $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , PMS, PDS), different catalytic systems with versatile sizes of catalytic sites, catalytic membranes, catalytic mechanisms and pathways (Liu et al., 2023a; Pan et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023a; Zhao et al., 2023b; Zuo et al., 2023). Over the past several years, a number of studies have concentrated on Fenton-like systems characterized by low peroxide consumption, aiming to facilitate cost-effective applications of these processes (Cao et al., 2021; Cao et al., 2023a; Cao et al., 2023b; Sun et al., 2022; Zhan et al., 2020). Recent strategies for reducing peroxide consumption have primarily focused on (i) enhancing radical generation and maximizing radical efficiency through advancements in catalysts/catalytic systems engineering (Chen et al., 2024a; Chen et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023c; Xing et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2023), or (ii) the regulation of nonradical pathways for lowering peroxide consumptions with higher selectivity and longer life, or (iii) reactor designs for promoting the mass transfer and peroxides decomposition to improve the yield of radicals/nonradicals (Guo et al., 2024a, b; Yin et al., 2023a; Yin et al., 2023b). However, a comparative review summarizing the mechanisms and pathways of these strategies (such as modulating metal loading and dispersion, co-catalysis strategies, dual-reaction-center (DRC) catalysis, confinement effect, singlet oxygen oxidation, high-valent metal-oxo oxidation, electron transfer pathway (ETP), oxidative polymerization process) has not yet been published (Fig. 1b). In this review, for the first time, we endeavor to demonstrate the strategies for reducing peroxides while ensuring high catalytic activity from the perspective of the above strategic mechanisms. An in-depth understanding of these aspects will help elucidate the key mechanisms for economic peroxide usage. Finally, the existing challenges of these strategies are addressed, and new ideas and future research directions for reducing peroxide consumption are proposed, with the aim of promoting the application of various Fenton-like systems in real-world wastewater purification.

# 2. Effective radical generation and high-efficiency radical utilization

It was known that the oxidation kinetics between various organics and radicals $( k = 1 0 ^ { 6 } - 1 0 ^ { 1 1 } \mathrm { ~ M ~ } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { ~ } s ^ { - 1 } )$ are almost several orders of magnitude faster than those of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ( k = - 1 0 ^ { 2 } - 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { ~ M ~ } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ and highvalent metal-oxo species $( k = 1 0 ^ { 2 }  – 1 0 ^ { 4 } \textrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 } )$ (Guo et al., 2023b; Lee et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2018; Wu and Kim, 2022; Yun et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020b). However, free radicals are easily quenched by various impurities in the water matrixes (Lee et al., 2020). Therefore, AOPs often consume a large amount of peroxides to produce enough free radicals to compensate for the low utilizations of radicals. However, the addition of excessive peroxides in the Fenton-like reactions would cause excessive application costs and environmental burdens. Moreover, the hazards of excessive peroxides in Fenton/Fenton-like systems are well recognized as one of the major challenges. Recently, a series of catalyst/reactor/system engineering strategies (i.e., modulation of metal loading and dispersion, dual-reaction-centers (DRCs) catalysis, confinement catalysis, and co-catalysis systems) have been used to activate various peroxides (such as $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ , PMS, PDS) for (i) the generation of high populations of free radicals; and (ii) high-efficiency radical utilization (Chen et al., 2024c; Chen et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2023c; Ran et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023). Since the peroxide consumption in different systems is closely related to the concentrations of pollutants, the peroxide consumption is normalized as the peroxide usage per mass pollutants $\mathrm { { ( m m o l ~ m g ^ { - 1 } } ; }$ ). The normalized results obtained in different radical-based strategies comparing with conventional Fenton-like systems are shown in Fig. 2a and b.

# 2.1. Modulation strategies of metal loading and dispersion

Recent advances in rational designs of nanocatalysts with high metal loadings have provided tremendous opportunities for lowering the consumption of peroxides in various Fenton-like systems (Feng et al., 2024; Lyu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a). For example, the regulation of the surface structure of sulfurized zero-valent iron enables ultrafast oxidation of contaminants at very low $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ usage (1 mM, 0.1 mmol $\mathbf { m } { \mathbf { g } } ^ { - 1 }$ $\mathrm { { H _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } } }$ per mass pollutants)) (Feng et al., 2024), which accounts for only $2 { - } 1 0 \%$ of the $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ usage in most reported Fenton-like systems (Li et al., 2023a; Ma et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2018; Xin et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2022). However, the high metal dissolution for these bare metal nanoparticles (NPs) resulted in the rapid passivation of the catalysts and associated secondary pollution. It was reported that the encapsulation of metal NPs into the carbon matrixes not only improves the stability of metal NPs and metal dissolution, but also avoids the agglomeration of neighboring metal NPs, to achieve rapid and long-term activation of peroxides (Fig. 3a) (Wang et al., 2023a; Wang et al., 2023c). The key to this strategy is that specific functional groups (i.e., carboxy groups, hydroxyl groups) on carbon-based structures can chelate a large number of metal ions under carbothermal reduction, and then rapidly self-polymerize to inhibit metal aggregation, ensuring the formation of well-dispersed metal NPs in situ (Guo et al., Shang et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2023a). The resulting catalysts could induce the electrons migrating from the catalysts to the peroxides (i.e., $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , PMS, PDS), resulting in the continuous generation of $_ \mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and high utilization efficiency (Wang et al., 2023a; Wang et al., 2023c). Xiong and colleagues fabricated a Co NPs/carbon composites with the Co NPs fully dispersed in the carbon frameworks (Wang et al., 2023c), and the high metal NPs loading could significantly promote the strong metal-supports interactions and facilitate the binding of peroxide for subsequent dissociation and generation of radicals. Thus, the peroxide amount (0.3 mM of PMS, $0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { m g } ^ { - 1 }$ (PMS per mass pollutants)) can be greatly reduced as compared with those $\left( > 5 . 0 \ \mathrm { m M } \right)$ in conventional heterogeneous PMS systems (corresponding to $0 . 2 { - } 0 . 5 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { m g } ^ { - 1 }$ or even more than $0 . 5 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { m g } ^ { - 1 }$ (PMS/PDS per mass pollutants)) (Qu et al., 2022). Although increasing the metal loading can simultaneously increase the catalytic activity for pollutant degradation while reducing peroxide consumption. There are two demerits to this strategy. For example, (i) large amounts of metal species are required and consumed with very low metal utilization efficiencies (Shang et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2023), and (ii) secondary pollution caused by a high amount of metal dissolving in the Fenton-like systems (Shang et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024b). As a result, maximizing the metal utilization efficiency to ensure high-efficiency peroxide activation and controlled metal dissolving will be essential for the actual applications of this strategy (Guo et al., Shang et al., 2021, Wu and Kim, 2022).

Exploring stable and efficient single-atom catalysts (SACs) have been inspired by downsizing the large metal particles into the atom-level sizes and applied for the resource-saving and sustainable environmental remediations (Banerjee et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2017; DeRita et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022; Qiao et al., 2011). The atomically dispersed metal atoms can serve as the catalytic sites and are exposed to efficiently activate the peroxides with very high metal utilization efficiencies (Huang et al., 2021; Weon et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020a). Although the metal loadings (0.25–10.0 wt.%) of SACs is almost 1–2 orders of magnitude lower than those of metal NPs $( 2 . 0 { - } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { w t . } \% )$ , their oxidation reactivity, stability and utilization efficiency of peroxides are always superior to those of NPs counterparts (Guo et al., Shang et al., 2023; Shang et al., 2021). These ultra-high metal availability and high inherent peroxide-activating activity of SACs can be attributed to the stronger metal-support interactions with improved metal site dispersion and robustness (Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023c; Yang et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2023d; Zhao et al., 2023a). In addition, the unique coordination electronic properties of SACs can also show strong activation ability towards peroxide and significantly enhance the generation of reactive species under low peroxide consumption (Guo et al., Li et al., 2023c; Shang et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2024a). For example, fluorine atom coordinated with iron site could rapidly activate $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and selectively decompose to produce HO• with a ultra-high HO• yield up to $4 5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { M }$ , which was 1 to 3 orders of magnitude higher than typical iron-based Fenton catalysts that could only utilize $1 0 \ \%$ of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ (Yu et al., 2024a). The metal-N configurations with high spin states could facilitate the coupling of oxygen-containing substances at metal sites, thus promoting the adsorption of PMS and elevating the oxidation potential of PMS intermediates for generating more radicals under lower PMS consumptions with the range of $0 . 0 1 { - } 0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m m o l \ m g ^ { - 1 } }$ , accounting for 2–25 $\%$ of PMS consumptions in conventional Fenton-like systems (Miao et al., 2021; Qian et al., 2021). We should point out that not all SACs would consume a low peroxide dosage for pollutant degradation (Chen et al., 2024a; Chen et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023b; Yang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2023c). Different single-atom catalytic systems exhibited different consumptions of peroxides, which might be ascribed to several aspects related to the structures of catalysts, such as metal types, coordination configurations, loadings of metal species, and interactions between metal-supports in different SACs (Chen et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2023; Shang et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023a; Wu et al., 2023b; Yang et al., 2023c).

# 2.2. Confinement effect for improving radical utilization

When catalysis sites are confined within nano-sized spaces, their intrinsic activity and kinetics are significantly regulated, known as the \`\`nanoconfinement effect’’ (Qian et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021b). In the confined nanoscale environment, electron mobility is constrained, altering electron dynamics and impacting the electronic structure, particularly the valence electron configuration (Du et al., 2017; Kumar et al., 2017). Consequently, this reduction in reaction energy barriers would substantially enhance the Fenton-like catalytic efficiency and selectivity, while alleviating the problems of excessive and low utilization of peroxides. Instead of being limited by the material itself, this subsection will critically discuss the mechanism of nanoconfinement catalysis to achieve low peroxide consumption reduction from a new perspective, including regional pollutant ROS enrichment, enhanced mass transfer, and curvature-induced spatial microenvironment.

# 2.2.1. Pollutants and reactive oxygen species enrichment effect

The peroxide utilization efficiency is low due to the extremely limited effective activity radius of ROS $( < 2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { \ n m } )$ . The nanoconfinement effect could enhance the enrichment of pollutant molecules and augment their local concentrations, facilitating the oxidizing substances generated at the catalytic interface to react with surrounding pollutants more efficiently, thereby minimizing the required peroxide dosage (Fig. 3b) (Zhang et al., 2023c; Zhou et al., 2022b). For example, the interior cavity of yolk-shell nanoreactors with a typical core@void@shell architecture would create an ideal microenvironment for confining reactants, allowing the enriched organic compounds to be rapidly targeted by the fresh radicals produced on the active sites over the core, fostering a localized, highly concentrated environment conducive to AOPs, leading to highly efficient and rapid degradation (Du et al., 2017; Kumar et al., 2017; Zeng et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). The augmented removal of organic pollutants via nanoconfinement catalysis can also be attributed to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within the nanoscale environment, which facilitates the rapid reaction of pollutants with ROS and thus the efficient utilization of ROS (Cheng et al., 2024; Yin et al., $2 0 2 4 \mathrm { a }$ ; Yin et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2020b). For example, the activation of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ based on different membrane pore sizes confirmed that HO• is mainly generated in smaller pores $2 5 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ , while the concentration and diffusion capacity of $_ \mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ decrease significantly with the increase of pore size (Zhang et al., 2020b). The interfacial tunneling in the layered minerals could also provide the confined spacing for ROS accumulation and reducing the electron migration distance toward organic contaminants (Cheng et al., 2024). Therefore, these pore or layer confined structures can effectively promote the generation and accumulation of short-lived ROS, thereby improving the catalytic efficiency and minimizing the consumption of peroxides during pollutant oxidation (accounting for $5 - 2 0 ~ \%$ of conventional peroxide consumption, as shown in Fig. 2) (Zhang et al., 2020b). Thus, the effective enrichment of both contaminants and ROS on the catalysts can reduce the migration distance and improve the utilization efficiency of free radicals.

# 2.2.2. Mass transfer effect

Enhancing mass transfer of short-lived ROS actives under the \`\`nanoconfinement effect’’ phenomenon can lead to improved degradation of micropollutants and reduced peroxide dosage (Qian et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2019). The core principle is to control the distance between the micropollutant, catalyst surface and peroxide at the nanoscale where the reaction mainly takes place. As compared with the conventional Fenton-like reaction processes, the mass transfer distance of reactants can be substantially reduced by confining the catalytic reaction within the nanospace, leading to enhanced reaction kinetics at millimolar-level $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ dosage (Yang et al., 2019). Meng et al., investigated that the angstrom-scale interlayer spacing in ${ \mathrm { C o } } { \mathrm { - T i O } } _ { x }$ membranes can significantly enhance the interaction between reactive oxygen species and target pollutants by facilitating more effective diffusion and reactions. Such enhanced collision efficiency was demonstrated through ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which can notably accelerate the catalytic kinetics (Meng et al., 2022a). The confined structures could effectively promote the diffusion of mass transfer within the nanochannels, thus improving the utilization efficiency of ROS.

# 2.3. Dual-reaction-center (DRC) catalysis for low peroxide consumption

Recent studies have reported that reconstructing the DRCs with electron-poor and -rich centers on the surfaces of Fenton-like catalysts could effectively direct the surface electron migration and achieve great utilization efficiency of various peroxides (i.e., $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , PMS/PDS, or dissolved oxygen), reducing the consumption of peroxides and breaking the pH bottlenecks (Cao et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022b; Zhan et al., 2020). The radicals can be triggered in the regions of electron-rich centers via activating the peroxides, while the pollutants and their degradation products are adsorbed onto the electron-poor regions of the catalysts with their electrons quickly transferring to the electron-rich regions. The pollutants are then substantially oxidized due to the redox reactions that occur between DRCs. Such a DRC catalysis process could greatly inhibit the overoxidation of peroxides via the use of pollutants as electron donors, which would realize a high utilization efficiency of peroxides (i.e., $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ utilization efficiency rate of ${ \sim } 9 0 ~ \%$ ) (Lyu et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2022).

At present, two strategies are mainly used to construct DRCs catalytic systems, including (i) using metal lattice substitution with different electronegativity to induce uneven electron distribution on the surface; (ii) metal and organic surface complexation or no metal molecules doping to construct electron transfer bridge. The former is easy to achieve via modified synthesis, which will fine-tune the surface chemistry, while the latter is more likely to form a strong polarity at the electronrich/electron-deficient center. For example, a series of dual-cationic $\pi$ -assembly catalysts composed of the cross-linked carbon-framework hybrids with various metal species (e.g., ZnS, $\mathrm { F e O } _ { x } ,$ $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ , $\mathrm { C u } _ { 0 } \mathrm { , }$ were developed, and the electrons of pollutants can be captured by $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ via the dual cation- $\pi$ interactions (electron transfer of $\pi $ cation $\scriptstyle { \overbrace { \boldsymbol { \sigma } } }$ -donated) as well as the cation $\to \pi ^ { * }$ $\overbar { \boldsymbol { \pi } }$ -back-donated)), inhibiting the oxidative decomposition of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and promoting its hydroxylation (Lyu et al., 2023). As a result, the as-prepared catalytic systems with DRCs exhibited excellent oxidation activities by using pollutants as electron donors to replace the consumption of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ with the consumption of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ only $6 { - } 8 ~ \%$ of those in the ordinary Fenton-like systems.

Recently, the DRCs-based catalysis systems have been further fabricated by using green and low-cost waste resources (Cao et al., 2023b; Sun et al., 2022). The complexation of metal and organic supports in some waste resources could form a cation– $\textstyle { \mathfrak { \pi } }$ bond, which was conducive to optimizing the charge redistribution of catalyst surface and reconstructing DRCs, driving the high-efficiency degradation of various refractory organic pollutants (Fig. 4a and b). For example, various livestock manures are rich in various organic and metallic substances, such as Mg, Ca, Fe, C, O, N, and P species, serving as the ideal raw materials for the preparation of the DRCs-based catalysts (Sun et al., 2022). Sun et al., pioneered a sustainable strategy to create DRC-based catalysts by orderly combining metal-organic species inherent in untreated livestock manure through an in-situ annealing process (Sun et al., 2022). The directional electron transfer and the resulting metal cation- $\pi$ interaction facilitated the formation of electron-rich/electron-poor regions. $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ was mainly decomposed into HO• in the electron-rich centers, while the resulting organic radical intermediates $( \bullet \mathrm { \mathbf { R } } )$ could be adsorbed and oxidized in the electron-poor centers. These separated oxidation/reduction reaction sites would greatly increase the catalytic activity and high $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ utilization rate via triggering less than $5 \%$ of conventional $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ usage $\left( < 0 . 2 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { m g } ^ { - 1 } \right.$ $\mathrm { ( H _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } }$ per mass pollutants)). The oxidation of pollutants by the livestock manure-based catalysts can even overcome the bottleneck of salinity, with the degradation activities increasing by 1.73–6.58 times in the presence of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ , and $\mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ . The enhanced water self-purification via salinity-mediated process was due to the intermolecular forces between the pollutants and the anions, which facilitated the bonding of pollutants with electron-rich sites (active Fe sites) accompanied by enhanced electron exchange, further inducing the self-purification of pollutants as well as spontaneous activation of $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ and DO to generate the $_ \mathrm { H O } \cdot$ and $\cdot _ { 0 _ { 2 } ^ { - } }$ .(Cao et al., 2023a) In addition, the local bonding network of $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ molecules on the surface of as-prepared catalysts can be distorted by anions (especially $\mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - } .$ ), opening a channel for the preferential binding of DO and pollutants molecules on the surface of catalyst, as well as simultaneously facilitating the electron donating of pollutants via intermolecular interaction (Fig. 4c). This salinity-mediated self-purification process could effectively solve the bottleneck problem of high salinity wastewater treatment by leveraging the background substances, i.e., DO, pollutants and salts, that synergistically work with DRCs-based catalysts. Through this salinity-mediated water self-purification technique, the cost of water treatment can be significantly reduced, especially in terms of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ expenses.

In terms of application scope, DRC technology extends beyond traditional uses in municipal and industrial wastewater treatment, similar to the classical Fenton reaction. It also enhances the advanced purification of drinking water and polluted groundwater. Specifically, the DRC process effectively removes organic micropollutants, including trace amounts of antibiotics and pesticides, from water environments with economic peroxide consumption.

Up to sum, the above strategies, such as modulation strategies of metal loading and dispersion, confinement effect, and DRC catalysis, could efficiently realize the ultra-high population of radicals with very low peroxide consumption, but the major demerits for these strategies are the need for sophisticated catalyst design and high cost of use. In addition, whether these ingenious catalysts can be used in real environments is also a challenge.

# 2.4. Co-catalysis strategy for high peroxide utilization

In various Fenton-like oxidation systems, especially in $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ -based systems, the steps in the rate-limiting reactions are mainly based on the recoveries of Fe(III)/Fe(II) cycling. These recovery cycles were always in kinetic sluggishness, resulting in insufficient electron supplementation with extremely inefficient utilization of peroxides and unsustainable production of ROS. In order to improve the utilization efficiency of peroxides in various Fenton-like reactions, a series of organic cocatalysts (i.e., organic acids or amines) or inorganic co-catalysts (e.g., metal sulfides, metal borides) were added to promote the cycles of metal redox for ROS generation as well as reduce ineffective consumption of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ (Chen et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2023c; Ran et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2023b; Zhou et al., 2023), thereby promoting the oxidation kinetics and reducing the peroxide usages with improved peroxide utilization.

At present, the studies on co-catalysis are mainly focused on the development and application of inorganic co-catalysts, because inorganic cocatalysts have more material sources and less secondary pollution than organic co-catalysts. A series of metal sulfides (e.g., $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ , ${ \mathrm { W } } { \mathrm { S } } _ { 2 } ,$ FeS, and ${ \bf Z n S }$ ) have been used as co-catalysts for promoting the cycle of iron ions in Fenton reaction-\`\`surface defect sulfide’’ represented by $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ with very low $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ addition $\mathbf { \zeta } < 1 \mathbf { m } \mathbf { M } )$ (Cai et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023a; Yu et al., 2023b), with the dosage of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ 1–2 orders of magnitude lower than those of traditional Fenton-like systems (Dong et al., 2018; Gao et al., 2024b; Xing et al., 2018). The $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ co-catalyzed Fenton-like reactions can also be applied in the $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ feeding way of “small amount for multiple times” (Chen et al., 2022) with the degradation of sulfadiazine significantly increasing from $3 6 . 9 ~ \%$ to $9 1 . 1 ~ \%$ while using only $1 0 ~ \%$ of conventional $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ usage. As for the organic co-catalysts, conventional organic acids or amines could efficiently accelerate the rate-determining Fe(II)/Fe(II) circulation in various Fenton-like systems (Fukuchi et al., 2014). For example, the redox-active polymers could serve as efficient electron shuttles to promote the $\mathrm { F e ( I I I ) / F e ( I I ) }$ cycling and accelerate $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ activation (Zhou et al., 2023). Due to its appropriate redox capacity, polyaniline (PANi) was superior for Fe(III) reduction with only $2 { - } 1 0 ~ \%$ of conventional $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ consumption than other redox-active substances, such as polypyrrole, and hydroquinone resin. However, still few organic co-catalysts have been reported for actual Fenton-like scenarios so far, which may be due to the difficulty in recycling these organic co-catalysts

Basically, various inorganic composites or organic polymers can serve as co-catalysts to assist in the adsorption of organic molecules and to provide multiple electron migration pathways. As a result, these cocatalysts significantly improve the populations of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ in Fenton-like systems and maintain a stable $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ cycling, thus facilitating the activation of various peroxides such as $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and PMS (Zhu et al., 2020). This process can dramatically reduce the required amounts of peroxide to as little as one-tenth (or even less) compared to co-catalyst-free systems. Additionally, it can improve the degradation rate of organic pollutants by 1–2 orders of magnitude compared to systems without co-catalysts, showing promising application prospects in environmental remediation. As a result, these stable, recyclable, and efficient co-catalysts can induce the regenerating $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ from $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ , providing an environmentally friendly and highly innovative strategy for practical utilization. However, to date, there have been few studies on the long-term stability (more than 1 month) of the cocatalytic Fenton reaction. The longer the duration of Fenton reaction, the more engineering problems such as cocatalyst flocculation, metal ion dissolution and wastewater color deepening are exposed. Therefore, how to solve the long-term stable operation of these cocatalysts is still a key issue to be considered in the future.

# 3. Nonradical pathways for lowering peroxide consumption

The reactive oxygen species produced by the reaction process of Fenton-like technology based on various peroxides are mainly divided into two categories: radicals (e.g., $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and HO• ) and nonradical species/pathways (singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ , high-valent metal species and electron transfer processes (ETP), polymerization, etc.) (Li et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2023c; Yang et al., 2017). Although the molecules of refractory organics can be oxidized by the radical-based pathways, the life and migration distance of free radicals are limited, resulting in a low peroxide utilization rate (Yang et al., 2017). Nonradical pathways mediated by $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , high-valent metal species and ETP not only exhibit longer lifetime, but also demonstrate great immunity to the interference of anions and natural organic matter due to its high selectivity toward specific pollutants (Li et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023d). These features are particularly important in improving the utilization efficiency of various nonradical species triggered from the peroxides and thus reducing the amounts of peroxides. In recent years, regulating versatile AOP systems to mediate nonradical pathways has become a research focus in water treatment (He et al., 2021; Li et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2024b; Zhu et al., 2020). The precise analysis of consumption and utilization of peroxides in these nonradical mechanisms will help establish green Fenton-like systems with economic peroxide consumption. Although the high selectivity and strong environmental adaptability of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ can achieve a high peroxide utilization rate in $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ -dominant catalytic systems, its lower oxidation kinetics raise concerns. The role of traditional catalysts, especially carbonaceous catalysts, in activating PMS/PDS to generate $^ { \bar { 1 } } 0 _ { 2 }$ for effective peroxide reduction remains controversial (Kohantorabi et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b; Xiao et al., 2023b). This is because lowering the peroxide consumption in $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ -dominant systems required the high $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ production efficiency to compensate for its inferior kinetics $( k = \sim 1 0 ^ { 2 } - 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ compared with radicals $( k = 1 0 ^ { 6 } - 1 0 ^ { 1 1 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ (Lee et al., 2020; Wu and Kim, 2022), which is closely associated to the properties of the catalysts (Kohantorabi et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b). In recent studies, $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ -dominant systems with high $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ abundance have also been constructed by regulating various catalytic sites and structures (such as the size of metal sites and metal-nonmetal coordination configurations) or integrating with other strategies (e.g., confinement catalysis, and reactors engineering) (Mi et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023b; Yang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021a). These approaches have achieved superior catalytic activities with lower peroxide consumption in $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ -dominant oxidation systems. For example, the SACs-anchored $\mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ exhibited densely loaded and highly uniform metal active sites, which facilitated efficient PMS activation under low PMS consumption $\ l < 0 . 0 5$ mmol $\mathbf { m } \mathbf { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ for $1 0 0 \%$ production of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ (Zhang et al., 2021a). Additionally, $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ could be generated via $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ activation by the $\mathrm { F e _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ -embedded carbon nanotubes under nanoconfinement (Yang et al., 2019). As a result, the substrate-specific selectivity of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ with low peroxide consumption can be achieved in some AOPs water treatment systems to achieve selective degradation of electron-rich antibiotic contaminants, as well as amino acids containing aromatic or sulfur functions in specific proteins (Dolmans et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2009; Manjon ´ et al., 2008; Yao et al., 2022).

# 3.2. High-valent metal-oxo oxidation $( M ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = O )$

A series of $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ species, including $\mathrm { F e ^ { ( V ) } }$ , $\mathrm { F e ^ { ( I V ) } }$ , ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { ( \mathrm { I I I } ) }$ , ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { \mathrm { { ( I V ) } } }$ , $\mathrm { M n } ^ { \mathrm { ( V ) } }$ , and $\mathrm { N i ^ { ( I V ) } }$ (Gao et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021b; Sharma et al., 2023; Zong et al., 2020), have been reported as the dominated ROS in some dissolved metal ions, metal single-atom or metal oxides/nanoparticles systems via two-electron transfer reaction with the breakage of peroxy bond (O–O) in various peroxides (i.e., $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , PDS, PMS, and PAA) (Li et al., 2023c; Liu et al., 2021b; Song et al., 2023; Wu and Kim, 2022). $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ species have been widely concerned in Fenton-like chemistry because of their mild oxidation capacities (e.g., $\mathrm { E ^ { 0 } ( F e ^ { ( I V ) } / F e ^ { ( I I I ) } ) = }$ $1 . 8 \mathrm { V } )$ , high selectivity, long lifetime (i.e., ${ \sim } 7 s$ of $\mathrm { F e ^ { ( I V ) } }$ , at $\mathrm { \ p H } = 3 \mathrm { \cdot }$ , and superior anti-interference compared with radicals (Liu et al., 2021b; Song et al., 2023; Wu and Kim, 2022). For example, it was reported that cobalt ions could undergo a two-electron transfer (oxygen-atom transfer) with PAA to produce ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { \mathrm { { ( I V ) } } }$ under acidic conditions (Liu et al., 2021a). Due to the strong oxidation capacity $( E _ { 0 } = 1 . 8 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ and selectivity of ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { \mathrm { { ( I V ) } } }$ , the cobalt-based PAA system can efficiently oxidize electron-rich pollutants with strong resistance to inorganic interfering ions and humic acids in the substrates, resulting in a high ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { \mathrm { { ( I V ) } } }$ utilization rate (Liu et al., 2021a). Similar to the literature on $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ -dominated systems, there exist some controversies around the peroxide consumption of $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ species. Although high utilization of $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ can be achieved, the kinetics $( k = 1 0 ^ { 2 } - 1 0 ^ { 4 } \mathbf { \bar { M } } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ of $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ toward common organic pollutants were much lower than radicals $( k = 1 0 ^ { 6 } ~ -$ $1 0 ^ { 1 1 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ , even slower than $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ( k = \sim 1 0 ^ { 2 } \mathrm { - } 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { ~ M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ , which theoretically requires more peroxides for equivalent efficacy in $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + }$ ${ } = \mathbf { O }$ -dominated systems regardless of other factors (Lee et al., 2020; Wu and Kim, 2022). The degradation efficiencies of refractory organics by various $\mathbf { M } ^ { \left( n + 2 \right) + } \mathbf { \Lambda } = \mathbf { \Lambda } 0$ require comprehensive assessments of their oxidizing capacity, selectivity, lifetime as well as abundance in the catalytic systems (Lee et al., 2020; Wu and Kim, 2022). The cleavage of $\mathrm { O - H }$ and $\mathrm { o - } \mathrm { o }$ bonds in peroxide and efficient electron transfer with the metal center are critical for the formation of $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ species in PMS/PDS or PAA systems (Song et al., 2023; Wu and Kim, 2022). Similar to $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 } ,$ targeted structural designs of catalysts as well as combined strategies (e.g., confined catalysis and active sites engineering) to achieve effective and selective generation of $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ are essential to utilize the advantages of high selectivity and long lifetime of $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } =$ O to realize the low peroxide consumption systems (Song et al., 2023).

# 3.3. Electron transfer pathway (ETP)

Compared with the generation of ROS (i.e., $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , $_ \mathrm { H O } \cdot$ , $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and highvalent metal-oxo) due to persulfate abstracting one or two electrons from catalysts (Cao et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2024c; Jiang et al., 2023; Miao et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2021; Wu and Kim, 2022; Yin et al., 2023a; Yin et al., 2023b; Zhang et al., 2022), the ETP oxidation is triggered by directly extracting two electrons from the pollutants to the persulfate (i. e., PMS/PDS)/catalyst complexes with the catalysts serving as the electron shuttles or complexes directly capturing electrons from adjacent pollutants (Lai et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022). These ROS will be rapidly produced between the catalysts and persulfates, but their survival times are short, and they are also easily quenched by environmental media, resulting in a low utilization rate of persulfates (Wu and Kim, 2022). In contrast to the radical, $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and high-valent metal-oxo, ETP is a direct reaction between the pollutants and persulfate (or persulfate can be only efficiently consumed upon interacting with pollutants), guaranteeing a very high persulfate utilization efficiency (Khiem et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023d; Ren et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Xin et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023c). Compared with conventional AOPs, the stoichiometry between the consumed persulfate and the target pollutants can be significantly reduced (always less than $1 0 ~ \%$ of conventional peroxide usages), thus helping to reduce the chemical inputs in the ETP-based AOPs systems (Fig. 6a) (Miao et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2021; Wu and Kim, 2022; Yin et al., 2023a). In addition to low persulfate consumption, ETP also has some unique behaviors over radical oxidation in removing pollutants, including but not limited to the following aspects (Duan et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2024a,b; Lee et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023b; Zeng et al., 2024b): (i) various ETP systems show high selectivity to electron-rich pollutants, but are always impeded for the degradation of pollutants with electron-withdrawing groups or an high half-wave/ironized potential; (ii) ETP systems will not produce toxic halogenated products even in high concentrations of halides; (iii) ETP oxidation can maintain its excellent efficiency in complex water substrates within a wide pH operating condition; (iv) the oxidation capacity of the ETP pathway towards the pollutants is adjustable, regulated by the physicochemical structure/composition of the catalysts (e.g. metal structure, heteroatoms and defects). Basically, there persulfate-based ETP pathway can be triggered over a wide array of carbon-based materials (e.g., pristine carbon catalysts, surface modified carbonaceous catalysts, biomass-derived carbonaceous materials) (Duan et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2020; Mao et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2024a), nanosized metals/metal oxides (e.g., $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ , $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 } )$ (Guo et al., 2022; Lai et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022), and metal/carbon hybrids (Liu et al., 2023d; Yin et al., 2023a; Yin et al., 2023c; Zeng et al., 2024b).

# 3.3.1. ETP pathway triggered by carbon-based materials

The ETP-dominated oxidation reaction was discovered in an NCNT/ PMS system (Duan et al., 2015). The $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ carbons with conjugated $\pi$ and great conductivity are beneficial to the ETP pathway than $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 }$ carbons. In addition, the graphitic carbon networks in the pristine carbon catalysts (e.g., rGO, CNTs, nanodiamond) not only exhibit strong adsorption toward the benzene ring of organics via $\pi - \pi$ interactions, but also contain abundant free-flowing electrons to facilitate ETP from the adsorbed organics to the surface-activated persulfate. Basically, these pristine carbon catalysts primarily serve as model catalysts for exploring the mechanism of ETP coordinated by specific carbons.

Recent studies reported that the surface modifications, including defect engineering and heteroatom doping, are effective strategies to induce more active sites and improve the ETP-based catalytic reactions with economic peroxide consumption (Cheng et al., 2019; Duan et al., 2015; Figueiredo and Pereira, 2010; Hotova ´ et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019). Moreover, surface defects can provide coordinatively unsaturated carbon sites with partially localized electrons to mediate redox reactions (Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2019). Nitrogen doping has been most intensively applied to improve the performance of carbocatalysts due to (i) the similar atomic radius $( 0 . { \bar { 7 } } 5 { \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } }$ of nitrogen vs. 0.77 Å of carbon) to obtain high doping levels, and (ii) the different electronegativity (3.04 of nitrogen vs. 2.55 of carbon) to regulate the electronic microenvironment for creating polarized sites/regions and increasing the conductivity of carbocatalysts (Cheng et al., 2019; Figueiredo and Pereira, 2010; Hotova ´ et al., 2018). In addition to nitrogen doping, other heteroatoms, such as oxygen, boron, sulfur, silicon and phosphorus, can also fine-tune the activity of carbocatalysts (Ding et al., 2020; Miao et al., 2020). For example, co-modification of Si and O atoms at the C site can effectively enhance the interaction between the reactants and the Si–O–C substrate, which served as the main PMS-binding and electron-donating sites for the low-PMS-consumption ETP oxidation (lower than $0 . 0 1 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ) (Mao et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024a). The introduction of these heteroatoms could efficiently govern the spin density and charge distribution of carbon lattice that facilitated the binding of PMS or pollutants. Consequently, the enhanced binding of PMS or pollutants would further elevate the oxidative potential of carbocatalysts and promote the ETP oxidation kinetics for lowering PMS consumption (Lee et al., 2017; Ren et al., 2020a).

# 3.3.2. ETP pathway triggered by nanosized metals/metal oxides

As compared with carbon-based substrates, due to the poor bulk conductivity of metal oxides (semiconductor), the electron shuttle pathway through the catalyst is impeded, and adjacent persulfatepollutant electron transfer is the dominant oxidation route (Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020a). Thus, metal oxides always trigger radical oxidation and exhibit a much more tardive ETP than highly conductive materials (e.g., noble metals and graphitic carbons) (Ahn et al., 2019; Ahn et al., 2016; Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020b). Recently, it was reported that the ETP pathway can also be induced by modulating the physicochemical properties of metal oxides, such as defective sites (e.g., oxygen and iodine vacancies), crystal structures, and electronic configurations (Zeng et al., 2024a). The catalytic behavior of metal oxides is closely related to their surface functional groups, coordination geometry and electronic structure, which would change with the crystal structure of metal oxides. Therefore, the crystal modulation strategy of metal oxides can effectively fine-tune metal oxide-mediated pathways in Fenton-like oxidation from radical oxidation to ETP pathway, thus reducing the consumption of persulfate (Guo et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2023b). In fact, the catalytic pathways for persulfate activation can be determined by both the crystallinity-dependent surface atomic coordination and electronic structures of metal oxides (Feng et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2023b). The electron delocalization of the metal active sites on the surface was a key drive for its ETP-based nonradical oxidation. For example, the amorphous/crystalline Co-Mn spinel oxides $( \mathsf { A } / \mathsf { C } \cdot \mathsf { C o M n O } _ { x } )$ exhibited the most favorable PMS binding capacity and charge transfer energy, which could trigger regulable radical/ETP reactions for the degradation of pollutants (Fig. 6b).53 Such an electron delocalization-triggered ETP pathway could realize ultrafast pollutant degradation with only $5 \mathrm { - } 1 0 ~ \%$ of PMS consumption as compared with metal-induced radical oxidation (Feng et al., 2022).

# 3.3.3. ETP pathway triggered by metal/carbon hybrids

Based on the nature of ETP, the kinetics of organic oxidation depend on the electron population and charge-transfer rates, which correspond to persulfate input and consumption rate (Ren et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2023). Hence, persulfate is adsorbed on the surface of catalysts to form reactive surface complexes to trigger the ETP rather than being decomposed into radicals. Most metal-based systems have relatively low peroxides adsorption capacity compared to carbonaceous materials with higher persulfate adsorption capacity because carbonaceous materials have stronger persulfate-catalyst interaction and larger specific surface area and pore structures than metal catalysts.(Ren et al., 2022) As a result, some metal/carbon hybrids have been developed for integrating carbon-based materials and metal sites (Meng et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024a; Yu et al., 2024b; Zeng et al., 2024b). These metal/carbon hybrids always exhibited the synergy of ETP and radicals with other nonradical pathways (e.g., $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = \mathbf { \bar { O } }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } )$ (Guo et al., 2024a,b; Yin et al., 2024a; Yin et al., 2023b; Yin et al., 2023c). Recent studies have found that downsizing the metal sites on carbon substrates could enhance the metal− support interaction that facilitates charge transfer for triggering the persulfate-based ETP pathways. For example, downsizing the confined metal nanoparticles to ultra-fine clusters or atomic levels could improve the degradation performances by 1–2 orders of magnitude increase and realize a mechanism transformation from radical oxidation to nonradical ETP (Shang et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023c). There are several possible reasons explaining this phenomenon: (i) Strong co-adsorption facilitate the mass transfer of both persulfate and pollutants to facilitate ETP over the surface region from the kinetic aspect (Duan et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2024b); (ii) Highly dispersed metal sites will fine-tuned molecular coordination regulated the peroxide activation behavior to form surface complex without decomposition, which will elevate the surface potential and trigger ETP from the thermodynamic perspective (Wu and Kim, 2022); (iii) Improved bulk conductivity of the composites due to the downsized metal species facilitated the electron shuttle between the single metal atoms and carbon substrates, exhibiting a much stronger ETP pathway than metal NPs/carbon materials (Shang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023c; Yin et al., 2023a; Yin et al., 2023c; Zeng et al., 2024b). The significantly enhanced electron shuttling in the single metal atoms centered carbon substrates can immediately promote the utilizaiton efficiency of persulfate by adding appropriate amounts of organic pollutants, thereby significantly reducing the persulfate input (Yin et al., 2023a).

# 3.3.4. Scale-up application of persulfate-based ETP systems

Based on the ETP pathway, contaminants can be degraded by transferring electrons through ligands formed between catalysts and peroxides (such as PMS and PDS), and the contaminant solution can be purified in a separate cell/reactor without direct contact with peroxides during this process (Chen et al., 2024d; Ren et al., 2022). Therefore, this provides a strategy to solve the secondary pollution caused by by-products (e.g., excessive persulfate and high concentration of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } \Upsilon$ ) produced by persulfate-based catalytic systems. A series of galvanic oxidation systems have been developed with persulfate and pollutants placed in two separated cells connecting via an electron channel (Chai et al., 2024; Chai et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024a). Such small-scale reactors provided the direct evidence of the ETP pathway between the pollutants and metastable PMS/catalysts complexes with high removal efficiency without direct interactions. The reactor scale-up based on the ETP mechanism is the key to realizing the application of ETP in large-scale applications. Yu et al., developed a module packed with electrodes loaded with copper single atom catalysts to oxidize and degrade organic pollutants in gross water $\scriptstyle ( > 2 0 0 \mathrm { ~ L ~ } )$ , while transferring PDS into an isolated floating chamber (Yu et al., 2024b). The floating chamber and the treated water body are connected through wires and proton exchange membranes, which can realize the efficient degradation of electron-donating pollutants while only requiring $2 0 \%$ of batch system. In addition, the generated sulfate in the separate cell will not enter the water body, and can be recycled as $\mathrm { C a S O _ { 4 } }$ for engineering use. Compared to similar traditional techniques for ex-situ processing, the technology based on the ETP strategy is more convenient, with costs about $4 \%$ of off-site treatment (Yu et al., 2024b). This ETP-based module could also be used for other commercial catalysts, such as carbon nanotubes and copper oxide.

In summary, the ETP process provides a general strategy to prevent direct release of peroxide additives into the aquatic environment, thereby eliminating the potential environmental hazards associated with AOPs. More interestingly, ETP-based modules (e.g., divided tank reactors) can be easily integrated into large-scale water treatment because of the low peroxide consumption as well as on-site long-term wastewater treatment by regularly replacing the peroxide (Gao et al., 2024b; Yang et al., 2022a; Yin et al., 2023a; Yin et al., 2023b; Zhang et al., 2024b). Nevertheless, the ETP process primarily relies on the oxidation of electron-donating pollutants, while its efficiency in oxidizing electron-withdrawing pollutants remains low, thereby constraining its applicability for pollutant removal.

# 3.4. Oxidative polymerization process

Recent studies on the AOPs systems have revealed polymerization pathways, where organics will be transformed to organic radicals and as monomers which undergo coupling reactions toward polymerization, resulting in polymeric products.58 The oxidative polymerization process is fundamentally different from the adsorption and traditional AOPs in the pathways of pollutant removal and product distribution (Chen et al., 2024e; Zhang et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2023d; Zhang et al., 2022). In polymerization process, the catalysts can interact with peroxides (mainly PMS or PDS) to trigger several types of ROS in the polymerization of organic matters, including surface $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and HO• , $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } , \mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + }$ $\qquad = \mathrm { ~ o ~ }$ , persulfate‒catalyst complexes (Zhang et al., 2024a), thus achieving the partial oxidation or transformation of the pollutants (Chen et al., 2024e; Zhang et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2022). In addition, oxidative polymerization process requires low peroxide consumption (even without peroxides, which is distinguished from the ETP and $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ pathways), produces value-added byproducts, performs high selectivity toward target pollutants, and causes low $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emisions in watertreatment (Zhang et al., 2023d; Zhang et al., 2022). Recent studies also reported that the polymerization reaction in AOPs is influenced by multiple factors, including the radical/nonradical pathways, catalyst types, and properties of organics (e.g., molecular structures, functional groups) (Zhang et al., 2024a). As a result, there are significant knowledge gaps in understanding different polymerization processes within AOPs for lowering the peroxide consumptions.

It should be pointed out that various peroxides (typically PMS, PDS, or $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } )$ can be attached to the surface of catalysts to initiate a polymerization reaction and activated via electric field, ultrasound, heat, homogeneous or heterogeneous Fenton agents (Chen et al., 2024e; Li et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022b; Mei et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024a). Radicals (e.g., $\mathrm { { \ s O _ { 4 } ^ { bullet - } / H O ^ { \bullet } } }$ generated from these systems can also induced polymerization products, requiring optimized reaction conditions, i.e. high concentrations of organic substrates and radical initiators (e.g., peroxides and catalysts) (Bai et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024e; Lee et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022c; Zhang et al., 2023b; Zhang et al., 2024a). Basically, radicals-based ROS would primarily induce the mineralization of pollutants, and the detected polymerization products are often degradation by-products (Chen et al., 2024e; Shang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a). Polymerization via radical systems always occurred in the presence of insufficient free radicals. Therefore, despite the fact that the persulfate usage is low, the pollutant removal efficiency is typically unsatisfactory, and partial polymeric products can be further mineralized.

# 3.4.1. ETP-based polymerization process regulated by metal-based catalysts

The PMS or PDS can directly and efficiently bond with the catalysts to form the PMS/PDS‒catalyst complexes by substituting the hydroxyl group $\left( - \mathrm { O H } \right)$ on the catalyst surface (Chen et al., 2024e; Zhang et al., 2024a). The complexes could directly initiate the polymerization process via electron shuttles. Both carbon and metal-based catalysts can activate PMS/PDS and generate the complexes to remove pollutants via lowering the PMS/PDS consumption (Yang et al., 2022b). Zhang et al., proposed the use of various nanocatalysts (i.e., $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ , $\bf { M n } _ { 2 } \bf { O } _ { 3 }$ , and $\mathrm { C u O } _ { x } )$ to remove organics via a non-decomposing direct oxidation pathway (DOTP) to enable surface-dependent coupling and polymerization (Zhang et al., 2023d; Zhang et al., 2022). A direct two-electron transfer reaction first happened between PMS and phenol to produce the phenoxonium ions. They can be stabilized on the catalyst surface and undergo transient resonance transfer, inducing stable phenoxonium ions to spontaneously undergo C–C coupling or C–O polymerization with phenol (Fig. 7a). The degradation/mineralization of pollutants via ROS attack in conventional AOPs are achieved by the expense of high energy or intensive chemical inputs that increase peroxide potential energy (Lei et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2021; Qi et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2024). As for the DOTP, it could also increase the potential differences between the peroxides and pollutants via a fundamentally different approach, i.e. the peroxides and pollutants could be synchronously activated by the catalysts (Zhang et al., 2022). By using the thermodynamic potential energy via DFT calculation, Zhang et al., confirmed that the active substances would undergo a spontaneous and direct redox reaction, forming the intermediates stabilized and accumulated on the catalyst surface through the polymerization and coupling reactions (Zhang et al., 2022). As a result, a very low peroxide (mainly based on PMS/PDS) consumption $( < 0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { m m o l \ m g ^ { - 1 } } )$ ) was required in the DOTP for the polymeric transformation of pollutants.

The universal existence of DOTP with low peroxide consumption was also confirmed in various catalytic oxidation systems, such as $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } /$ persulfate, $\mathrm { C u O } _ { x } ,$ /persulfate, and high-valent $\mathrm { M n O } _ { x }$ (i.e., $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ , $\scriptstyle \mathrm { M n } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 } ,$ , and $\bf { M n } _ { 3 } 0 _ { 4 } ,$ ) systems (Zhang et al., 2023d; Zhang et al., 2022). For example, PDS–CuO complexes could abstract one or more H atoms from the phenol, aniline or thiophene substrates and receive one electron from the above substrates, thereby promoting the formation of the radical monomers (i.e., a phenoxy radical) and pairing with other $\mathbb { R } ^ { \bullet }$ through a coupling reaction for the polymerization process (Wang et al.,

2021a). Especially for the high-valent $\mathrm { M n O } _ { x }$ (i.e., $\bf { M n } _ { 3 } \bf { O } _ { 4 }$ , $\mathrm { M n } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 } ,$ and $\mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } ) }$ , the heterogeneous high-valent $\mathtt { M n O } _ { x }$ provided unique surface properties to trigger a DOTP pathway without the addition of peroxides because the Mn(II, III) of $\mathrm { M n O } _ { x }$ could substitute PMS for receiving two electrons from phenol (Zhang et al., 2023d). The oxidized intermediates of pollutant were deposited onto the surface of $\mathrm { M n O } _ { x } ,$ subsequently experiencing polymerization and coupling reactions. These intermediates would accumulate on the $\mathrm { M n O } _ { x }$ surface until all the pollutants in the aqueous solution were completely removed (Fig. 7b). These new findings on the metal-based DOTP pathways provided new insights into the pollutant oxidation process at solid-water interface with low peroxide dosage (or no peroxide added), accounting for only less than $5 \mathrm { - } 1 0 ~ \%$ of theoretical peroxide consumption in radical-based mineralization systems (Fig. 7c).

# 3.4.2. ETP-based polymerization process regulated by carbon-based catalysts

As for the carbon-based catalytic systems, the non-radical ETP process can be easily triggered due to the elevated oxidation potential of the whole carbon surface via the formation of activated peroxide complexes, which can further initiate a radical chain reaction by selectively oxidizing organic monomers to the corresponding organic radicals without further oxidation (non-mineralization) (Chen et al., 2024e; Zhang et al., 2024a). The ETP process selectively extracts an electron from the organic matter (aromatic ring/thiophene ring), which combines with proton elimination to form the monomer radical as a key intermediate (Dou et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024a,b; Yin et al., 2023a; Yin et al., 2023b; Yin et al., 2023c). Subsequently, a series of coupling reactions can occur based on these monomer radicals to form various chain polymers (Fig. 8a) with less than $5 \%$ of peroxide consumption that theoretically required for radical mineralization (Dou et al., 2023). In addition, the strong adhesion of oligomer to the catalyst surface can also be emphasized and visualized (Fig. 8b and c), and the adhesion of product increases with the degree of polymerization (Fig. 8d) (Liu et al., 2024).

The polymerization reaction based on carbon-based catalysts is also closely related to the functional groups and graphitization degree of carbon materials (Chen et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019). Graphitic carbon networks (such as biochar, carbon nanotubes, and graphene) have excellent adsorption capacity to structures such as aromatic/thiophene rings due to their $\pi { - } \pi$ conjugation and hydrophobicity (Chen et al., 2020). The conjugated systems can also provide an abundance of free-flowing electrons and high electrical conductivity, allowing electrons to be transferred from surface-activated persulfates to adsorbed organic substitutes, thereby enhancing the polymerization process and further reducing the amounts of peroxides used in the catalytic systems (Ren et al., 2020b; Yu et al., 2023c). In addition, the intrinsic oxygen-containing functional groups of carbon-based catalysts can serve as the adsorption or catalytic active sites or regulate carbon framework activity during polymerization (Ren et al., 2022). Therefore, multiple factors collectively affect the adsorption and activation behaviors of carbonaceous catalysts, consequently, oxidation/polymerization pathways and kinetics. In addition, the layered porous structure can promote the adsorption and mass migration in ETP, and promote the coupling and polymerization kinetics in the nanoconfined pores, so the confined porous structure can achieve high catalytic activity at low peroxide dosages (Tian et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024c). For example, when UiO-66- $\mathrm { \cdot N H _ { 2 } }$ - $\mathrm { ( Z r / F e ) }$ nanoparticles were constrained by graphene aerogel substrates, the removal of phenol shifted from a conventional ring-opening pathway to a thermodynamically favorable oligomerization reaction (Zhang et al., 2024c). This nanoconfinement resulted in a high $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ result for phenol that was two orders of magnitude higher than that of the unconfined analogues, while requiring only $5 \%$ of the $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ usage.

Intriguingly, the nonradical AOPs systems can directly initiate the selective oxidation and polymerization reactions, with peroxide consumptions an order of magnitude lower or even less than those of radical-based mineralization systems (Chen et al., 2024e; Zhang et al., 2024a). The organics undergo a simple H-abstraction and electron-transfer process rather than deep oxidation into organic acids or $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } .$ , due to the relatively low oxidation capacity of surface complexes and nonradical ROS generated in polymerization-based reactions. In contrast to the traditional ring-opening route that requires excessive chemical inputs for complete mineralization, polymerization route opens an avenue to develop AOPs technologies with lowered chemical usage and carbon emission for rapid and complete pollutant removal. Nonetheless, the recovery of polymerized pollutants remains a significant challenge, as the polymerization process is often intricately linked to the ETP process. Consequently, this relationship introduces similar limitations inherent in ETP systems, particularly regarding strategies to enhance the efficacy of broad-spectrum pollutant removal.

# 4. Catalytic membranes/filters/beds

For laboratory-scale suspended Fenton-like catalytic systems (e.g., beakers and conical bottles), water-dispersed powder catalysts need to address the following challenges: (i) particle catalysts are easy to agglomerate, reducing the exposure of the active site; (ii) the low mass transfer efficiency reduces the oxidation capacity of the Fenton-like system and leads to more residual peroxides (low activation efficiency); (iii) the produced radicals cannot reach the target pollutants effectively, resulting in self-quenching of free radicals/nonradicals or side reactions (low utilization efficiency) (Ly et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020b). In addition to the rational design of catalysts and catalytic systems, improving the design and operation of reaction devices is essential for achieving effective peroxide reduction. This approach aims to enhance catalytic efficiency for generating large amounts of radicals/nonradicals, and more importantly, to facilitate the utilization efficiency of these reactive species. As a result, a series of catalytic devices, e.g., catalytic membrane/filters and fluidized reactors, have been inspired and developed for high reaction metrological efficiency with the advantage of low peroxide consumption as well as other advantages, such as easy recycling and reuse of treated materials, less secondary pollution to the environment, and great feasibility in actual water treatments (Chen et al., 2024c; Huang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023b; Zhu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023).

These membrane/filters catalytic units (such as polymer organic membranes, inorganic ceramic membranes, catalytic column, filters, etc.) with stable catalyst loading and high reaction activity can be constructed via facile strategies such as “self-support” and “polymercoating” (Guo et al., 2024a,b; Ly et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2022a; Yin et al., 2023a; Zhang et al., 2024d; Zhu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023). A large number of micro-channel structures in the membranes can effectively increase the mass transfer of pollutants and peroxides (Meng et al., 2022a). Combined with the local enrichment of micro-channels, the activation efficiency of peroxides can be further enhanced to rapidly generate massive radicals for in situ remediation within the pores, which could realize the simultaneous enhancement of membrane filtration and advanced oxidation technology (Meng et al., 2022a; Tian et al., 2024). Therefore, the bottleneck of low radical production efficiency and inferior radical utilization in the radical-dominated systems can be effectively solved by the catalytic membrane/filters, with the reaction kinetics increasing by more than several orders of magnitude compared with the traditional heterogeneous Fenton-like systems (Meng et al., 2022a; Tian et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2020b). Some catalytic devices with co-catalysis systems $( \mathrm { M o S } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { P M S }$ or $\mathrm { M o S _ { 2 } / H _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } }$ have been developed for complex environments (Wang et al., 2024c; Zhu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023). Aiming at the problem that a large amount of dissolved organic matters (DOM) in water can easily lead to membrane fouling, Wang et al., used iron salt $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } )$ as a coagulant to remove these DOM to reduce membrane fouling (Fig. 9a), and further fabricated the $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ nanosheets-anchored membrane by the combination of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ cycle to drive the low-PMS-consumption Fenton system $\left( 0 . 0 4 ~ \mathrm { m m o l } ~ \mathrm { m g } ^ { - 1 } \right.$ $5 \mathrm { - } 1 0 ~ \%$ of conventional PMS consumption) (Wang et al., 2024c). In addition, some pilot-scale experiments based on these co-catalysis devices have been further carried out for field treatments (Zhu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023). Typical co-catalyst (i.e., commercial $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ ) could be constructed on the sponge skeleton, loaded into polypropylene suspension spheres (Fig. 9b and c), or made into large-sized sponge catalyst modules (Zhu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023). Such co-catalysis modules can effectively remove high concentrations of COD and total suspended solids in dehydration wastewater with low $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ dosage (Fig. 9d). Furthermore, previous studies have shown that precise control over the structures and substrate chemistry of the membrane can significantly improve the dispersion of catalytic sites and promote the mass transfer of radicals (Ly et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2022a; Meng et al., 2022b). For example, $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ functionalized anodized alumina oxides with precise pore sizes exhibited different degradation performances via activating $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ (Zhang et al., 2020b). Combined with the meter simulation of free radical spatial distribution, the concentration of hydroxyl free radical is closely related to the pore sizes of anodized alumina oxide. This pore size dependence would significantly affect the short-term diffusion of radicals and pollutants, thereby resulting in regulated $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ utilization efficiencies. These results pave the way for further assessment of catalytic membranes/filters for lowering peroxide consumption.

The fluidized beds are packed with granular catalysts, and the solid particles are suspended by gas or liquid through the granular solid layer to facilitate the gas-solid reaction process or liquid-solid reaction process, which can effectively improve the reaction efficiency and mass transfer with effective radical generation and high-efficiency radical utilization (Cai et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2024a; Lin et al., 2023). The prerequisite for the application of Fenton-like fluidized bed is that the powder catalyst should be prepared as a granular structure, which is more conducive to the circulated mass transfer of the fluid flow, thereby improving the catalytic activity and avoiding the blockage of the fluidized bed (Huang et al., 2022). For instance, the CuFeMnO catalyst was laden on the commercial ceramsite employed in a pilot-scale fluidized bed, which could maximize the PMS utilization with stable and superior degradation activity towards the organic pollutants in field wastewaters (Huang et al., 2022).

In summary, engineered catalytic devices can (i) promote the mass transfer and decomposition of various peroxides to improve the yield of radicals/nonradicals under low usages of peroxides, and (ii) greatly shorten the migration distance of radicals/nonradicals to the target pollutants, ensuring the effective contact between free radicals/nonradicals and pollutants within the half-life, to achieve high intensity and intensive space utilization of these reactive species. Therefore, catalytic efficiency can be significantly improved through catalytic devices under the condition of low peroxide consumption. However, it must be pointed out that these devices are mainly based on the laboratory scale, and catalytic devices for large-scale applications are still rare. Only a few magnified sponge modules loaded with $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ in co-catalytic systems or ETP-based modules (e.g., divided tank reactors) have been reported for practical applications (Xing et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023).

# 5. Summary and perspective

Recent strategies for lowering peroxide consumption are mainly devoted to effective radical generation and high-efficiency radical utilization through confinement and reactor/catalyst engineering strategies or the regulation of nonradical mechanisms for lowering peroxide consumption with higher selectivity and longer life. As for the radical pathways, a series of catalysts/ reactor/system engineering strategies (i. e., modulation of metal loading and dispersion, DRC catalysis, confinement catalysis, co-catalysis systems) have been used to activate various peroxides (such as $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ , PMS, PDS, PAA) for generating highdensity free radicals and high-efficiency radical utilization. Nonradical oxidation pathways ensure a relatively long life for peroxide during remediation and can resist the interference of anions and natural organic matters because of the mild oxidation capacity and high selectivity. Therefore, the highly selective property of nonradical oxidation is particularly important in improving the utilization efficiency of nonradical pathways and thus reducing the amounts of peroxides. Although various strategies for lowering the peroxide consumption based on radical/nonradical pathways have been proposed, some standing challenges and perspectives in future research are listed as follows.

The ultimate goal of developing various strategies for lowering peroxide consumption is to implement them in real and large-scale water treatment. Many strategies for achieving effective radical generation and high-efficiency radical utilization are mainly based on the rational design of catalytic sites or catalyst structures. The complex fabrication routines as well as the high costs of these catalysts would impede their actual deployment. In addition, Fenton-like catalytic devices are the key to the application of various efficient catalysts in practical water treatment systems. In contrast to the complex design of various catalysts, the combination of catalytic devices with economic peroxide consumption can better enhance the applicability. Reactor engineering can promote the decomposition of various peroxides to improve the yield of radicals/nonradical reactive species and greatly shorten their migration distances to the target pollutants, ensuring effective contact between radicals/nonradicals and pollutants to achieve efficient utilization of these species. Therefore, these catalytic devices can achieve superior catalytic efficiency with economic peroxide consumption. In fact, there have been a few examples of commercial catalysts (i.e., $\mathrm { F e } _ { 0 } , \mathrm { M o S } _ { 2 } )$ based catalytic devices for pilot-scale or large-scale applications, but large-scale applications of catalytic devices loaded with other high-efficiency catalysts are rarely reported. In addition, the use of scaled-up devices in large-scale trials or engineering systems also poses challenges to the design and cost of these devices.

The high selectivity and strong environmental adaptability of the nonradical pathways (e.g., $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0 )$ can achieve high peroxide utilization rates in $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ or $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ -dominant catalytic systems. However, lowering the peroxide consumption in 1 O2 or M(n+2)+ $= 0$ -dominant catalytic systems required the high $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ or $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ production efficiency to compensate for their inferior kinetics. As a result, targeted structural designs of catalysts as well as combined strategies (e.g., confinement catalysis and system/reactor engineering) to achieve fast formation and selective conversion of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ or $\mathbf { M } ^ { \left( n + 2 \right) + } = \mathbf { \bar { O } }$ are required, to fully leverage their advantages of high selectivity and long lifetime to realize the economic peroxide consumptions based on $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ or $\mathbf { M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ oxidation. In contrast, ETP typically can be realized on well-defined catalysts (such as carbons and transition metal oxides). At present, there are already cases of large-scale ETP treatment in water through electrode devices or divided tank reactors. In addition to the use of these catalytic devices, how to promote the qualitative regulation of ETP for lowering the peroxide consumption without significantly increasing the cost of the catalyst and sacrificing the stability remains challenging and needs further consideration. It is worth noting that the mechanism study on polymerization processes in AOPs is still in its early stages, and most polymerization processes are based on PS-derived ETP or $\mathbf { \bar { M } } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = 0$ pathways. In general, low chemical inputs of polymerization processes (only less than $1 0 ~ \%$ of conventional PS-AOPs) can induce rapid removal of micropollutants from water. Future studies of polymerization technologies will focus on the oxidative removal of industrial wastewater rich in heterocyclic pollutants and its selective conversion to polymer by-products. The polymer or coupled intermediate will adhere to the surface of the catalyst, effectively accelerating the conversion of organic carbon from a soluble toxic contaminant to a solid non-toxic polymer for facile separation.

Many studies have used DFT calculations to derive how specific catalyst structures enhance or regulate the formation of radicals and nonradicals (i.e., ${ } ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } , { \bf M } ^ { ( n + 2 ) + } = \bar { 0 }$ , ETP, polymerization) to achieve a series of Fenton-like reactions with low peroxide consumption. However, the models of catalytic sites as well as the adsorption models towards different peroxides are always over-optimized in these simulation processes, especially in analyzing the peroxide activation mechanisms of complex catalysts (e.g., multi-component materials, amorphous materials, and polycrystallines). Therefore, it is not always feasible to explain the mechanism of low peroxide consumption in these catalytic systems through DFT calculations. Therefore, to describe or predict the catalytic process with low peroxide consumption, more complex calculation methods need to be combined. For example, molecular dynamics can simulate the real surface dynamic reaction process, and the number of molecules can be set according to the reaction conditions, which can be used as a DFT method to study the low peroxide consumption process. On the other hand, the combination of specific experimental studies with DFT calculations is also one of the keys to clarifying the catalytic mechanism of low peroxide consumption, which has also been successful in revealing the reaction mechanism and rationalizing the observed reaction trends. In addition, DFT computation-based and datadriven strategies, including machine learning and computational highthroughput screening, can be used to predict and screen catalyst performance, explore various oxidation reaction mechanisms, and describe how they can be used to achieve low peroxide consumption under different mechanisms. Data-driven machine learning methods can learn patterns from complex data. Therefore, the data set of strategy-catalyst performance-peroxide consumption as well as targeted pollutants can be constructed by collecting the data of catalyst structures, pollutant parameters and properties of different strategies as mentioned above and corresponding peroxide dosages in the published literature (Fig. 10). By optimizing the model parameters and comparing the results generated by multiple models, the different impact features are analyzed hierarchically to enhance the explainability of the model. Finally, a model is constructed that can predict the most cost-effective peroxide consumption for targeted pollutants or application scenarios under different strategies.
