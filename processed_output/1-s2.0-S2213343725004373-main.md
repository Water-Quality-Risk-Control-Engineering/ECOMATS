# . Introduction

With the rapid socio-economic development and the increasing demand for medical and health care, antibiotic drugs are mass-produced and widely used to treat diseases in living organisms. Research indicates that $5 \mathrm { - } 9 0 \%$ of antibiotics entering the human or animal body are excreted in urine or faeces as primary structures or metabolites in urine or faeces. Eventually, they enter water bodies and soil environments through domestic, medical, and aquaculture wastewater [1,2]. Tetracycline antibiotics are challenging to biodegrade due to their stable benzene ring structure. At the same time, they kill the active microorganisms in the sewage, reducing the biochemical performance and forming a vicious circle of water quality deterioration. The prolonged presence of such compounds disrupts the ecological balance of water bodies and is transmitted to humans through the food chain, posing a potential risk to public health. Given the potential hazards of tetracycline pollutants, there is an urgent need to develop efficient and environmentally friendly water treatment technologies to reduce their concentration in the aquatic environment.

The treatment methods for tetracycline primarily included physical, chemical, and biological approaches. Physical processes are straightforward to operate but often lead to secondary pollution [3]. Biological methods are cost-effective but limited by reaction times and environmental conditions [4]. Chemical methods, incredibly advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), are highly effective and widely used for treating recalcitrant organics by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that break down pollutants into less harmful substances [5]. Among these, AOPs based on persulfates (PS) hold substantial practical application potential in water treatment. This is attributed to their outstanding oxidative-reductive capabilities, suitability across a wide pH range, and longer half-lives, enabling organic compounds' complete oxidation and degradation. It is characterized by the activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) or peroxydisulfate (PDS) to trigger the generation of sulfate radicals $( S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet } )$ and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) for the oxidative degradation of organic matter by thermal treatment [6], ultrasound [7] and transition metals [8]. Previous studies indicated that homogeneous metal ions such as $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ could activate persulfate and rapidly degrade pollutants [9,10]. However, their application could have been improved due to difficulties in recovery, reagent waste and high sensitivity to water quality [11]. To address these drawbacks, research efforts focused on developing heterogeneous catalysts, including carbon-based, metal, and composite catalysts (Table S1). Bimetallic-carbon composites demonstrated superior catalytic activity and stability due to their tunable properties and synergistic effects [12, 13].

Biochar is a carbonaceous material prepared under oxygen-limited pyrolysis conditions. Following appropriate modifications, it can offer a diversified array of active sites, surface functional groups, and layered porous structures. Therefore, biochar is extensively employed as a heterogeneous catalyst for activating PS [14,15]. Coffee grounds contain caffeine and tannic acid, which can pollute the environment if not properly treated. Biochar derived from coffee grounds by pyrolysis has emerged as a potential catalytic material for PMS activation owing to its structural characteristics and oxygen-containing functional groups [16]. Furthermore, metal modification can enhance the surface active sites of biochar, thereby significantly boosting its catalytic capability and reaction efficiency. For instance, Zhu et al. successfully enhanced biochar's electrochemical performance and electron transfer capability by modifying it with Mn/Fe layered double hydroxides (BC-LDH). They achieved highly efficient removal of ciprofloxacin with a removal efficiency as high as $9 4 ~ \%$ [17]. Xu et al. synthesized a three-dimensional flower-like zero-valent bimetallic shell/core-iron/copper/biochar composite material (NZVI/CuO/BC), obtaining complete removal of metronidazole $( 1 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L } )$ within 6 min through PMS activation [18]. Despite their high reactivity, powdered catalytic materials faced limitations in practical applications due to complex preparation, scale-up challenges, and post-reaction separation difficulties.

Previous studies indicated that incorporating suitable framework materials (e.g., sodium alginate, chitosan) into metal-based catalysts enhanced the particle size, facilitating effective separation and recovery from treated water. Sodium alginate, a natural linear polysaccharide, could chelate with multivalent metal cations to form hydrogels. However, alginate beads often exhibited poor mechanical strength and risked breaking during use. Including fillers such as biochar, metals, and oxides within alginate gel systems improved the mechanical strength of hydrogels and mitigated the recovery challenges associated with nanomaterials [19]. Iron, abundant, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly, demonstrated high catalytic performance in Fe(II)/alginate heterogeneous catalysts in prior studies, although its stability was affected by low pH. To overcome this limitation, new methods must be explored to maintain iron's efficacy as a catalyst under neutral pH conditions [20]. Lanthanum (La), a rare earth element with a unique electronic structure, was reported to enhance composite degradation efficiency for dyes at neutral $\mathrm { p H }$ , reduce iron leaching, and extend the composite's lifespan [21]. The presence of metal oxides in composites prepared through co-pyrolysis of lotus root with Fe/La salts promoted the exposure of oxygen-containing functional groups and aromatic structures, altering the catalyst's surface and structural properties [22]. To date, no feasibility studies have investigated the activation of PMS using millimetre-scale biochar-based Fe/La metal oxide composites.

Research in this area could further simplify the catalyst preparation process, enhance its practical application potential, and provide new insights and solutions for the more effective treatment of organic pollutants in aquatic environments.

This study, therefore, employed a gel calcination method to prepare magnetic biochar-iron lanthanum crosslinked alginate beads (Fe/ $\mathbf { L a } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { C B } )$ composite material, utilizing low-cost coffee ground waste as an effective catalyst for PMS. The aim was to evaluate the potential of the composite material with the macroscopic support structure and good stability in treating antibiotics in wastewater. The study was carried out in the following aspects: (1) investigating the effects of preparation conditions and reaction conditions on the performance of the Fe/ La@BCB/PMS system for TCH removal; (2) analyzing the stability and recyclability of Fe/La@BCB, as well as the performance of combined PMS for TCH removal in natural water bodies; (3) revealing possible catalytic oxidation mechanisms by reactive radical quenching assay, EPR experiments and XPS analysis; and (4) inferring the possible degradation pathways of TCH utilized GC-MS. This study is expected to provide a new strategy for the in-depth development of metal-modified biochar materials with cost-effective and more straightforward preparation, as well as solve the problem of difficult removal of emerging pollutants in practical water treatment.

# 2.2. Catalyst preparation and optimization

The magnetic biochar-iron lanthanum crosslinked alginate beads were prepared by the gel-calcination method (Fig. 1). The technique was based on the study of Wang et al. with some modifications [23], which was prepared as follows: firstly, $2 . 0 \ g$ of SA was dissolved in $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ of deionized water and stirred thoroughly on a magnetic stirrer at $6 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to obtain a homogeneous and transparent SA solution. Afterwards, a certain amount of pretreated coffee grounds and $0 . 5 ~ g$ of calcium carbonate powder were added and stirred until the mixture was uniform and cooled to room temperature. Next, the mixture was loaded into a $2 0 \mathrm { m L }$ syringe and gravity-dropped into $1 0 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of $0 . 4 \mathrm { m o l / L }$ crosslinked metal ion solution to form homogeneous spherical particles and cured for $^ \mathrm { ~ 1 8 ~ } \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ During this period, electrostatic attraction formed a three-dimensional network structure on the surface of alginate, resulting in the homogeneous dispersion of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ and $\mathrm { L a } ^ { 3 + }$ ions on the organic substrate [24]. After immersion, the crosslinked beads were rinsed several times with deionized water to remove the excess salt on the surface and dried in a $1 0 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ blast oven until constant weight. Finally, the dried crosslinked beads were placed in a muffle furnace under oxygen-limited conditions and heated to the optimized pyrolysis temperature for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ , More importantly, the carbonization by simple pyrolysis facilitates the formation of nanoparticles of metal ions loaded on porous carbon derived from the alginate backbone [25]. Ultimately, magnetic biochar-iron lanthanum crosslinked alginate beads were formed and named $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ .

# 3.1. Optimization of Fe/La@BCB preparation conditions

# 3.1.1. Effect of pyrolysis temperature

The effect of pyrolysis temperature (500, 700, and $9 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ on TCH removal performance was investigated, under preset conditions of a Fe/ La molar ratio of 2:2 and a coffee ground dosage of $1 0 ~ g$ As depicted in Fig. 2. (a), it can be seen that the removal of TCH rose sequentially with the increase of pyrolysis temperature. Under the condition that the mass of both catalyst and PMS was $0 . 6 \ g / \mathrm { L }$ , the removal efficiency were $7 6 . 0 1 \ \%$ , $8 6 . 3 4 \%$ and $9 1 . 5 5 ~ \%$ , respectively, which indicated the positive correlation between the pyrolysis temperatures and the catalytic performance of Fe/La@BCB.

As can be seen from the FTIR shown in Fig. 2. (b), Fe/La@BCB-T (500, 700 and $9 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ exhibited similar absorption peaks at various pyrolysis temperatures. Specifically, the broader characteristic peak at $3 4 0 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ was related to alginate's hydroxyl O-H bond stretching vibration. The vibrational intensities were in descending order: $5 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ $> 7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } > 9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ indicating that the pyrolysis process of SA may have been accompanied by the decomposition of -OH [26]. The stretching vibrations of -COOH and C-O-H/C-O-C bonds were observed at $1 7 0 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 0 5 3 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively [27,28]. An absorption peak at $1 4 2 3 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ , corresponding to the aromatic $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = C }$ ring stretching, was detected, and its intensity increased with higher pyrolysis temperatures. This indicated an enhanced degree of aromatization, which provided $\pi$ electrons and improved the conductivity of the carbon lattice [29].

The absorption peaks observed at $6 8 3 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ and $4 5 7 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ were attributed to the La-O and Fe-O bonds, confirming the formation of iron/ lanthanum oxides after modification [30,31]. These results indicated that high-temperature pyrolysis favoured the formation of more abundant metal oxide active sites, thereby enhancing catalytic performance. The SEM results and EDS analysis (Fig. S1. (a), Fig. S2. (b) and (c)) further confirmed the successful incorporation of iron and lanthanum elements into $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ , which exhibited a surface characterized by evenly distributed microsphere aggregates. The TEM (Fig. S2. (a)) analysis showed lattice spacings of $0 . 1 7 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and $0 . 3 6 8 \mathrm { n m }$ , corresponding to the (422) and (022) planes of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ and $\mathrm { F e _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ ,respectively. This further confirmed the SEM results. Additionally, lattice fringes of $0 . 3 1 9 \mathrm { n m }$ and 0.341 nm were attributed to the (100) and (024) planes of $\mathrm { L a } _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 }$ and $\mathbf { L a _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ , respectively.

Additionally, the carbon crystalline structure characteristics obtained through Raman spectroscopy testing further elucidated the positive correlation between pyrolysis temperature and catalytic performance. As shown in Fig. 2. (c), the two prominent peaks near $1 3 4 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (D-band) and $1 5 8 7 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (G-band) correspond to defective carbon (D-peak) and graphitized carbon (G-peak), respectively. Consequently, a larger ratio of $\mathrm { I _ { D } / I _ { G } }$ values indicated a higher level of carbon deficiency [32]. As the pyrolysis temperature ascended from $5 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the $\mathrm { I _ { D } / I _ { G } }$ ratio of the Fe/La@BCB composite material rose from 1.019 to 1.299. This was attributed to the conversion of small organic molecules into conjugated aromatic rings during biomass thermal polymerization, accompanied by the formation of numerous edge defects at the boundary of biochar [33]. It was shown that the elevated pyrolysis temperature improved the material, weakening the excess oxygen functional groups in the biochar and regulating and transforming the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 }$ -carbon, thus providing more carbon defects [34]. This structure was also considered an active site for mediating PMS activation and forming radical, highly reactive oxidative complexes [35]. Consequently, the defect structure generated at a pyrolysis temperature of $9 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ provided abundant and free-flowing electrons for PMS, thereby promoting the cleavage of O-O bonds in PMS to generate •OH and ${ \bf S } { \bf O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ [36] (Eqs. (4)(5)), which played a crucial role in the activation of PMS.

# 3.1.2. Effect of Fe/La ratio

The superiority of the catalytic performance of metal-modified biochar materials is closely related to the ratio of added metal dopants. Under the condition of both catalyst and PMS concentrations of $0 . 6 \ g / \mathrm { L }$ the removal efficiencies were $8 3 . 5 1 \ \%$ , $8 8 . 7 6 \%$ $9 1 . 5 5 ~ \%$ and $9 2 . 0 8 ~ \%$ for Fe/La molar ratios of 0:4, 1:3, 2:2 and 3:1 (Fig. 3. (a)), respectively. Clearly, with increasing iron content, the removal efficiency gradually improved. This improvement was attributed to two main factors. First, the formation of additional Fe-O bonds during the sufficient cross-linked pyrolysis process enhanced the heterogeneous electron transfer pathway, thereby promoting the activation of PMS [37]. Second, the increased specific surface area of the biochar beads contributed to their catalytic performance. Based on the BET characterization of five materials (Fig. S3), the $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ adsorption-desorption isotherms of all samples exhibited Type IV curves with $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 }$ hysteresis loops, indicating a mesoporous structure. Among these, the beads with a Fe/La molar ratio of 3:1 demonstrated an average pore diameter of $9 . 3 9 \mathrm { n m }$ , which may have provided favorable spatial conditions for TCH attachment and PMS activation. However, when the molar ratio was 4:0, although the biochar beads had the largest specific surface area $( 3 4 . 9 3 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ , the TCH removal efficiency decreased to $8 3 . 1 2 \%$ . The results showed that lanthanum doping positively effected on the catalytic performance. Specifically, incorporating $\mathbf { L } \mathbf { a } ^ { 3 + }$ distorted the ferrite lattice, generating more oxygen vacancy surface-active sites and increasing PMS activation's efficiency to produce free radicals [38].

Notably, at Fe/La molar ratios of 2:2 and 3:1, similar removal efficiencies of TCH were observed across the four dosage combinations. Therefore, for a more comprehensive analysis of the performance of the two materials, their mineralization capabilities towards TCH were compared by measuring TOC (shown in Fig. 3. (b)). The results revealed that compared to the 2:2 ratio, the Fe/La ratio of 3:1 exhibited a higher mineralization rate, at $3 6 . 4 0 \%$ and $4 9 . 7 6 ~ \%$ , respectively, representing a 1.3-fold increase. Fe/La of 3:1 exhibited a higher $\mathrm { I _ { D } / I _ { G } }$ ratio of 1.344 compared to Fe/La of 2:1, suggesting greater catalytic potential and electron conductivity (Fig. S4) [39]. The increased $\mathrm { I _ { D } / I _ { G } }$ ratio indicated a higher level of defects, implying that an optimal Fe/La ratio induced greater defect density and structural disorder. These characteristics likely provided additional active sites for PMS activation, enhancing TCH degradation [40,41]. Therefore, further investigation was conducted using Fe/La@BCB (3:1).

# 3.1.4. Performance comparison

Finally, the degradation effect of Fe/La $@$ BCB on TCH under different systems was compared (Fig. 3. (d)). It was observed that $0 . 6 \ g / \mathrm { L }$ PMS degraded $5 4 . 2 0 \%$ of TCH within $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ , indicating the difficulty in completely removing TCH pollutants solely through PMS oxidation. Moreover, when only $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ composite beads were added, the removal efficiency was only $1 1 . 6 8 \%$ , suggesting poor adsorption performance of the composite material towards TCH. # 3.2.2. Effect of solution $p H$

Since the pH varies in actual wastewater, the materials required in water treatment need to be adapted to a wide $\mathsf { p H }$ range. The Fe/ La@BCB/PMS system's performance for removing TCH at different pH values is shown in Fig. 5. (a) and Fig. S6. (c). The results showed that removing TCH presented an uptrend in the $\mathsf { p H }$ range of 37 while declining when pH exceeded 7. At pH 11, the removal efficiency of TCH was only $7 9 . 7 0 \%$ , indicating that the solid alkaline condition had a significant inhibition effect on the catalytic performance of the material.

This pH-dependent phenomenon may be related to the morphological structure present in TCH and PMS. Tetracycline has been reported to have three forms at different pH: the protonated form $\mathrm { ( T C H ^ { 3 + } }$ ; $\tt p H <$ 3.3), the neutral form $\mathrm { T C H _ { 2 } O _ { \cdot } }$ $\mathrm { p H } = 3 . 3 – 7 . 7 ]$ , and the monoanionic form (TC; $\mathrm { p H } > 7 . 7 )$ [46]. The $\tt p H _ { \tt P Z C }$ of $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ was 7.48 (Fig. S7). Therefore, when the system's pH was less than 7.48, the catalyst's surface was positively charged, which could enhance its adsorption of PMS. Simultaneously, tetracycline existed in the form of $\mathrm { T C H } ^ { 3 + }$ and $\mathrm { T C H _ { 2 } O }$ under acidic and neutral conditions, making it easy for $\mathrm { H S O } \bar { 5 }$ to produce electrostatic attraction and increase the contact chance. Conversely, when $\mathrm { p H } > 7 . 4 8$ , a certain electrostatic repulsion was generated between the catalyst with a negative charge on the surface and TC'and $S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { 2 \cdot }$ , which hindered the contact with each other, resulting in a decrease in the removal efficiency. In addition, the formation of iron and lanthanum hydroxide complexes $\mathrm { ( F e ( O H ) _ { 3 } }$ , $\mathrm { L a } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 3 } )$ when the initial pH reached 11.0 also further inhibited the performance of the catalyst. Typically, the pH of the actual wastewater is in the range of 5.59.0. Therefore, this suggests that $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ can be effectively used for antibiotic removal in practical wastewater treatment.

# 3.2.4. Effect of TCH concentration

The study investigated the removal efficiency of TCH by the Fe/ La@BCB/PMS system under varying initial concentrations (Fig. 5. (c) and Fig. S6. (e)). The results demonstrated that with the increase of TCH concentration, TCH removal efficiency by catalytic oxidation system generally initially increased and then decreased, following a pattern similar to that reported in previous studies [4951]. High TCH removal efficiencies were maintained at $2 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ and $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ ,achieving $9 1 . 2 1 \ \%$ and $9 0 . 5 2 \%$ , respectively. At $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , the removal efficiency slightly declined to $8 8 . 7 4 \%$ . This slight difference may be attributed to higher TCH concentrations providing the necessary driving force to overcome mass transfer resistance between the aqueous phase and the Fe/La@BCB, thereby enhancing effective contact between TCH molecules and the Fe/La@BCB surface and improving the removal efficiency [52]. During this process, complex physicochemical interactions at the TCH-Fe/La@BCB interface were crucial for pollutant removal. Mechanisms such as $\textstyle \mathrm { n - } \pi$ interactions, hydrogen bonding, and cation $\mathfrak { - } \pi$ interactions facilitated the adsorption of TCH, followed by oxidative degradation by surface-generated radicals [53,54]. However, with further increases in concentration (40, $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L } )$ , TCH removal efficiency gradually declined, and the observed $k _ { o b s }$ also decreased. This decline can be explained by intensified competition between TCH, intermediate products, and active surface sites at a constant $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ concentration, limiting sustained oxidation within the $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ reaction timeframe [55]. Additionally, high TCH concentrations directly consumed PMS in the system, reducing radical generation and impacting pollutant removal [56]. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the Fe/La@BCB/PMS system effectively removes TCH across different concentrations and exhibits strong resilience against load shocks.

# 3.2.5. Effects of inorganic anions and HA

Inorganic anions or natural macromolecular organic acids present in actual aquatic environments react with ROS, exerting varying degrees of influence on the removal efficiency of target pollutants. As observed in Fig. 5. (d) and Fig. S6. (f), the degradation of TCH in the presence of Cl, $\mathrm { C O } _ { 3 } ^ { 2 \cdot }$ , $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ ,and $\mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ at a concentration of 5 mmol/L showed slight inhibition, with a reduction in the removal efficiency of $4 . 7 3 \%$ $1 0 . 4 0 \%$ , $6 . 1 8 ~ \%$ and $7 . 4 3 ~ \%$ at $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , respectively. This was because a certain amount of inorganic anions consumed $\mathbf { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ and •OH in solution through a series of reactions to form less reactive and oxidizing radicals (Table S4, Eqs. (1)-(9)). The oxidation potential of these reactive species was lower than that of •OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ , thus exerting a specific inhibitory effect on the removal of TCH in the Fe/La@BCB/PMS system in this study.

When the same amount of $\mathrm { N a H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } }$ was added, the degradation effect of the Fe/La@BCB/PMS system was severely inhibited. The removal of TCH drastically dropped to $6 7 . 1 7 \%$ , and the $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ decreased sharply to 0.11. This result could be attributed to the quenching effect of $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } }$ on ROS (Table S4, Eqs. (10)-(11)). Additionally, the strong affinity of $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } }$ for transition metals might have also played a significant role in the degradation performance reduction, as $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } }$ could occupy the active metal sites on the Fe/La@BCB surface, thereby impeding the activation of PMS and the degradation of TCH [57]. Furthermore, adding $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } }$ lowered the system's pH, which could have been another potential factor inhibiting TCH removal.

In addition, HA is a class of organic pollutants commonly found in wastewater, which can inhibit the degradation of contaminants by masking or quenching the free radicals in the system [58]. The addition of 5 mg/L HA inhibited the removal of TCH to a certain extent, with a removal efficiency of $5 0 . 8 0 \%$ in $1 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . This phenomenon could be explained by (1) HA itself, as an organic substance, depleted the active radicals, thereby inhibiting the removal of TCH; (2) The functional groups in HA were adsorbed onto the catalyst surface, hindering the contact between the active centre and TCH. According to the above results, several ions had no significant effect on the TCH degradation except for $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } }$ and HA, indicating that the Fe/La@BCB/PMS system had a specific anti-interference ability.

# 3.3. Feasibility of Fe/La@BCB applications

# 3.1tability and usability

Fe/La@BCB was prepared three times under optimized conditions, and the catalytic performance of the four dose combinations was compared under the same experimental conditions to investigate the reproducibility of the material preparation. The results indicated that the three separately prepared batches of Fe/La@BCB exhibited consistent degradation efficiency towards TCH, demonstrating the repeatability of the sol-gel-calcination preparation method (shown in Fig. 6. (a)). The catalytic performance of $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ was scarcely affected by storage time, indicating the outstanding stability of the catalyst and its ability for long-term storage (Fig. 6. (b)). This can meet the requirements for large-scale production at the practical application stage and possess practical feasibility.

Furthermore, the reusability of the material was also a crucial factor in determining the catalyst's performance. The results of $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ catalyst reuse found that the degradation efficiency of Fe/La@BCB only descended to $7 5 . 6 4 ~ \%$ after four cycles (Fig. 6. (c)). The slight decline in the TCH removal efficiency could be attributed to several factors, including (1) undegraded TCH being adsorbed on the catalyst surface, (2) loss of catalyst during recycling, and (3) depletion of metal oxides in the catalyst. Therefore, $1 0 ~ \%$ mass $\left( 1 0 ~ \mathrm { m g } \right)$ of Fe/La@BCB was supplemented at the onset of the 5th and 6th cycles. Consequently, the corresponding degradation recovered to $8 0 . 3 0 \%$ and $7 9 . 8 7 \%$ respectively. These results underscore the beneficial role of the bead structure in enhancing the cycling stability of the $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ catalysts and maintaining satisfactory removal performance.

The leaching of metal ions was a crucial criterion for assessing catalyst stability. According to the concentrations of Fe and La measured in the reaction solution, their leaching concentrations had decreased from $0 . 8 5 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ and $0 . 2 7 \mathrm { m g L ^ { - 1 } }$ to $0 . 3 1 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 1 2 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ after six cycles, respectively (Fig. S9). This leaching of metal ions was likely due to the acidic nature of PMS, which caused the metal oxides on the catalyst surface to detach more easily in an acidic environment. Overall, the amount of metal ions leached was minimal and negligible compared to the amount of catalyst used, indicating that the synthesized Fe/ $\operatorname { L a @ B C B }$ possessed excellent stability and reusability.

# 3.4.2. Electron transfer mechanism

The efficient activation of PMS by doped metal ions was often achieved through enhanced electron transfer. This reduction in impedance was likely attributed to the codoping of Fe and La into the biochar during pyrolysis, which generated abundant defect sites. These defects decreased the interfacial charge transfer resistance of $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ , enhancing the electron conductivity and charge transfer rate during the PMS activation process [62]. Under these conditions, the redox cycling rate of Fe(II)/Fe(III) and La(II)/La(IV) on the catalyst surface was enhanced, leading to the generation of $\mathrm { \Delta } \mathsf { S } \mathsf { O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ ,•OH and $S O _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ (Eqs. Among the catalysts, Fe/La@BCB showed the most significant current variation, which may be due to the synergistic effect between Fe and La, increasing the number of active sites and accelerating electron conduction between the pollutant and PMS, thus promoting the oxidative degradation of the pollutant. Among the various catalysts, Fe/La@BCB exhibited the most significant current changes, further confirming the synergistic interaction between Fe and La.

(24)) [68]. Meanwhile, the migration of lattice oxygen (M-O) on the surface of the iron-lanthanum metal oxide material resulted in a shift of the binding energy to higher values after the reaction. This suggested a reduction in the electronic density of oxygen on the catalyst surface, which could lead to oxygen vacancies or reactive oxygen species forming, thereby providing additional active sites for the reaction [69, 70]. The biochar microspheres possessed abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, hierarchical pores, and a high degree of material defects. These features contributed to the cleavage of the O-O bond in PMS, leading to activation, hydrolysis, and oxidation reactions that generated $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ and •OH (Eqs. (26)-(28)) [71].

From the spectrum of elemental Fe (see Fig. 8. (d)), the binding energies near $7 2 5 . 1 8 \mathrm { e V }$ and $7 1 1 . 5 2 \mathrm { e V }$ corresponded to the absorption peaks of $\mathrm { F e } ( 2 \mathbf { p } _ { 1 / 2 } )$ and $\mathrm { F e } ( 2 \mathbf { p } _ { 3 / 2 } )$ , respectively. Of these, the vicinity of $7 1 0 . 4 6 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and 723.71 eV represented $\mathrm { F e ( I I ) }$ , and the vicinity of $7 1 1 . 7 5 \mathrm { e V }$ and $7 2 5 . 5 5 \mathrm { e V }$ represented Fe(III) [72]. This proved that Fe in the biochar beads was present as compounds with different valence states. Moreover, after the catalytic reaction, Fe(III) in the Fe/La@BCB decreased from $7 0 . 8 7 \%$ to $5 7 . 9 5 ~ \%$ , while Fe(II) ascended from $2 1 . 1 3 \%$ to $3 4 . 0 7 \%$ . This suggested that the active iron sites within the catalyst underwent electron transfer, thereby participating in the heterogeneous activation process of PMS. In addition, the absorption peaks corresponding to $\mathrm { L a } ( 3 \mathrm { d } _ { 5 / 2 } )$ underwent twofold splitting at $8 3 5 . 5 5 \mathrm { e V }$ and 839.01 eV $\mathbf { \Delta } \Delta \mathrm { E } = 3 . 5 \mathrm { e V } .$ due to the creation of La-H and La-O bonds (see Fig. 8. (e)) [73]. This indicated that the La element was stable in $\mathrm { L a } _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 }$ in Fe/La $@$ BCB [74].

The stable chemical states of Fe and La elements were further confirmed by XRD patterns (Fig. S11). The diffraction peaks of Fe/ $\operatorname { L a @ B C B }$ at $3 0 . 1 ^ { \circ }$ , $3 5 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ , $4 3 . 0 ^ { \circ }$ , and $5 6 . 9 ^ { \circ }$ were attributed to the (220), (311), (400), and (511) crystal planes of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ (JCPDS No. 72-2303), respectively. The peaks at $2 5 . 5 5 ^ { \circ }$ , $4 4 . 3 5 ^ { \circ }$ , and $5 6 . 8 0 ^ { \circ }$ were assigned to the (100), (110), and (004) planes of cubic $\mathbf { L a _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ (JCPDS No. 05-0602). The peaks at $1 3 . 1 7 ^ { \circ }$ and $2 4 . 0 4 ^ { \circ }$ also matched the standard diffraction patterns of $\mathrm { L a } _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 }$ (JCPDS No. 25-1400). The persistence of these characteristic peaks after usage, compared to the original Fe/La@BCB, provides a reliable guarantee supporting the recyclability of Fe/ $\operatorname { L a @ B C B }$ .

As depicted in the FT-IR spectrum in Fig. 8(f), the stretching vibrations of the -COOH bond induced absorption peaks at $1 7 0 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , which could effectively activate PMS to generate free radicals (Eq. (25)). After participating in the catalytic reaction, the absorption peaks of Fe-O, LaO, and other groups still existed at the same position in the IR spectrum. This indicated that the active sites in $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ were not completely consumed. This effect was primarily attributed to the carbon framework structure provided by the biochar beads, which not only exposed surface catalytic sites and shortened the diffusion pathways of reactants but also protected the iron/lanthanum oxides from leaching (Eqs. # 3.4.4. Possible degradation pathways and toxicity assessment

Based on the TOC removal at the end of the reaction, it was known that TCH was not utterly degraded to inorganic substances such as $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ but was partially converted to organic intermediates. Hence, water samples taken at different degradation times (0, 5, 10, 15, 30, 45 and $6 0 \mathrm { \ m i n } \mathrm { \cdot }$ ) were tested using GC-MS. The possible intermediates were further analyzed and screened by the mass-to-charge ratio $\left( m / z \right)$ further analyzed and screened the possible intermediates. Three possible pathways for TC degradation in the Fe/La@BCB/PMS system (Fig. 10) and the chemical structural formulae of the intermediates (Table S6) were summarised and speculated.

Pathway I: The carbon-carbon double bond of the C-ring in TC was first attacked by •OH, and a hydroxylation reaction took place, producing the intermediate product $m / z = 4 6 1$ . Afterwards, •OH further attacked the carbon-carbon double bond of the A ring and the methyl group on the amino group, which was oxidized to produce ketones with higher molecular weight $m / z = 4 9 1$ [75]. Further reactions included oxidative ring opening, deamination and dealkylation to form the intermediates P4-P7. Pathway II: The demethylation of •OH and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ attacked the N-C bonds with lower bond energies, destroying the N-methyl group of TC [76]. This was accompanied by a hydroxylation reaction of the carbon to produce the substance P8 with an $m / z$ of 436 [77]. Under further attack by the active substance ROS, the aromatic ring D's deamidation reaction and ring opening generated the degradation product P9 with $m / z = 3 2 5$ Subsequently, the deamidation and demethylation reactions formed the products with $m / z = 2 7 8$ and $m / z = 2 5 4$ in that order. Pathway III: P12 $( m / z = 4 5 0$ ) was attributed to demethylation reaction, hydroxyl rearrangement under the attack of ${ \bf S } { \bf O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ and oxidation of •OH. This resulted in the loss of the carbonyl group to produce the product with $m / z = 4 5 0$ [78]. P13 $( m / z = 3 4 0 )$ was formed based on P12 due to the deamidation reaction and destruction of phenolic hydroxyl and amide groups. The non-selective action of ROS continuously triggered the terminal oxidation and ring-opening transformations. Further smaller intermediates $m / z = 1 5 8$ , 132, 102 were produced and finally converted to small organic molecules, $\mathrm { N H } _ { 4 } ^ { + }$ , $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } ,$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ .

The acute toxicity, development toxicity, and mutagenicity of TCH degradation intermediates were assessed using the Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (T.E.S.T.), which was based on the Quantitative StructureActivity Relationship (QSAR) method [79,80]. Detailed data can be found in Table S7. As shown in Fig. S12. (a) and (b), the median lethal concentration $\left( \mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 } \right)$ values of all tetracycline degradation intermediates were higher than that of TCH, indicating a reduction in toxicity compared to the parent compound. The Fe/La@BCB/PMS system effectively reduced the developmental toxicity of most intermediates, with P13, P16, P17, and P18 showing no developmental toxicity (Fig. S12. (c)). Except for P1, P2, P3, P5, P8, and P12, all intermediates were converted to non-mutagenic forms, suggesting a significant reduction in the genotoxicity of the target pollutants (Fig. S12. (d)). In conclusion, the Fe/La@BCB/PMS system effectively reduced the overall toxicity of TCH during the degradation process, thereby lowering environmental risks.

# 4. Conclusion

A novel iron and lanthanum co-doped alginate composite material, biochar beads $\left( \mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B } \right)$ , was successfully synthesized using waste coffee grounds as the carbon base. The results indicated that in a system with $0 . 2 { \ g } / \mathrm { L }$ of $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ and $0 . 3 { \ g } / \mathrm { L }$ of PMS, a TCH removal efficiency of $9 0 . 5 2 \%$ and a mineralization rate of $3 0 . 4 9 \%$ were achieved, indicating excellent performance in catalyzing PMS. The catalytic mechanism was attributed to the synergistic interaction between the abundant functional groups on the biochar surface and the metal elements, facilitating electron transfer within the reaction system. The combined $\mathrm { F e / L a @ B C B }$ and PMS system maintained a TCH removal efficiency exceeding $7 8 \%$ , even in anions and organic matter interference in aqueous environments. Reactive species such as $\mathbf { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ , •OH, $\mathbf { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ were identified as the primary contributors to TCH degradation. Furthermore, three potential degradation pathways of TCH molecules were proposed, with intermediate products exhibiting reduced toxicity, thereby minimizing environmental risks. Overall, Fe/La@BCB offered a practical method for valorizing waste coffee grounds, provided a novel and effective strategy for PMS activation, and presented an economical, stable solution for antibiotic remediation.
