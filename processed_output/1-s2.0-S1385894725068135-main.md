Sustainable water purification

The key to ensure drinking water safety is efficient and continuous water purification. Fe–Mo bimetallic catalysts have attracted much attention because of high efficiency, recyclability and environmental friendliness in Fenton-like process. Herein, we obtained a novelty carbon-based ferric molybdenum sulfide bimetallic catalyst as $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ through hydrothermal process and high temperature incineration at ${ \mathrm { H } } _ { 2 } { - } \mathbf { A } \mathbf { r }$ atmosphere with Prussian blue as a crystalline core and sodium molybdate, thiourea and glucose as precursors, which had a special functionally zoned structure including biochar with lots of -CN, -CH and -OH groups as the main absorption sites, ferric molybdenum sulfide composite as the main catalytic sites. Synchronous and asynchronous adsorption oxidation experiments proved that the biochar sites absorbed and enriched the EPs, and the catalytic sites acted with PMS to produce ROS, which immediately degrade the EPs absorbed by the biochar sites, thus realizing the cooperative reinforcement of adsorption oxidation process. Therefore, the degradation efficiency of atrazine can reach $1 0 0 ~ \%$ within $8 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . In the degradation process, sulfur and molybdenum in the catalytic sites could promote the cycling of Fe (II) and Fe (III) with action of PMS to produce ROS. In ${ \mathrm { F e M o S } } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ { \mathrm { C } }$ , Fe was the active center, where sulfur and molybdenum could promote the cycling of Fe (II) and Fe (III) to produce ROS. The evaluation of cycle performance and the stable operation of water purification device showed that $\operatorname { F e M o S } _ { \mathbf { x } } @ \mathbf { C } /$ PMS system had broad prospects for treating actual wastewater. This study verified the feasibility of fractional adsorption oxidation, and provided ideas for rational system design of sustainable water purification to ensure drinking water safety.

# 1. Introduction

Emerging pollutants (EPs) have been detected in aquatic ecosystems which can accumulate in organisms throughout the food chain, posing long-term risks to ecological environment and human health [1]. In order to reduce the impact of EPs on ecological environment, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are frequently used to degrade EPs during water treatment. In recent years, peroxymonosulfate-based AOPs (PMSAOPs) have been widely studied for removal of organic pollutants [2,3], because the breaking of O–O bonds in PMS can lead to the generation of a variety of reactive oxygen species (ROS) which have high redox potential, but the short lifetime [4–6]. There is consumption in the process of migration because of the short half-life of ROS, which reduces the efficiency, so it is crucial to make the active species react with the target pollutants as soon as possible to improve the removal efficiency of pollutants [7]. PMS alone cannot produce the above ROS and usually require a catalyst to activate them. Among different activation methods, transition metals (e.g. Fe, Zn, Mo, Co, Cu, Mn,) are promising strategies for PMS activation due to their high efficiency [8–11]. Transition metal oxides, sulfides and zero-valent metals have been widely used in PMS activation, but most of them have the phenomenon of particle aggregation, easy dissolution and instability in the reaction process. Therefore, the search for suitable substrate materials, in order to disperse transition metal compounds as much as possible and produce a certain anchoring effect, has become a research focus.

Common substrate materials are carbon nitride [12], grapheme [13] and biochar [14], especially biochar, such as ferric oxide/biochar [15], nZVI/biochar [16], $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 } ,$ /biochar [17], etc. Among these materials, biochar has high adsorption ability to EPs due to its high porosity and specific surface area, so whether EPs will occupy active site after adsorption and then affect catalytic degradation efficiency is the focus of research. Tan [18] prepared xFe@LSBCy with loofah and $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } 0$ as raw materials by one-step pyrolysis process and specifically studied the effect of adsorption on the subsequent catalytic oxidation efficiency through pre-adsorption and synchronous adsorption oxidation process. The study showed that in the asynchronous adsorption oxidation process of adding 0.5Fe@LSBC700 catalyst for $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ and then adding PMS, carbamazepine was removed by $2 5 \%$ in the early adsorption stage, and the final carbamazepine removal rate was only $4 6 ~ \%$ . In the process of adsorption and oxidation with the simultaneous addition of 0.5Fe@LSBC700 and PMS, the degradation rate reached $1 0 0 ~ \%$ within $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . It could be seen from the above research results that catalytic active sites were occupies due to the adsorption of pollutants, which greatly reduced the catalytic oxidation efficiency. According to the theory of EPs catalytic reaction, the reaction rate of EPs was related to the catalytic active species and the concentration of EPs. In the case of a certain number of active species, the reaction rate increased with the increase of EPs concentration. Similarly, at a given EPs concentration, the reaction rate obviously increased with the increase in the number of active species. Therefore, it is necessary to synthesize a catalyst that has a certain adsorption effect to enrich EPs, and the absorbed EPs does not occupy the active site.

Prussian blue (PB) and its analogues (PBAs), which can have regular cube, hollow microspheres and nanotubes structure after a series of adjustments, are widely used in the field of catalysis and is very novel due to its special structural characteristics [19]. PB and PBAs can not only provide carbon frame structure to increase adsorption and active sites, but also will not affect the activation of internal Fe ions or other metal ions due to the occupation of sites by adsorption. The internal Fe reacted with oxidizing agents to produce reactive oxygen species, which oxidized and degraded pollutants, but the cyclic continuous process was limited due to the slow conversion rate of Fe (III) to Fe (II). Previous studies have shown that $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ could participate in the Fe (II) and Fe (III) cycle process, and then enhance the Fe (II)/Fe (III) conversion to achieve enhanced catalytic performance. For instance, Zhang et al [20] deposited molybdenum disulfide on Prussian blue as the base material to synthesize $\mathrm { P B } @ \mathrm { M o S } _ { 2 }$ , which could efficiently degrade BPA. The adsorption effect of $\mathrm { P B } @ \mathrm { M o S } _ { 2 }$ on the target pollutants was small, and the catalytic efficiency was not reduced because the adsorption of pollutants occupies the active site. However, the enhancement of catalytic degradation of pollutants by adsorption process had not been proved.

In this study, we used Prussian blue with MOFs structure characteristics as a template, ammonium molybdate, thiourea and glucose as precursors, and nano-spherical catalyst, which was a functionally zoned structure composite catalytic material with adsorptive carbon regions and ferrosulfide and molybdenum active sites. This special structural material was conducive to the adsorption of target pollutants in the structure layer dominated by carbon to achieve concentration enrichment, and the production of ROS in the catalytic site layer of iron and molybdenum sulfide, so as to achieve the adsorption and catalytic oxidation of pollutants in AOPs process to achieve synergic enhancement of adsorption and catalysis. Due to the anchoring effect of carbon on ferric molybdenum sulfide, the dissolution of molybdenum ion was also greatly reduced. The main contents of this study were as follows: 1) Using Prussian blue, ammonium molybdate, thiourea and glucose as precursors, the nanospherical core-shell composite of ferric molybdenum sulfide and biochar was synthesized by hydrothermal and hightemperature thermal decomposition; 2) ATZ was taken as the target pollutant to confirm efficient degradation efficiency of the system, and synergic enhancement mechanism of adsorption and catalysis was analyzed; 3) To explore the mechanism and path of active substances and catalytic degradation; 4) To evaluate application conditions and

practical application.

# 2.1. Reagent

# 2.2. Synthesis of catalyst

$\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was prepared using the two-step method of hydrothermal synthesis and thermolysis. First, $2 1 0 \mathrm { m } g$ Prussian blue were dissolved in $2 0 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of $0 . 0 3 { \mathrm { M } }$ glucose $\mathrm { ( C _ { 6 } H _ { 1 2 } O _ { 6 } ) }$ solution under magnetic stirring for $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at room temperature to form solution A. Subsequently, $2 0 0 \mathrm { m } g$ of sodium molybdate $\mathrm { ( N a _ { 2 } M o O _ { 4 } { \bullet } 2 H _ { 2 } O ) }$ and $4 8 0 \mathrm { m g }$ of thiourea $\mathrm { ( C N _ { 2 } H _ { 4 } S ) }$ were added in solution A at the same time. After being stirred for $4 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at room temperature, the well-dispersed solution was transferred to 300 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclaves and then heated at $2 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . After cooling down to the room temperature, the obtained black precipitate was collected via centrifugation and washed three times with deionized water and ethanol, then dried in the vacuum oven at $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 6 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . Finally, the resulting powder was calcined at $8 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at a heating rate of $5 ~ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ in $5 ~ \% ~ \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } – 9 5 \%$ Ar flow [21].

$\mathrm { M o S _ { 2 } / C }$ was also synthesized according to previous report. First of all, $3 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m g } ~ N a _ { 2 } M o O _ { 4 } { \bullet } 2 H _ { 2 } O }$ were added to $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of 0.03 M ${ \mathrm { C } } _ { 6 } { \mathrm { H } } _ { 1 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 6 }$ solution and stirred for $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at room temperature. Then $0 . 6  g \mathrm { N _ { 2 } H _ { 4 } C S }$ was dispersed in above solution under continuous stirring for $4 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . Subsequently, the solution was placed in a $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclaves and heated at $2 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 2 \mathrm { h }$ . After cooling down to the room temperature, the solidity was collected via centrifugation and washed three times with deionized water and ethanol. After that, the powder was dried in the vacuum oven at $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 6 \mathrm { h } }$ . Finally, the black powder was calcined at $6 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under an Ar atmosphere to obtain ${ \bf M o S } _ { 2 } /$ C.

$\mathbf { F e M o S _ { x } }$ was synthesized in the same way as $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ except adding glucose. $\mathbf { F e M o S _ { x } }$ before the calcination was named $\mathbf { F e M o S _ { x } }$ BC and $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ before the calcination was called $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ -BC, PB after the calcination was named PB-AC. $\mathrm { F e S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was synthesized in the same way as $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ except that $\mathrm { N a _ { 2 } M o O _ { 4 } { \bullet } 2 H _ { 2 } O }$ was added. The synthesis procedure of $\mathrm { M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was also the same as $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ except that Prussian was added.

# 2.3. Experiment procedure

The stock solution of ATZ was dissolved in the deionized water in advance. All the degradation experiments were performed in a $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ glass beaker containing $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ reaction solution at initial $\mathrm { p H } 6 . 4$ at a stirring rate of $3 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ (rpm). ATZ was the typical contaminant to explore catalytic degradation effect and mechanism. Buffer agent was not used in order to avoid the buffer reacting with active species. The initial ATZ concentration was $1 2 \mu \mathrm { M }$ . And then a predetermined dosage of ${ \mathrm { F e M o S } } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ { \mathrm { C } }$ and predetermined concentration of PMS were added to the solution. Approximately $1 . 5 \mathrm { m L }$ sample was collected at specific time intervals, filtered through $0 . 2 2 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ PTFE syringe filter discs and quenched immediately with 0.1 M $\mathrm { N a _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ solution $( 3 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L } )$ for further analysis. The pH of reaction solution was adjusted by $0 . 1 \mathrm { ~ M ~ N a O H }$ and 0.1 M $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } S 0 _ { 4 }$ . Synchronous adsorption oxidation meant that the predetermined catalyst and PMS were added to EPs solution at the same time, while asynchronous adsorption oxidation meant that the catalyst was first added to EPs solution and adsorbed for $2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ to achieve the maximum adsorption amount, and then the specified amount of PMS solution was added. Of note, all experiments were conducted independently at least three times, and all data in the graph were plotted as average values with standard deviation errors.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Characterization of $F e M o S _ { x } @ C$

The morphology characteristics and element distribution of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ were observed by SEM and EDS-mapping. It could be seen from Fig. S1a and Fig. S1b that PB and PB-AC were a smaller irregular cubes. The $\mathbf { F e M o S _ { x } }$ -BC had an uneven lamellar structure while $\mathbf { F e M o S _ { x } }$ had a uniform lamellar structure (Fig. S1c and Fig. S1d). It is worth mentioning that $\mathbf { F e M o S } _ { \mathbf { x } } @ \mathbf { C } { \cdot } \mathbf { B } \mathbf { C }$ (Fig. S1e) and $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ (Fig. 1a) both exhibited regular uniform spherical structure that was different from $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } .$ -BC and $\operatorname { F e M o S } _ { \mathbf { x } }$ because of the addition of glucose in the synthesis process, which indicated that glucose played a regulatory role in the synthesis of catalysts to form the nanosphere. This meant that the main morphology of the catalyst did not change before and after calcination. After $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was successfully synthesized, the diameter of sphere was correspondingly larger compared to PB and $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } ,$ about $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ (Fig. 1b). TEM was performed to further observe the internal structure of the sphere. As shown in Fig. 1c, the varying shades of color of the spheres indicated that carbon and metal were not evenly distributed but were concentrated in certain areas. In addition, crystal lattice could be clearly seen in HRTEM images with d-spacing of $0 . 6 2 \mathrm { n m }$ and $0 . 2 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. 1d) which were in good agreement with the typical $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ (002) and FeS (114) planes [22], while the outer layer of the sphere was mostly an amorphous carbon layer structure. And then it was evident from mapping results (Fig. 1e) that the Fe, Mo, C and S were distributed throughout the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ sphere, but there was more C in some areas and more Fe and Mo in others. This indicated that the catalyst formed regional micro-nano spheres, which formed the structural basis of the adsorption and catalytic oxidation zones. On the one hand, this special structure could reduce the dissolution of the metal and maintain the stability of the material. On the other hand, EPs could be enriched through carbon region adsorption, and then metal active sites could be used to activate PMS to degrade EPs. The adsorption of the former had a strong promoting effect on the oxidation of the latter.

# 3.2. Performance of $F e M o S _ { x } @ C / P M S$ system

In order to explore the properties brought by the special structure of the material, the experiment of asynchronous adsorption oxidation and synchronous adsorption oxidation was compared.

The degradation rate of ATZ during $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ $ C-BC/PMS synchronous adsorption oxidation was $3 3 . 7 \%$ . However, after adsorption for 20 min and then oxidation, the degradation rate could reach $7 8 . 2 \ \%$ , although the adsorption rate was only $5 \%$ . It was worth mentioning that the degradation rate could reach $3 3 . 4 ~ \%$ within one minute after adsorption (Fig. S3). Similarly, the results showed that the adsorption effect of $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ on ATZ was $2 2 . 2 \%$ for $2 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , the degradation rate was $7 9 . 7 \%$ after $1 \mathrm { m i n }$ , and the degradation rate was only $4 3 . 5 \%$ when the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was simultaneously adsorbed and oxidized for 1 min (Fig. 2b). ATZ could be completely degraded within $5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ when first adsorbed and then oxidized, while it could be completely degraded within $8 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ when simultaneously adsorbed and oxidized. This meant that the adsorption effect of the ${ \mathrm { F e M o S } } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ { \mathrm { C } }$ on ATZ could promote the subsequent degradation. To further explore the role of adsorption in subsequent oxidation, adsorption isotherm was first used to assist in obtaining the catalytic capacity of the catalyst. In order to obtain the accurate saturated adsorption capacity, the catalyst and the solution were oscillated together for $4 8 \ \mathrm { h }$ . As can be seen from Fig. 2c, with the increase of the residual concentration of ATZ after adsorption $\mathrm { ( C _ { e } ) }$ , the maximum adsorption capacity $\left( \mathrm { Q _ { e } } \right)$ of $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ gradually increased. However, when $\mathrm { C _ { e } }$ reached $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , $\mathrm { Q _ { e } }$ no longer increased, which was more in line with the monolayer adsorption law. Therefore, this catalyst was Langmuir adsorption. Based on the fitted data (Fig. S4), the value of the Langmuir adsorption equilibrium constant $\mathrm { ( K _ { L } ) }$ was calculated to be $0 . 3 1 \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m g }$ [26]. According to the value of $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { L } } ,$ the separation factor $\left( \operatorname { R } _ { \mathrm { L } } \right)$ was also calculated according to following equation [27], and the result was 0.56. The value between 0 and 1 was considered favorable adsorption.

$$

After the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ adsorbed EPs, EPs were enriched on its surface or in its pores, significantly increasing the local concentration and thereby accelerating the degradation reaction rate. This indicated that Fe, Mo and S loaded with carbon was significantly improved the degradation effect. The amount of catalyst, PMS concentration and pH parameters were optimized in Note.S4-S6.

# 3.3.4. Identification of active sites

To further explore the role of Fe, Mo, C and S species in the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C / P M S }$ system, the variation of their valence and content during process were characterized by XPS analysis. As presented in the XPS spectra, the C 1 s spectra showed five main peaks located at 284.51, 285.82, 286.52, 287.71 and $2 8 9 . 0 2 \mathrm { e V }$ which corresponded to $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 } { \cdot } \mathsf { C }$ (75 $\% )$ , $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 } \mathbf { - C }$ $( 1 0 ~ \% )$ , C-OH $( 6 ~ \% )$ , $\scriptstyle { \mathsf { C } } = 0$ $( 4 ~ \% )$ and ${ \bf O - C = O }$ $( 5 ~ \% )$ , respectively (Fig. 4a). It indicated that $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was rich in graphene carbon and contained hydroxyl groups. After seven reactions, $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 } { \cdot } \mathsf { C }$ and $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 } \mathbf { - C }$ had hardly changed, and the proportion of C-OH decreased from $6 \%$ to $4 \%$ (Fig. S14), which proved that the hydroxyl group played a role in the degradation process, consistent with the FTIR results (Fig. S15). As displayed in Fig. 4b, the curve-fitted peaks at 162.10 and $1 6 3 . 4 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ were assigned to $S ^ { 2 - }$ species and the third peak emerged at $1 6 8 . 3 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ which often observed to be produced by sulfur oxide $\left( \mathsf { S O } _ { \mathsf { n } } \right)$ in S-doped carbon materials [43]. It was worth mentioning that the XPS peak of S increased significantly at 160.94 and 164.27 eV which ascribed to the polysulfides (terminal S) and $S ^ { 2 - } / S _ { 2 } ^ { 2 - }$ (edge S) after seven reactions (Fig. S16) [44]. This meant that the edge S and terminal S of $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ could be strengthened in solution, and edge S could easily combine with $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ in solution, thus exposing Mo (IV) in the material for electron transfer [10]. The content of $\mathrm { \bar { s o } } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ in PMS solution before and after adding $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was also measured. The concentration of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ was $2 2 . 1 \mathrm { \ m g / L }$ before adding the material and $2 6 . 3 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ after reaction (Fig. S17), which meant that the lower valence of S played a reducing role in reaction. During the experiment, it was found that $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ was added to the reaction solution, the pH of the solution decreased from 6.4 to 4.5 which was because the existence of sulfur vacancy. The presence of sulfur vacancy in the catalyst was also found by solid EPR test (Fig. S18). Sulfur vacancies not only provide abundant active sites but also accelerate electron transfer, which is conducive to the rapid progress of catalytic reactions [45].

# 3.3.5. ATZ degradation mechanism in the system

In order to further explore the mechanism of $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ activation of PMS, the adsorption energy of PMS was calculated by DFT calculation. It could be seen that the adsorption energy of single $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ and FeS on PMS were − 0.29 eV and − 8.47 eV respectively (Fig. S21). However, the adsorption energy increased to $- 1 0 . 2 6 \mathrm { e V }$ when FeS was used as the active center (Fig. 5a), while the adsorption energy of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ to PMS decreases to $- 4 . 1 9 \mathrm { e V }$ when $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ was the active center (Fig. 5b). According to the experimental results, Fe was the main active site in the system.

On the other hand, the electron density difference was also calculated, and the calculation model and results were displayed. The absolute value of adsorption energy $\mathrm { ( E _ { a d } ) }$ of $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ site for terminal oxygen of PMS was greater than other oxygen sites, indicating that it was the optimal adsorption configuration. The Bader charge analysis showed that $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ when FeS was the active center $\left( 0 . 7 6 ~ \mathrm { e } ^ { - } \right)$ (Fig. 5c) has stronger electron transfer to PMS than FeS $\left( 0 . 6 9 \mathrm { e } ^ { - } \right)$ (Fig. S22a), $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ $( 0 . 5 0 \mathrm { e } ^ { - } )$ (Fig. S22b) and $\mathsf { M o F e S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ when $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 }$ was the active center $\left( 0 . 5 9 \mathrm { e } ^ { - } \right)$ (Fig. 5d). All electrons were transferred from the catalyst to the PMS, and the more the number of electrons transferred, the stronger the adsorption effect of the material on the PMS. The results were consistent with the previous adsorption energy. The above results showed that $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ with Fe as the active center had the best activation effect on PMS.

In the process of degradation, the unstable edge sulfur rapidly fell off from $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ in the reaction solution to expose the internal Mo (IV), which could then activate the PMS to produce $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ (Eq. (4)). Similarly, Fe (II) could activate PMS to generate $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and then Fe (II) was transformed into Fe (III) (Eq. (5)). Some of the $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ in the solution would convert to HO• (Eq. (2)). Fe (II) and Fe (III) could also react with PMS to produce $\mathrm { F e } ( \mathrm { I V } ) = 0$ (Eq. (6)). The high-valent Fe species was another path which could degrade ATZ. Fe (III) and Mo (VI) reacted with PMS to produce the low reactive $S O _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ which could form $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ reacting with $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ (Eqs. (7)–(9)) or by self-reaction (Eq. (10)) [47]. Fe (III) and Mo (VI) formed in the reaction could be reduced by edge sulfur to Fe (II) and Mo (IV) (Eqs. (11)–(12)) which continually activate PMS efficiently to generate ROS [48]. The XPS results showed that there was also valence change between Fe and Mo. Mo (IV) could transfer electron to Fe (III) for the regeneration of Fe (II) because the potential of Fe (III) was $0 . 7 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $\mathrm { \Delta \tilde { M o } ^ { ( V I ) } / M o ^ { ( I V ) } = 0 . 6 5 ~ e V }$ vs NHE (Eq. # 3.4.1. Reusability of $F e M o S _ { x } @ C$

In order to evaluate the practical application value of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C } ,$ , the reusability of the material was explored. Desired ATZ was added into the solution in reaction and the consumed PMS was supplemented to start the next cycle every $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . As established in the Fig. 7a, the degradation rate of ATZ was $9 0 ~ \%$ after the seven reactions and the degradation rate was slightly reduced under the same reaction conditions. As the reaction progresses, the sites of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ were gradually occupied and the adsorption capacity of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ reduced, which might be one of the reasons for the reduced degradation efficiency. The great reusability might be due to the reaction between Mo, Fe and S elements in the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ during the reaction process. It could be seen that the proportion of Mo, Fe and S in various valence states and the crystal form of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C }$ hardly changed after seven times of reaction (Fig. S23). In particular, the content of ${ \mathrm { M o } } ^ { 4 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ which could efficiently activate PMS almost did not decrease.

# 3.4.2. Oxidation performance on different EPs

It was expected that the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C / P M S }$ system would maintain high degradation efficiency for hormones and drugs other than ATZ. Therefore, several representative EPs were selected to test the performance of the system, such as sulfisoxazole (SIZ), bisphenol A (BPA), carbamazepine (CBZ), enrofloxacin (ENR), diclofenac sodium (DFC), ibuprofen (IBU) and acetaminophen (ACM). As demonstrated in Fig. 7b, the EPs which contain hydroxyl, carboxyl, fluorine, chlorine and amino functional groups could be efficiently degraded within eight minutes, though the rate of degradation was slightly different. CBZ, SIZ and DFC had similar amino and carbon‑oxygen double bond structures and therefore had similar degradation rates. As for ENR, about $9 0 \%$ could be degraded in one minute in the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C / P M S }$ system, which showed excellent performance. The efficient degradation of a variety of EPs was due to the ability of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C / P M S }$ system to produce both free radicals and non-free radicals which could react with different EPs rapidly. In conclusion, the system could be applied to various EPs and had great practical application potential.

# 3.4.3. Practical application of $F e M o S _ { x } @ C / P M S$ system

In order to further verify the practical application of the $\operatorname { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C } /$ PMS system, a continuous flow device which was displayed in Fig. 7c and Fig. S24, was designed to investigate the degradation efficiency of the system after $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . It could be seen from Fig. 7d that PMS alone had almost no degradation effect within $\mathbf { 2 4 } \mathrm { h }$ . $0 . 3 \ g \ F e \mathbf { M } 0 \mathbf { S } _ { \mathbf { x } } @ \mathbf { C }$ was placed in the syringe, and both sides were stuffed with cotton to prevent the catalyst from leaking. The concentration of the added PMS was $0 . 1 \mathrm { m M }$ , and the treated solution was $1 2 \mu \mathrm { M }$ of ATZ solution. The total processing volume was $^ { 3 \mathrm { L } }$ . The degradation of ATZ by FeS/PMS system and $\mathbf { M o S } _ { 2 } /$ PMS system was $4 7 . 8 ~ \%$ and $7 1 . 6 \%$ respectively. The efficiency of ATZ in above systems showed a gradually decreasing trend, and there was no degradation effect on ATZ in the system at $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . The degradation rate of ATZ by $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C / P M S }$ system had always been $1 0 0 \%$ within $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . In order to verify the purification effect on the water body of the drinking water source, the raw water of the reservoir was used as background water and ATZ solution was added for degradation. The relevant indicators of reservoir water were listed in Table.S5. The experiment found that a degradation rate of more than $9 7 \ \%$ of ATZ could be guaranteed within $2 4 \ \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ . The influence of different anions on the degradation efficiency of this system was displayed in Note.S11. The above experiments demonstrated the potential of the $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C / P M S }$ system for practical water applications.

# 4. Conclusion

In this study, catalyst with special structure, which was carbon coated ferric molybdenum sulfide on the Prusse blue core, was successfully synthesized by a special loading strategy. The carbon shell could not only inhibit the dissolution of metal ions, but also effectively adsorb and aggregate EPs. The adsorption of the carbon region did not affect the subsequent efficient oxidation and degradation of EPs by the iron‑molybdenum bimetallic material. Mechanistic investigations revealed that Fe (II) and Mo (IV) as active sites could activate PMS to produce a variety of ROS, including free radicals $( \mathrm { { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ and HO• ) and non-free radical (high-valent Fe species and $^ 1 { \cal O } _ { 2 } \dot { }$ ) which was also why different types of EPs were efficiently degraded. The reduction effect of molybdenum and edge sulfur on Fe (III) during the reaction significantly improved the activation performance of PMS during EPs degradation. The subsequent experimental results showed that this system had good cyclability. After seven degradations, the degradation rate could still reach $9 0 ~ \%$ , and it could achieve $1 0 0 ~ \%$ degradation of various pollutants. Further experiments of continuous water purification equipment showed that $\mathrm { F e M o S } _ { \mathrm { x } } @ \mathrm { C / P M S }$ system had stability and practical application value. These results provided new ideas for the development of efficient and fractional adsorption oxidation catalysts for sustainable water purification.
