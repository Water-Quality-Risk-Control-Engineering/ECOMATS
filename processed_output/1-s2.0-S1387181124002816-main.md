# . Introduction

3D porous carbon materials like activated carbon, carbon nanotubes, carbon fibers, or graphene are known to be very good adsorbents because of large specific surface area (SsA), porous structure and hydrophobic nature [1,2]. Removing various kinds of pollutants is a major application of porous carbons which are widely used in commercial water filters, as sorbents for removal oil spills, etc. [35]. Advanced sorption properties of porous carbons are related to very high surface area, which can be increased up to ${ \sim } 2 5 0 0 { - } 3 3 0 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ using advanced activation methods and pure carbon biochar's as precursors [69].

Extremely high surface area indicates atomically thin carbon pore walls. However, in contrast to graphene, porous carbons exhibit completely disordered amorphous structure and irregular shape of pore walls. Highly abundant defects on the surface of porous carbons can act as active centers for sorption [1,10]. These active sites can easily bond with other elements such as oxygen, nitrogen, chlorine, bromine, phosphorus, or sulfur, forming different surface functional groups which can significantly modify their adsorption capacity or catalytic properties [1013]. Hydrophobic nature of porous carbon is advantage for removal of certain pollutants from wastewater (e.g. oil) [14,15] but also disadvantage for sorption of other contaminants.

Among the elements that can modify the surface properties of carbon materials, oxygen is the most common. Different oxygen functionalities, such as phenol, quinone, lactone, carbonyl or carboxyl, can be formed on carbon surface [16] using a variety of oxidizing agents, including for example ${ \bf O } _ { 3 }$ [1719], $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ [1,13,16], persulfates [2025], permanganate [26,27], chromate [26], and $\mathrm { H N O _ { 3 } }$ [1,9,11,2831]. Depending on the method and conditions of oxidation (type of oxidant, oxidant concentration, temperature), the properties of modified carbon materials can vary significantly [11,28,32,33]. However, the main drawback of oxidized porous carbon materials is drastic loss of the SSA. It can be assumed that to improve the sorption properties of porous carbon materials, the concentration of surface oxygen complexes should be significantly increased while preserving high value of SSA [25,34]. It is expected also that oxygen functionalization will modify hydrophobic porous carbon precursors into hydrophilic materials thus also dramatically changing sorption properties.

A variety of oxidation treatments have been reported also for carbon nanotubes (CNT) [19,22,30,31,3539]. Oxygen functionalization of CNT surface improves its dispersibility, stability and hydrophilicity in aqueous solution. Surface oxygen functionalities can also act as catalyst in oxygen reduction reaction [30] or organic synthesis [37].

It is known that the increase in concentration of surface oxygen functional groups can significantly improve the adsorption capacity of different heavy metal ions such as copper, lead, chromium, mercury, etc. [4,23,29,40]. Recently our group demonstrated the mild oxidation method which allows to convert hydrophobic activated reduced graphene oxide (arGO) into hydrophilic super-oxidized porous carbon (SOPC) using oxidation treatment with saturated solution of ammonium persulfate [24]. This oxidation treatment had allowed firstly to achieve carbon to oxygen (C/O) ratio 3.3 for material with BET SSA of ${ \sim } 8 8 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ ${ \mathbf g } ^ { - 1 }$ .Optimization of oxidation time procedure resulted in even stronger oxidation degree corresponding to ${ \mathrm { C } } / { \mathrm { O } } = 2 . 4$ [25]. This is similar to the oxidation degree of standard graphene oxide (GO) which is typically in the range of $C / 0 = 2 \AA { - } 3$ [41,42]. The super-oxidized hydrophilic arGO with relatively high SSA of ${ \sim } 6 9 0 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ demonstrated an 8-fold increase in the uranium (VI) sorption compared to hydrophobic precursor arGO due to high abundance of oxygen surface groups.

However, the high cost of precursor arGO can be considered as a major hinder to industrial application of SOPC. Complex synthesis procedure in this case includes first oxidation of graphite using dangerous concentrated acids, then explosive thermal reduction of GO to produce reduced graphene oxide (rGO), and only after that activation can be applied to increase surface area in the final arGO [43,44]. Therefore, using more easily produced and less expensive activated carbons as a precursor for industrial scale manufacturing of SOPC would be a significant advantage. However, our initial attempts to apply the same oxidation treatment to the commercial activated carbon (Kuraray) resulted in complete collapse of porous structure with BET SSA dropping from ${ \sim } 2 5 0 0 \ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ to only ${ \sim } 4 0 \ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ [25]. It is obvious that the stability of different activated carbons to oxidation treatment is likely to depend on many factors, e.g. pore size, method of preparation, defect state, etc. Therefore, we decided to test ammonium persulfate oxidation method with several types of porous carbons, including activated carbons prepared from cheap bio-precursors using exactly the same activation methods as the one used for arGO synthesis. Our earlier studies demonstrated that details of activation method are more important than the type of biochar precursor. Nearly cost-free waste of wood industry was converted in our earlier experiments into activated carbons with BET SSA and pore size distribution nearly identical to arGO [45,46]. Pine and spruce cones, wood chips, bark and even spent mushroom substrate were used as precursors to produce nearly pure carbon biochar's and then converted into activated carbons with SSA exceeding $3 0 0 0 { \mathrm { ~ m } } ^ { 2 } { \mathrm { ~ g } } ^ { - 1 }$ [46,47].

Here we report the preparation of highly oxidized 3D porous carbon materials by "soft" oxidation method preserving micropores and a significant part of surface area while allowing to achieve a very high degree of oxidation on the level with the best graphene oxides. This method was applied to activated carbon produced from spruce cones, commercial porous carbon (PC), single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT), and activated graphene (arGO). Prolonged oxidation with ammonium persulfate resulted in synthesis of super-oxidized forms of spruce conederived activated carbon (SCAC), PC and arGO achieving C/O ratio ${ \sim } 2 . 1$ while oxidation of SWCNT was much less extensive resulting in C/ $0 \sim 3 . 3$ It is found that decrease of the SSA is related to collapse of larger pores, while micropores preserve after oxidation treatment. Removal of copper contaminant from water was used as an example demonstrating the influence of surface oxidation on sorption properties of SOPC. The sorption of Cu $( \sim 1 0 5 \mathrm { ~ m g ~ g ^ { - 1 } } )$ was found to increase as a result of oxidation showing for the best samples almost 8-fold increase compared to non-oxidized precursor.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials

Ammonium persulfate, $\mathrm { ( N H } _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 }$ $( > 9 9 ~ \% )$ was obtained from Signa-Aldrich (Germany), Copper (II) nitrate hemi(pentahydrate), Cu $( \mathrm { N O _ { 3 } } ) { \cdot } 2 { . } 5 \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ $( 9 8 + \% )$ were purchased from Fisher Scientific (USA), ammonium hydroxide, $\mathrm { N H _ { 4 } O H }$ $2 5 \% )$ were obtained from VWR (USA), potassium hydroxide, KOH $( > 8 5 \% )$ and Acetic acid (glacial), $\mathrm { C H _ { 3 } C O O H }$ $( 1 0 0 ~ \% )$ were purchased from Merck (Germany), Porous carbon (PC) and high-purity single-walled carbon nanotubes with large surface area (SWCNT) were purchased from ACS Materials (USA). This specific porous carbon was selected due to the large number of mesopores and produced by hard template method according to manufacturer. Graphite oxide produced by Hummers oxidation method (powder, 50100 mesh) was purchased from Abalonyx (Norway). All reagents were used as received without any further purification.

# 2.2. Synthesis of SCAC

Preparation of SCAC was described in detail in our earlier paper [45]. Shortly, scales from spruce cones were collected in the Umeå region (Sweden), washed repeatedly with ethanol and water, dried at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight, and carbonized by pyrolysis in the tube furnace at $5 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (heating rate $5 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \ \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 } $ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ under Ar flow. Next, the biochar was milled for 5 min using an oscillatory ball mill. $5 g$ of milled material was mixed with $_ { 4 0 \ g }$ of KOH, the mixture was transferred to the beaker containing $2 0 0 \mathrm { m l }$ of $7 5 \%$ ethanol and stirred magnetically overnight to provide homogeneous samples. The mixture was dried in vacuum oven at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $4 8 \ \mathrm { h }$ prior to activation treatment.

The activation was performed in a tube furnace under the flow of Ar. The sample was heated up to $2 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with a heating rate $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and to remove water and other volatile impurities kept at this temperature for $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . Then the temperature was increased to $8 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with heating rate $4 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and annealing was performed at this temperature for $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ . After cooling the activated sample was washed overnight with $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 1 L }$ of acetic acid solution $( 1 0 \mathrm { \ v o l \% } )$ under stirring in order to remove acid soluble impurities. Finally, the sample was washed with $4 \mathrm { L }$ of deionized (DI) water using vacuum filtration setup and dried overnight in vacuum oven at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ .

# 2.3. Synthesis of arGO

Synthesis of arGO was performed using the same procedure as in our earlier studies [25,45]. Hummers graphite oxide (HGO) was thermally exfoliated by shock thermal treatment. Large volume $_ { ( \sim 1 \mathrm { ~ L ~ } ) }$ aluminum cylinder with HGO powder was rapidly inserted into muffle furnace heated to temperature ${ \sim } 2 3 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ The reduced graphite oxide (rGO) produced by the thermal exfoliation was removed from the furnace after $8 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Next, precursor rGO was activated using the same procedure as for SCAC preparation.

# 2.4. Oxidation of carbon materials

Each of chosen carbon materials was oxidized using following procedure: $_ { 0 . 5 ~ g }$ of carbon material was placed in glass beaker containing $3 0 \mathrm { m L }$ saturated ammonium persulfate solution $_ { ( \sim 0 . 8 5 g }$ of $\mathrm { ( N H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { ) } _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 }$ per $^ { 1 \ m L }$ of $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } O } )$ and covered by aluminum foil to minimize water evaporation. The prepared mixture was stirred magnetically for 14 days at room temperature. Oxidized carbon materials were washed with DI water in a vacuum filtration setup followed by $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ of drying in a vacuum oven at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ , Prepared oxidized 3D porous carbon samples were named by adding -APS to the abbreviation of unoxidized sample (SWCNT-APS, PC-APS, SCAC-APS, and arGO-APS, respectively).

# 2.5.Testing of Cu(II) sorption

To evaluate the sorption capacity of porous carbon materials, batch sorption experiments were used. The sorption of $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ ions was examined under the following conditions: pH range 25, dose of $2 0 0 { - } 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m g }$ $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , and initial $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ concentration of $1 0 0 { - } 4 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L ^ { - 1 } }$ . Copper solutions were prepared by dissolving $\mathrm { C u ( N O _ { 3 } ) } \cdot 2 . 5 \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ in DI water and the pH was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH solutions. The porous carbons were exposed to solutions under continuous magnetic stirring for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . The carbon materials were separated from the copper solution by vacuum filtration. UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy was used to determine the copper concentrations in solution before and after its adsorption based on Beer's law analysis. For this purpose, the absorption of copper-ammonia complex was examined at the wavelength $6 0 7 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ .

# 2.6. Water vapor sorption

Testing of water vapor sorption was done using gravimetric method. The sample $( \sim 2 0 \ \mathrm { m g } )$ was exposed to increasing humidity while the changes in weight were measured using a magnetic suspension balance Rubotherm with $\pm 0 . 0 2 \mathrm { m g }$ accuracy. The balance was equipped with a specially prepared cell with installed SHT35 humidity sensor connected to Elegoo Mega 2560 R3 board. After the weight stabilization, a $5 ~ \mathrm { m l }$ beaker filled with water was placed in the cell and the cell was sealed. The humidity was increasing over time due to water evaporation from the beaker. The weight changes inside the sealed cell were measured until the humidity reached $7 0 \%$ .

# 2.7. Characterization

The nitrogen sorption isotherms were recorded using Autosorb iQ XR by Quantochrome at liquid nitrogen temperature. The relative pressure interval $\mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 }$ for the BET plot was selected using a procedure optimized for microporous materials. The slit-pore QSDFT equilibrium model was used to evaluate cumulative surface area, pore volume and pore size distribution. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed using a Kratos Axis Ultra electron spectrometer equipped with a delay line detector. A monochromatic $1 4 0 ~ \mathrm { W }$ Al $\mathtt { K @ }$ source was used as the excitation source. The binding energy scale was adjusted with respect to the C1s line of aliphatic carbon, set at $2 8 5 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using Vertex 80v spectrometer in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode equipped with diamond crystal under vacuum conditions. TGA was performed using a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC1 STARe System with a heating rate of $5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ $\operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ under a nitrogen flow with a rate of $4 0 \ \mathrm { m L } \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . UV-Vis absorption of copper-ammonia complexes were recorded in quartz cuvettes using a PerkinElmer Lambda 1050 spectrophotometer. The morphology of carbon materials was investigated by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) using a Hitachi HD 2700 microscope. The microscope was operated at $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { k V }$ acceleration voltage leading to $2 . 5 1 \ \mathrm { p . m }$ ,electrons wavelength and $1 0 \mu \mathrm { A }$ emission current. Structural studies were also performed by Z Contrast (HAADF  High Angle Annular Dark Field, ZC-mode) and Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) Oxford Instruments X-max 100 TLE detector was employed for elemental composition analysis.

# 3Results and discussion

# 3.1. Oxidation and characterization of high surface area carbon materials

Surface oxidation of carbon materials was performed in our study using saturated solution of ammonium persulfate (APS) and optimized in our earlier study for oxidation of arGO [25]. The treatment was performed at ambient temperature for 14 days using activated carbon (AC) produced from spruce cones (SCAC), commercial porous carbon (PC), arGO and single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT's). It is important to note that the activation procedure used in preparation of arGO and SCAC samples was exactly the same and the only difference was in the type of used precursor (rGO and spruce cone biochar, respectively).

Analysis of nitrogen sorption isotherms was used to characterize surface area and pore size distribution for all materials before and after oxidation treatment. Chemical composition and degree of oxidation were evaluated using XPS. Results presented in Table 1 clearly show strong difference in oxidation degree and surface area depending on the type of carbon precursor. Identical oxidation treatment resulted in very similar oxygen content for ammonium persulfate oxidized porous carbon (PC-APS), oxidized SCAC (SCAC-APS) and oxidized arGO (arGOAPS). Extraordinary high oxidation degree of these materials $\left( \mathbf { C } / \mathbf { O } \right) =$ 2.12.2) is comparable only to relatively strongly oxidized graphene oxides $( \mathrm { C } / 0 = 2 . 1 – 3 . 3 )$ [41,42,48].

To the best of our knowledge, activated carbons with oxidation on the level of ${ \mathrm { C } } / { \mathrm { O } } = 2 . 1$ in combination with high SSA up to ${ \sim } 8 0 0 { - } 1 0 0 0$ $\mathbf { m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathbf { g } ^ { - 1 }$

All three SOPC materials showed a strong decrease in BET SSA compared to precursors. This decrease is strongest for PC-APS with final SSA dropping to $1 7 . 5 \%$ of initial value (from 2000 to $3 5 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ .SCAC sample had highest initial sSA of $3 4 1 9 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ which dropped to 1156 $\mathbf { m } ^ { 2 } \bar { \mathbf { g } } ^ { - 1 }$ b $3 3 . 8 ~ \%$ of initial value) after the oxidation. A similar drop was also found for arGO $3 0 . 1 ~ \%$ from initial value). Nevertheless, the surface area remains to be extraordinarily high for materials with exceptionally very strong oxidation degree.

Sample of SwCNT's subjected to the same oxidation treatment showed much smaller oxidation degree $( \mathrm { C } / 0 = 3 . 3 )$ and $5 1 . 9 \%$ decrease in surface area. Smaller oxidation of SWCNT's is likely related to the small diameter $( 1 - 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ according to manufacturer) making significant part of their inner surface not accessible for the oxidant. The oxygen content achieved by persulfate oxidation $( \sim 2 3 \ \mathrm { a t \% } )$ is similar to the highest values reported for oxidized SwCNT's in the literature, typically achieved using $\mathrm { H N O } _ { 3 }$ or $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } S O _ { 4 } / H N O _ { 3 } }$ oxidation [49,50].

Analysis of pore size distribution before and after oxidation (Fig. 1) shows strong changes related mostly to collapse of larger pores. It is well known that SwCNT's with larger diameter are less stable under pressure conditions. The larger pores collapse first when pressure is increased while small diameter nanotubes survive even after treatment at rather GPa-range pressures [51,52]. Similar effect was also reported for CNT's subjected to strong oxidation treatments with larger diameter nanotubes being less stable [53]. Therefore, it could be anticipated that smaller pores in porous carbons will also be more stable under oxidation treatment. Indeed, all porous carbon materials studied here also showed nearly complete collapse of pores with diameter $2 { \cdot } 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ while the smallest pores $( < 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ mostly preserved. It can be noted that this effect can also be partly attributed to the easier penetration of oxidants into the mesopores. It is interesting that PC has the narrowest pore size distribution with mostly microporous structure. Nevertheless, oxidation causes a more drastic decrease in the SSA for this material as compared to SCAC and arGO. The difference in stability against oxidative treatment is likely related to different synthesis methods. Identical KOH activation was used to produce SCAC and arGO while PC is template-etched carbon according to the information provided by the manufacturer. It can be concluded that not only the size of pores but also the exact structure of pore walls has a very strong effect on stability of pores. This result is also in agreement with our earlier observation of the nearly complete collapse of pore structure after similar oxidation treatment of Kuraray activated carbon [25]. Samples produced by the same activation method (SCAC and arGO) also demonstrated strongest similarity in degree of oxidation, pore size distribution and change in SSA values after oxidation indicating similar structure of pore walls.

The exact structure of pore walls in porous carbons is nearly impossible to characterize, while it is known only that it is strongly disordered and curved. As rather rough and oversimplified approximation, the pore walls can be considered as atomically thin and extremely defected graphene oxide [24,25].

High resolution microscopy is one of few methods which allows to observe pores in carbon materials. Fig. 2 shows high resolution STEM images confirming the highly porous structure of studies materials, also showing that at least part of pores survived the oxidation treatment. The images also confirm that the pore diameter decreases notably (Fig. 2). Images recorded from PC sample before oxidation showed abundant pores with diameter ${ \sim } 5 { \mathrm { - } } 6 \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. S4). After oxidation the average pore diameter observed for PC decreased to $1 { - } 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . SCAC-APS also shows porous structure with a pore's diameter of ${ \sim } 2 \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. S5). Note that the smallest pores are difficult to image using STEM even using the highest magnification and that oxygen groups are likely to be removed by the electron beam.

It is interesting also to analyze in more details XPS spectra recorded before and after oxidation treatment, as it provides information not only about degree of oxidation but also some information about the type of surface oxygen functional groups. Deconvolution of C1s XPS spectra (Fig. 3 and Table 2) was performed following recommendations provided for analysis of oxidized carbons in ref [54]. The main component $( 2 8 5 . 0 \ \mathrm { \ e V } )$ is assigned to $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C - C / C = C }$ of non-oxidized carbons, the component at $2 8 6 . 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ is assigned to CO bonds (hydroxyl, epoxide, ether), 288.7 eV component is associated with $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = } 0$ bonds (carbonyl, carboxyl), and the last one at $2 9 1 . 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ corresponds to $\pi - \pi ^ { * }$ satellites (Fig. 3) [54-56]. We note that more detailed deconvolutions are sometimes shown in the literature (e.g. trying to resolve C-OH and C-O-C groups, or $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = } 0$ from COOH), but it can be considered as "overfitting" [54]. Quantitative analysis of the deconvolution (Table 2) shows similar ${ \sf C = } 0 / { \sf C - } 0$ ratios for SWCNT-APS, SCAC-APS and arGO-APS (0.57, 0.65 and 0.71, respectively). However, in the case of PC-APS the contribution of $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = } 0$ bonds is visibly higher than the contribution of CO as compared to the rest of the oxidized materials and ${ \sf C = } 0 / { \sf C - } 0$ ratio for PC-APS is equal to 0.99. It is known that carbonyl and carboxyl groups are mostly formed on the edges of graphene layers [57,58] and we assume that the PC-APS which porous structure is dominated by defects in the pore walls with more edges and holes, terminated by carbonyl and carboxyl groups. We note that oxygen functional groups could also partially block the access to micropores for nitrogen molecules contributing to the drop in the BET SSA registered by analysis of nitrogen sorption isotherms. However, it is difficult to estimate the contribution of this factor to the overall value of the SSA.

XPS is surface related method with rather small (few nm) penetration depths. Therefore, it is often complimented with FTIR spectroscopy for analysis of the type of oxygen functional groups. However, FTIR spectra of oxidized carbons does not provide quantitative information about relative abundance of different groups and typically analyzed in terms of changes in relative intensities of main peaks. Analysis of FT-IR spectra (Fig. 4) reveals the presence of a variety of oxygen functional groups (in agreement with XPS spectra). New features appearing as a result of oxidation in the spectra of oxidized porous carbons can be detected easily by comparing them with the spectra of unoxidized samples (Fig. S3).

Broad signals at around 3400 and $3 2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ are assigned to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups [48,59]. The peak with maximum at ${ \sim } 1 7 2 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ arises from a stretching mode of $\scriptstyle { \mathsf { C } } = 0$ bonds [1,59]. The relative intensity of this peak is noticeably higher for PC-APS than for other oxidized carbon samples. It suggests that the abundance of carbonyl and/or carboxyl groups on PC-APS surface is higher as compared to other three samples, which is in agreement with the XPS observations. The $1 6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ peak is the signal originating from water which is most likely adsorbed on the hydrophilic surface of oxidized carbon materials. The FTIR spectra shown here were recorded under vacuum and correspond only to water which is more strongly bound to the surface of materials. This feature is overlapping with the signal arising from CC bonds [59]. The signal at $1 3 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ is assigned to tertiary COH groups and the signal at $1 1 8 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ originates from phenolic OH groups [1,48]. Overall, the spectra of oxidized SCAC, PCAC and arGO show very strong similarity to FTIR spectra of graphite oxides. Similar type of oxygen functionalization was noted in our earlier studies of super-oxidized arGO [25]. New results prove that the similarity is not related to "graphene" origin of material as it is found also for carbons produced starting from spruce cone biochar and templated carbon. A more detailed comparing of SOPC to graphene oxide is provided in Supplementary Information (SI) file. As expected, the FT-IR spectrum recorded for SWCNT-APS differs strongly from the spectra of the other three samples due to its lower oxidation degree and rather different structure.

Thermal stability of 3D porous carbons before and after oxidation process was investigated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) performed under inert gas flow (Fig. 5). Unoxidized carbon samples show very good thermal stability with the maximum total weight loss of $7 \%$ recorded for SCAC. SWCNT are most stable (around $4 \%$ total weight loss), probably because of the lowest oxygen functionalities content. What is interesting, the PC sample shows drastic decrease in mass below $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ .Such a behavior is most likely due to the removal of water molecules trapped in PC's well-developed microporous structure.

Further characterization using TGA allows to detect temperature points and weight loss steps due to removal of oxygen functional groups. As expected, TGA traces of precursor materials showed only rather small weight loss $( 2 - 4 \%$ below $4 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and up to $6 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ up to $7 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ . Continuous removal of trace amount of oxygen groups over extended temperature interval is likely responsible for the rest of weight loss in all studied samples.

TGA traces of all super-oxidized carbon materials showed three major mass loss steps with temperature points first ${ \bf < } 1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } { \bf C } ,$ second around $2 0 0 \ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ ,and third around $3 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Fig. 5b). Some continuous weight loss is also present up to $7 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , The traces indicate the presence of a variety of oxygen functional groups on the surface of SOPC's. It is well known that the total amount of oxygen in the carbon sample cannot be directly calculated from TGA curve, because some carbon atoms are removed from the material due to formation of carbon oxides during its thermal treatment [60,61]. However, it can be clearly seen that the oxidized carbon samples with similar oxidation degree given by XPS show almost the same total mass loss of $\sim 5 4 ~ \%$ . Less oxidized SWCNT-APS sample is characterized by smaller total mass loss of ${ \sim } 3 9 $ $\%$ .

Summarizing results of this section, we found that not only arGO but also relatively inexpensive activated carbons and templated porous carbons can be oxidized to the same degree as strongly oxidized graphene/graphite oxides. Nearly identical properties were found for two porous carbon materials prepared by identical activation procedure using rGO and spruce cone-derived biochar as precursors. These properties include oxidation degree with ${ \mathrm { C } } / { \mathrm { O } } = 2 . 1 $ , narrow pore size distribution (mostly $< 1$ nm pores), similar thermal stability and type of oxygen functional groups revealed by FTIR and XPS.

SOPC's are materials with stable 3D structure in contrast to GO consisting of single 2D sheets loosely bound to each other but not forming pore network in multilayered assemblies. Graphite oxides and multilayered GO assemblies exhibit negligible BET surface area if measured using nitrogen sorption and surface area of maximum $7 0 0 { - } 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m } ^ { 2 } ~ g ^ { - \bar { 1 } } }$ if measured by sorption of certain molecules in solutions (e.g. methylene blue) [62]. Unlike GO which needs to be dispersed in water to provide access to full surface area, porous 3D structure of SOPC materials is stable in aqueous solutions and consists of micrometer-size particles. Therefore, removal of pollutants can be done for SOPC by simple immersion of powder material (e.g. in suitable net) into contaminated water and removal after sorption. The dispersing treatments (e.g. sonication) and removal of dispersed sheets by vigorous centrifuging (after sorption) which are required for GO sorption experiments are not needed for SOPC. In fact, strong sonication of SOPC might result in the collapse of 3D porous structure and best to be avoided.

The chemical composition and geometric microstructure of solid surface strongly affects it hydrophilicity [63]. It is well known that the introduction of oxygen functional groups on carbon material surface makes it more hydrophilic, for example when hydrophobic graphene is converted into hydrophilic GO [32,64]. Similarly, hydrophobic porous carbons were converted into hydrophilic SOPC's in our experiments. Simple experiments confirming hydrophilic nature of SOPC's were performed using sensitive balance and measurements of weight increase due to sorption of water from vapors at different humidity levels, starting from ambient $( 3 5 ~ \% )$ and up to $7 0 ~ \%$ (Fig. 6). As expected, a significantly stronger increase in weight was observed for the carbon materials with increased abundance of oxygen functional groups despite a significant decrease in BET SSA after oxidation process. For arGO-APS, SCAC-APS, and PC-APS, the weight increase at $7 0 \%$ humidity was equal to 13.8, 8.5, and $5 . 4 \%$ , respectively. For unoxidized arGO, SCAC, and PC materials the weight increase was equal to 2.9, 1.6, and $^ { 3 , 2 \% }$ respectively. Conversion of hydrophobic porous carbons into hydrophilic materials was earlier demonstrated for arGO using dynamic vapor sorption (DVS) method which provides complete water sorption isotherms [24]. SOPC's exhibit water sorption isotherms very similar to those recorded from bulk and known to be hydrophilic graphite oxide, see example also in SI file. It can be concluded that increased abundance of oxygen functional groups on the surface of SOPC makes it more hydrophilic. SOPC's are functionalized with several oxygen functional groups, e.g. carboxyls, carbonyls, hydroxyls, all known to increase hydrophilicity [65,66]. However, relative contributions of these groups to hydrophilicity cannot be distinguished using our data.

# 3.2. Results of Cu(II) sorption experiments

Unprecedentedly high oxidation degree in combination with relatively high BET SSA makes SOPC's prepared in our experiments rather promising for applications related to removal of various contaminants from polluted water. Rather high abundance of oxygen functional groups provides potential sorption sites for many different types of contaminants. In this study we demonstrated how high degree of oxidation affects sorption of metal ions from aqueous solutions using simple sorption tests aimed on removal of $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ .

Removal of $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ by PC, SCAC and arGO before and after oxidation was verified using batch sorption experiments. SwCNT-APS samples were not tested due to significantly lower oxidation degree achieved for this material. The detailed data collected for $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption are presented in SI file. Table 3 shows the highest $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption capacities found for tested carbon materials before and after oxidation.

It is found that the strong oxidation of 3D porous carbons significantly improves the sorption capacity of $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ ions. PC, SCAC and arGO after oxidation show 7.9-, 3.7- and 5.4-fold higher sorption capacity for $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ ions, respectively. Somewhat surprisingly, the highest $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption capacity is found for PC-APS which is characterized by the smallest BET SSA of $3 5 1 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \ : g ^ { - 1 }$ . SCAC-APS and arGO-APS with almost the same oxidation degree and significantly higher BET SSA (1156 and $8 1 2 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ respeively show the $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption cpacits wer y ${ \sim } 3 0 \mathrm { m g g } ^ { - 1 }$ . This phenomenon can be explained by the relatively higher abundance of carboxyl functional groups on PC-APS surface compared to SCAC-APS and arGO-APS. The literature data shows that the carboxylic groups are most likely responsible for copper sorption [23,67] and sorption of many other contaminants [24,25,58]. XPS data presented in Table 2 show that PC has the largest fraction of $\scriptstyle { \mathsf { C } } = 0$ bonds among oxygen functional groups. We found that the ${ \sf C = } 0 / { \sf C - } 0$ ratio calculated based on deconvolution of C1s XPS spectra is correlated with $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption (Fig. 7). It can be assumed that total abundance of $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = } 0$ groups is more important for increase of $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption capacity than the value of BET SSA.

Analysis of XPS spectra in the $\mathtt { C u 2 p }$ region for PC-APS and SCAC-APS samples after copper sorption is presented in Fig. 8. Two peaks at ${ \sim } 9 3 4 $ and ${ \sim } 9 5 4 \mathrm { e V }$ can be assigned to $\mathrm { C u } 2 \mathsf { p } _ { 3 / 2 }$ and $\mathrm { C u 2 p 1 / 2 }$ and they consist of two signals [6870]. The weaker signals at 932.6 and 952.6 are associated with the presence of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ species and the stronger ones at 934.9 and $9 5 4 . 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ are associated with the presence of $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ species. The presence of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ species on both samples surface is most likely caused by the photo-reduction of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ which can occur during the XPS spectrum acquisition [70]. The observed shake-up satellite peaks at ${ \sim } 9 4 2$ and $9 6 3 \ \mathrm { \ e V }$ prove the charged state of copper adsorbed on oxidized carbon surface [68,69].

High resolution STEM images of PC-APS and SCAC-APS after copper sorption revealed absence of clearly visible nanoparticles and that the surface morphology was not largely affected by Cu sorption (Fig. 9a and b). For both materials, a homogenous nanoporous structure can be observed. EDX results (Fig. 9c) confirmed that the copper is homogeniously distributed over the whole carbon materials surface.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, the prolonged oxidation of certain porous carbons by saturated ammonium persulfate solution can be used to produce superoxidized carbon materials with extraordinary high oxidation degree in combination with high SSA.

- Both SCAC and arGO showed excellent stability against oxidation preserving large fraction of SSA after surface oxidation treatment.

![](images/879825c02ffac4a9ce806cc7510e9e77dd7cb407b6e486aed2533781b4c675a7.jpg)

- Oxidation of high surface area porous carbons resulted in addition of the variety oxygen functional groups (hydroxyl, epoxide, carbonyl and carboxyl) which converted hydrophobic precursors into hydrophilic materials.

- Surface-oxidized SCAC, arGO and PC showed C/O ratio ${ \sim } 2 . 1$ in combination with high surface areas of $1 1 5 6 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , $8 1 2 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , and $3 5 1 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively.

- Oxidized SWCNT showed smaller degree of oxidation with C/O ${ \sim } 3 . 3$ Therefore, we successfully synthesized porous carbons with oxidation degree which is typical for conventional graphene oxides.

- Strong surface oxidation resulted in collapse of mesopores for all tested materials while micropores showed stronger stability to oxidation treatment. Therefore, further increase of SSA is likely posisble to achieve even for materials with strongest oxidation degree $\mathrm { C } / 0 \sim 2 . 1 { - 2 . 2 } )$ by using precursors with larger part of micropores in the pore size distribution.

- The surface-oxidized porous carbons demonstrated most important properties very similar to graphene oxides, e.g. thermal stability, type of oxygen functionalization, and hydrophylicity.

It is expected that sorption properties of SOPC's will be very similar to defect-rich graphene oxides making it attractive alternative for removal of pollutants from contaminated water. However, the 3D structure of sOPC with fixed pore size can be considered as a strong advantage for sorbent applications, compared to graphene oxides which need to be dispersed in order to make all surface area accessible for sorption of pollutants.

As an example, we studied $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption by SOPC revealing significant improvement of $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption capacity due to oxidation, as compared to precursor porous carbons. The highest $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption of $1 0 5 ~ \mathrm { m g } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ was found for PC-APS sample which was almost 8-fold higher than $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ sorption capacity of PC. It was revealed that the Cu (II) sorption capacity is favored by larger abandance of $\scriptstyle { \mathsf { C } } = 0$ groups, rather than showing direct correlation with surface area values. Therefore, we demonstrate that the type of precursor has extremely strong effect on oxidation degree, pore size distribution and the type of surface functional groups as a result of identical oxidation treatment.

Moreover, we demonstrate that SOPC with high oxidation degree (C/ O up to 2.1) in combination with $1 1 0 0 \ m ^ { 2 } \ \bar { \ g } ^ { - 1 }$ SSA can be easily synthesized by treatment of relatively inexpensive activated carbons produced using cost-free waste of wood industry (spruce cones). Therefore, using SOPC for water puririfcation will also contribute to recycling of forest industry waste. The cone-derived SOPC is clearly beneficial for scaled up industrial production as compared to earlier reported arGO which is a lot more expensive and requires more complex and environmanetally dangerous synthesis procedures. Moreover, our results indicate that modification of oxidation treatment allowing higher abudance of carboxylic groups is likely to result in even higher sorption capacity of SOPC. We expect that high sorption capacity will be demonstarted in future studies of SOPC allowing to remove variety of contaminants from polluted water. Therefore, converting hydrophobic porous carbons into hydrophilic materials with high degree of surface oxidation and high SSA opens new posisbilities for using in water filters and removal of contaminants from waste solutions.
