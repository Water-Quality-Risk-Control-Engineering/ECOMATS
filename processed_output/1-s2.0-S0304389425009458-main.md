Although metal clusters are generally considered as impurities accompanying the synthesis of single atom catalysts, their extraordinary potential in regulating single atom catalytic reactions has attracted widespread attention. In this study, iron cluster decorated single atom iron catalyst (Fe-SACAs) were synthesized to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) for the removal of organic pollutants, and a differential PMS activation mechanism induced by selective adsorption of pollutants was observed for the first time. The chemical coordination between pollutants and active sites was identified as the dominant chemisorption mechanism for the adsorption of organic pollutants by Fe-SACAs. The competitive occupation of PMS activation sites by pollutants induced by the diversity of targeted pollutant molecules in ionization potentials was thereby revealed to be the inducement for the adsorption-dependent oxidation. The adsorption-dependent oxidation mechanism was fully elucidated using density functional theory (DFT). Finally, an optimized treatment process was proposed based on the adsorptiondependent oxidation mechanism, which achieved zero oxidant residue and pollutant emission simultaneously. This study unveils the crucial effect of reactant mass transfer and adsorption on the oxidation process in FeSACAs mediated Fenton-like reactions.

# 1. Introduction

With the increasing shortage of water resources, the issue of organic micropollutants in natural water has garnered global attention. Although most artificially synthesized organic compounds released into the environment exhibit limited short-term impacts on humans, their structural stability and antioxidant activity enable them to diffuse easily within ecosystems [1]. The accumulation of high concentrations of these pollutants in the human body through the food chain has been shown to be teratogenic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic [2]. Although measures have been taken to address the limitations of traditional water treatment processes in dealing with organic pollutants, issues such as high energy consumption, high maintenance costs, and secondary pollution remain significant obstacles to the large-scale application of these technologies [3,4]. The satisfactory performance of heterogeneous advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) in water treatment offers a potential solution for the effective disposal of organic pollutants. The combination of catalysts and oxidants can accelerate the degradation and mineralization of organic pollutants, involving mechanisms such as radical and nonradical pathways [5–7]. Most importantly, the advantages of low leakage and energy consumption endow heterogeneous AOPs with promising prospects.

Recently, single atom catalysts (SACs) have emerged as prominent candidates among heterogeneous catalysts due to their ultrahigh atomic utilization. SACs possess the advantages of both heterogeneous and homogeneous transition metal catalysts and usually exhibit superior performance in activating oxidants [8]. Especially in AOPs based on persulfate, the mechanism of persulfate activation can be directionally regulated by designing the coordination structure and the atomic $d$ -band center of SACs, thus realizing the efficient degradation of various pollutants [9,10]. As reported by Hu et, al., the coordination structure of Fe-pyridinic $\mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ not only created the catalytic sites from the electron-rich Fe single atom but also produced electron-poor carbon atoms neighboring pyridinic N as binding sites for persulfate activation [11]. The research of Zhang et, al. showed that the distance of two iron single atoms effected the generating high valent metal species [12]. In fact, although there are still some controversies, the mechanism at the atomic orbital level has been fully revealed due to the clear coordination structure of SACs. Upon reviewing extensive works on SACs, we found a point that may have been neglected for a long time. Metal clusters are typically considered as impurities that should be avoided during the synthesis of single atom catalysts [13,14]. However, recent studies revealed that metal clusters possessed potential in optimizing electronic configuration modulation of atomic sites and regulating redox reactions. For example, the electron shuttle between the adjacent clusters to $\mathrm { C o - N _ { X } }$ was enhanced, increasing the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) catalytic performance [15]. The cooperation of Fe clusters and $\mathrm { F e - N } _ { 4 }$ redistributed the charge configuration, thus promoting product desorption and reactant readsorption [16]. Significantly, viewed from the thermodynamic theories, atomic metals tend to oxidize and aggregate into more stable metal clusters during storage or long-term catalytic reactions [17]. Therefore, the mechanism of single atom catalysis is not immutable, and the sustainable activity of single atom catalysts may also be related to the balance between passivation and evolution of atomic sites. It can be said that understanding the role of metal clusters cooperated single atom catalysts in persulfate activation is necessary.

The strong adsorption behavior of metal clusters and atomic metals towards organic pollutants provides a new insight into the investigation of catalytic mechanism, and coincidentally, these adsorption sites that can capture pollutants are also essential catalytic sites for activating persulfate [18–20]. It is well known that adsorption is the initial step in all heterogeneous catalytic reactions. The mass transfer of oxidants or pollutants from the aqueous phase to the solid phase, or the mass transfer of reactive species from the solid phase to the aqueous phase, all involve adsorption and desorption [21]. However, it remains unclear whether the activation of persulfate is affected by the considerable adsorption of pollutants, especially when persulfate and pollutants share active sites [21]. Unfortunately, there is a lack of in-depth discussion on this point. The most common approach is still to adjust the dosage of the catalyst to minimize the influence of adsorption or to achieve adsorption equilibrium of the catalyst before the oxidation reaction. However, these simple treatments present clear problems. The oxidation process is difficult to fit within common catalytic kinetic models, and the synergy or antagonism between adsorption and oxidation is completely ignored [22,23].

As a result, to comprehensively consider the above process, a Fe cluster decorated atomic Fe catalyst (Fe-SACAs) was synthesized for activating peroxymonosulfate (PMS). In this study, firstly, the coordination structure of atomic Fe and Fe clusters in Fe-SACAs was clarified. Secondly, the adsorption mechanism of organic pollutants by Fe-SACAs was investigated. By combining experimental results with theoretical calculations, the potential adsorption-dependent oxidation mechanism was unveiled. Finally, the treatment process was optimized based on the adsorption-dependent oxidation mechanism for efficient PMS utilization and deep contaminant detoxification. This work provides a new perspective for the study of single atom catalysis.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Chemicals

Potassium peroxymonosulfate was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich®, and other reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. All reagents were analytical grade and used as received without further purification. Ultrapure water was used in all experiments.

# 2.2. Synthesis of Fe-SACAs

$1 . 3 g$ of melamine and $4 \mathrm { m L }$ of formaldehyde were dissolved in $1 6 \mathrm { m L }$ of ultrapure water under magnetic stirring at $3 4 3 \ \mathrm { K } .$ Then, $_ { 0 . 2 \ g }$ of ferrous chloride was added into the solution that had been adjusted to $\mathtt { p H 1 . 6 }$ using hydrochloric acid. After aging for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ at $3 6 8 \mathrm { K } ,$ the formed hydrogel was freeze dried and then carbonized at $1 1 2 3 \mathrm { K }$ for $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ under a $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ flow with a heating rate of $2 7 9 \mathrm { K / m i n }$ . Then, the aerogel was washed with dilute hydrochloric acid (1 M) until no metal ions was detected in the filtrate. Finally, the product was washed, collected, and dried to obtain Fe-SACAs. Especially, samples synthesized without adding ferrous chloride was labeled as CAs.

Characterization, experimental, analytic and calculation methods are provided in Texts S1-S5 and Table S1 in Supporting Information.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Characterization of Fe-SACAs

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to determine the crystalline phase of Fe-SACAs. As shown in Figure S1, only a diffraction peak at $2 \theta = 2 6 ^ { \circ }$ , representing the (002) lattice plane of graphitic carbon, was observed in the sample [24,25]. This suggests that no exposed Fe (or oxide) nanoparticles remained in the sample after acid pickling. Notably, Fe-SACAs exhibited a sharper and stronger diffraction peak intensity compared to CAs, indicating that the introduction of Fe elevated the crystallinity of CAs. Higher crystallinity demonstrates enhanced structural regularity and conductivity of the graphite layer, which is conducive to the shuttling of electron [24].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 1a and b) reveal that Fe-SACAs exhibited an open porous structure consisting of randomly oriented graphitic domains. Specifically, some monodispersed Fe clusters wrapped with carbon were found in the graphite layer of Fe-SACAs. Due to their relatively low content and the masking effect of the carbon network, Fe clusters are hardly recognizable by XRD. EDS-Mapping images further show the elemental composition of Fe-SACAs. As displayed in Fig. 1c, except for the main C element, Fe and N elements were uniformly dispersed on the Fe-SACAs surface. This is inconsistent with what was observed in TEM images, where only monodispersed Fe clusters were found, implying the formation of Fe single atom sites during sample synthesis.

Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) characterization was performed to further verify the atomically dispersed Fe and its coordination information. The Fe K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) curve (Fig. 1d) clearly shows that the absorption edge of Fe-SACAs was located between those of iron phthalocyanine (FePc) and Fe foil, suggesting that the valence state of Fe-SACAs $_ { ( 0 - + 2 ) }$ was lower than that of Fe single atom catalysts with saturated coordination $( + 2 \cdot + 3 )$ . This could be attributed to the decline of the Fe-N bond and the formation of metallic Fe [26]. Furthermore, the FT $\mathrm { k } ^ { 2 }$ -weighted EXAFS spectrum in the R-space of Fe-SACAs (Fig. 1e) shows two peaks at around 1.5 and $2 . 2 \mathring \mathrm { A } .$ , corresponding to Fe-N(O) and Fe-Fe scattering paths, respectively [27]. This demonstrates the coexistence of atomic Fe and Fe clusters in Fe-SACAs. Given the approximate peak positions of Fe-N and Fe-O and the uncertain coordination number of Fe, the quantitative coordination configuration of Fe in Fe-SACAs was obtained by FT-EXAFS fitting [28]. Table S2 gives the corresponding quantitative structural parameters. As seen, the coordination numbers of Fe-N and Fe-Fe in Fe-SACAs were 2.7 and 5.9, respectively. Therefore, the best-fitting structure of atomic Fe and Fe clusters in Fe-SACAs was identified as Fe- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { N } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { F e } _ { 6 }$ , respectively (Fig. 1f). In addition, the signals detected at around 7.5 and $3 . 8 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ in $k$ -space in the EXAFS wavelet transform (WT) contour plots of Fe-SACAs (Fig. 1g) were consistent with the reference Fe foil (Fig. 1h) and FePc (Fig. 1i), further verifying that the backscattered atoms were N and Fe [29]. Meanwhile, the stronger Fe-Fe signal indicates the predominance of Fe clusters in Fe-SACAs.

# 3.2. Contribution of adsorption in heterogeneous oxidation system

The performance of the Fe-SACAs/PMS system was evaluated using nitrobenzene (NB), benzoic acid (BA), $p$ -hydroxybenzoic acid (HBA), and bisphenol A (BPA) as the target contaminant. As shown in Fig. 2a, compared to the catalytic inactivity of pristine CAs for PMS activation (data not provided), the Fe-SACAs/PMS system achieved satisfactory removal of four pollutants within 30 minutes of reaction time. Herein, we observed considerable adsorption of Fe-SACAs to four pollutants. Fig. 2b shows that $2 2 \%$ of NB, $2 0 \%$ of BA, $3 2 \%$ of HBA, and $4 3 \%$ of BPA was enriched by an equal dosage of Fe-SACAs within the same reaction time. Adsorption is well-known to be one of the fundamental process in heterogeneous catalysis, as the adsorption and bonding of PMS and pollutants from the aqueous phase to active sites are prerequisites for the catalytic reaction [21]. Therefore, the pollutants concentration and Fe-SACAs dosage were optimized to further study the adsorption process (Fig. 2c).

# 3.2.1. Adsorption thermodynamics

To further reveal the adsorption behavior of pollutants on Fe-SACAs, the adsorption performance was evaluated with initial pollutants concentrations ranging from 0 to $2 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ at temperatures of 298, 308, and $3 1 8 \mathrm { K }$ $\mathrm { 0 { - } 5 0 \ m g / L }$ for BPA at 308 and 318 K). Fig. 2d and S2 show that higher temperatures boosted molecular thermal motion, thus facilitating the accumulation of pollutants on Fe-SACAs. From Tables S3-S6, compared to the Freundlich model, the Langmuir model was more suitable for describing the adsorption of pollutants on Fe-SACAs. Of course, it should be noted that the Freundlich model used to describe the adsorption process is not entirely unreliable $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } > 0 . 9 )$ . This demonstrates that the uptake of pollutants by Fe-SACAs were consistent with the characteristics of both the monolayer and multilayer adsorption [30, 31].

The standard free-energy change $\left( \Delta \mathbf { G } ^ { \circ } \right)$ , the standard enthalpy change $\left( \Delta \mathrm { H } ^ { \circ } \right)$ , and the standard entropy change $( \Delta \mathbf { S } ^ { \circ } )$ were calculated from the temperature-dependent adsorption isotherms to investigate the in-depth process about internal energy changes associated with adsorption. The dimensionless $\mathrm { K } ^ { 0 }$ was calculated by fitting the Freundlich model to the initial stage of the adsorption isotherm according to the method in Text S3, while $\Delta \mathrm { H } ^ { \circ }$ and $\Delta \mathbf { S } ^ { \circ }$ were determined from the slope and intercept of the ln $\mathrm { K } ^ { 0 }$ and $1 / \mathrm { T }$ curves [32]. As shown in Table S7, The negative $\Delta \mathsf { G } ^ { \circ }$ values suggest that the adsorption of pollutants onto Fe-SACAs were thermodynamically feasible and spontaneous [33]. Moreover, the positive $\Delta \mathrm { H } ^ { \circ }$ values and $\Delta \mathbf { S } ^ { \circ }$ values indicate that the adsorption processes were endothermic and entropy-increasing [34].

# 3.2.2. Adsorption mechanism

To investigate the adsorption mechanism and recognize the active sites of Fe-SACAs for the removal of pollutants, linear-regression analysis between the adsorption partition coefficient (Kd) of pollutants in initial stage of adsorption isotherm and the specific descriptors of pollutants were conducted to discern the interaction mechanism between contaminants and Fe-SACAs. The $\mathbf { l g } ( \mathbf { K d _ { \tau } ( L / k g ) } )$ values of NB, BA, HBA, and BPA at an initial pollutant concentration of $2 \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ were calculated to be 4.2, 4.1, 4.2, and 4.6, respectively.

The dipole moment $\ l ( \mu )$ is related to the hydrophilic groups of organic molecules, with organic contaminants having higher $\mu$ values preferring to interact with the adsorbent via hydrogen bonds [35]. From Fig. 2e, it can be seen that the correlation between the $\mu$ values and adsorption capacity was poor, suggesting the adsorption process was not controlled by this mechanism. The hydrophobic interaction between organic pollutants and carbon materials is considered a common adsorption mechanism. Herein, the hydrophobicity of organic molecules is characterized by the octanol-water partition coefficient (Kow) [36,37]. As shown in Fig. 2f, the correlation coefficient $( R ^ { 2 } )$ for Kow with adsorption capacity was 0.84. This demonstrates that hydrophobic interaction might be the main mechanism for Fe-SACAs to enrich pollutants. Moreover, the ionization potential (IP) represents the attraction of substituents on the benzene ring to electrons. Benzene derivatives with higher IP possess lower $\pi$ electron density and are more easily adsorbed onto carbon materials through $\pi { - } \pi$ electron donor-acceptor interactions [38]. Fig. 2g shows that $\pi { - } \pi$ electron donor-acceptor interactions could not be absolutely ignored in the adsorption process with $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.42.

In addition to $\pi { - } \pi$ electron donor-acceptor interactions, the IP values are also related to chemisorption between adsorption sites and functional groups [18]. Moreover, Figure S3 shows that CAs with larger contact angle can hardly adsorb pollutants (data not provided), indicating that hydrophobic interaction might not the only adsorption mechanism. Since CAs was ineffective for the adsorption of pollutants, Fe species were identified as the only possible adsorption sites. In this case, various metals (Fe, Cu, Co) were introduced to CAs to investigate the critical role of Fe sites in chemical adsorption. Fig. 2h shows that the adsorption of pollutants by SACAs was highly dependent on the doping metal. For example, BPA removal by Fe-SACAs, Cu-SACAs, and Co-SACAs were $8 5 ~ \%$ , $1 2 \%$ , and $8 2 \%$ , respectively. It is interesting that the vastly different adsorption capacities were closely related to the coordination environment of the doping metal. According to valence shell orbital hybridization theory, the available 3d orbital numbers of Fe and Co were 2 and 1, respectively, facilitating the coordination of electron pairs-rich atoms and enabling chemical adsorption. However, Cu with a full $_ { 3 d }$ orbital determined the insignificant adsorption of pollutants by Cu-SACAs.

The above results indeed verify the vital role of the physical absorption represented by hydrophobic interaction and the chemical interaction between atomic Fe/Fe clusters and pollutants in the adsorption process. Concretely, organic molecules with available coordination atoms can be rapidly adsorbed onto the Fe sites of Fe-SACAs.

Significantly, atomic Fe or Fe clusters may exhibit various adsorption preferences for pollutants with different substituents. However, the above process is first controlled by the mass transfer of pollutants from aqueous phase. Pollutants with lower hydrophobicity (lower Kow) are difficult to quickly approach the active sites on the surface of Fe-SACAs through physical adsorption processes such as hydrophobic interactions, resulting in the less efficient adsorption of Fe-SACAs to other pollutants than BPA.

# 3.3. Synergy and antagonism between adsorption and oxidation

To summarize, the sharing of catalytic sites between pollutants and oxidants may lead to elusive catalytic mechanisms, and the enrichment of organic pollutants on the catalyst does indeed affect subsequent PMS activation [39]. These effects challenge the effectiveness of traditional methods in identifying catalytic mechanisms.

# 3.3.1. Identification of reactive species

Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was performed to indentify the possible generation of radicals and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ in the catalytic system without pollutants presence. As shown in Fig. 3a, although a triplet-line signal representing $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ were found in the FeSACAs/PMS system, the signal intensity, which was similar to that of the sole PMS system, excludes $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ generation in the catalytic system (data not provided) [40]. Besides, the feeble superoxide radical $( \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ signal also denies the contribution of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ to pollutants degradation [41]. Significantly, instead of the typical sulfate radical $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ and hydroxyl radical ( • OH) signals, a seven-line signal attributed to the DMPOX adducts was detected in the Fe-SACAs/PMS system, providing equivocal information (Fig. 3b) [42]. To confirm the origin of the DMPOX adducts, spin trapping agents were added into the filtered solution without the catalyst. The vanished signal suggests that high valence iron $( { \mathrm { F e } } ( { \mathrm { I V } } ) =$ O), formed through the oxidation of Fe sites by PMS rather than leaked Fe ions, might be the main cause of the DMPOX adducts [43]. Confusingly, this conjecture was not reasonably supported by the experimental result using PMSO as the probe (Fig. 3c) [44]. Although the concentration of degraded PMSO was higher compared to the sole PMS system, the $\bf { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ yield in the Fe-SACAs/PMS system was not significantly improved. Figure S4 also shows that the conversion rate of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ $\left( \boldsymbol { \eta } [ \mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } } ] \right)$ in the Fe-SACAs/PMS system was lower than that in the sole PMS system, suggesting that $\mathrm { F e ( I V ) { = } } 0$ was not the main reactive species in the Fe SACAs/PMS system. The DMPOX adducts might be formed by the excessive oxidation of DMPO-radical adducts, while radicals were considered as the main reactive species in the Fe SACAs/PMS system.

# 3.3.2. Study of electron transfer process

The electron transfer process has recently been considered a common nonradical pathway in PMS activation induced by single atom catalysts [45]. Fig. 2a shows the removal of various contaminants by the Fe-SACAs/PMS system. It can be observed that pollutants with electron-donating groups are rapidly degraded, while pollutants with electron-withdrawing groups exhibit oxidation resistance. Although the degradation dependence on molecular structure suggests an obvious electron transfer mechanism [46], the removal of NB and BA by the Fe-SACAs/PMS system still reached $5 2 \%$ and $4 4 ~ \%$ , respectively. This implies that the Fe-SACAs/PMS system might switch oxidation modes depending on the pollutant type. A comparison of various pollutants adsorbed by Fe-SACAs (Fig. 2b) shows that oxidation contributed more to the removal than adsorption for all four typical pollutants. To further verify the electron transfer process, a galvanic oxidation reactor was constructed with PMS and pollutants in two separate cells (Fig. 3d) [47]. Fig. 3e shows that instantaneous currents were generally positively correlated with the degradation efficiency of contaminants (Fig. 2a). Additionally, negligible degradation of NB and BA was observed in the galvanic oxidation reactor (data not provided), demonstrating that pollutants with high IP were mainly degraded by radical oxidation.

Since the removal of pollutants involves both adsorption and oxidation, the reactions poorly fitted the pseudo-first-order kinetic model (Figure S5a). Therefore, considering that adsorption occurred in the first 5 minutes, revised $k _ { o b s }$ values were obtained by fitting the reactions with a stepwise pseudo-first-order kinetic model. As shown in Figure S5b, the $k _ { o b s }$ values for NB, BA, HBA, and BPA degradation were 0.02, 0.01, 0.04, and $0 . 1 2 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. Moreover, PMS depletion during oxidation was monitored. As shown in Fig. 3f, nearly $9 0 \%$ of PMS was consumed in the Fe-SACAs/PMS system under ultrapure water background, suggesting that Fe-SACAs possesses superior capability in activating PMS to generate reactive species. However, the addition of different pollutants resulted in distinct PMS decomposition trends.

Specifically, the involvement of contaminants that can be degraded by electron transfer processes dramatically reduced PMS consumption to less than $3 0 \%$ , contrary to previous literature suggesting that accelerated PMS decomposition triggered by low IP pollutants is characteristic of electron transfer processes [48]. However, the presence of high IP pollutants did not significantly alter the PMS attenuation trend. Above results suggest that the potential adsorption-dependent oxidation mechanism is highly correlated with the types of pollutants.

# 3.3.3. Targeted contaminant oriented adsorption-dependent oxidation

Herein, BPA was chosen as a representative pollutant with low IP value. Gradient preadsorption and premixing times were set to investigate the adsorption-dependent oxidation process. As displayed in Figure S6, the BPA removal improved with increasing preadsorption time. However, it should be noted that as the concentration of BPA in solution approaches equilibrium, the adsorption efficiency decreases. Therefore, the enhancement of the subsequent oxidation by preadsorption is speculated to be one of the possible reasons. Then, PMS depletion under different conditions was measured, as shown in Figure S7. Surprisingly, the negative correlation between PMS consumption and preadsorption time contradicts the above speculation. Specifically, the ratios of pollutant removal concentration to PMS decomposition concentration were 36.9, 40.1, 44.7, and $5 4 . 5 \mathrm { m g / m m o l }$ for preadsorption times of 0, 5, 10, and 20 minutes, respectively. This indicates that preadsorption actually enhanced the electron transfer process, and a reasonable explanation is that the preoccupation of Fe clusters by BPA prevented rapid PMS decomposition via radical pathway, which has a much lower PMS utilization rate than electron transfer process [46]. Nevertheless, the release of the underutilized oxidants also leaves an environmental problem.

Furthermore, Fe-SACAs were premixed with PMS in ultrapure water (UPW) for 5, 10, and 20 minutes to investigate the effect of PMS preferential occupation on BPA oxidation. As displayed in Figure S8, the rapid decomposition of PMS by Fe-SACAs reduced the residual PMS concentration, leading to the suppression of BPA oxidation, consistent with observations in Figure S9. From Fig. 3f, the residual PMS concentrations after premixing for 5 and 10 minutes were 0.45 and $0 . 2 5 \mathrm { m M }$ , respectively. However, such efficient performance was difficult to achieve by adding PMS of the same concentration $( k _ { o b s }$ values differed by three times). By excluding the enhanced adsorption of BPA and the boosted activation of PMS by Fe-SACAs washed with $^ { 1 \mathrm { m M } }$ of PMS for 30 minutes (Figure S10 and S11), it can be concluded that both the preoccupation of Fe sites by PMS and BPA can promote the electron transfer process.

For NB, a typical pollutant with high IP value, the situation was quite different. Figure S12 shows that preadsorption barely affected NB oxidation, suggesting that the active sites responsible for PMS activation and NB adsorption were independent. Furthermore, it was observed that PMS decomposition (Figure S13) was also hardly affected by preadsorption time. Since the decomposition of PMS by Fe clusters in pure water was certain, the involvement of NB did not alter this process, indicating that $\mathrm { F e - N _ { 3 } }$ sites were the actual adsorption sites for NB. Figure S14 shows that although premixing Fe-SACAs with PMS resulted in a waste of reactive species, the essential adsorption of NB by FeSACAs should be observed. However, when the premixing time was extended to 20 minutes, the NB adsorption on Fe-SACAs was even inconspicuous. This could be attributed to the oxidation of Fe- $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { 3 } }$ sites by PMS, as evidenced by the decreased intensity of the Fe-N peak in the used (oxidation) Fe-SACAs (Figure S15).

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed to gain further insights into the adsorption-dependent oxidation mechanism [49]. As shown in Fig. 3g, the adsorption energies of PMS, BPA, and NB on $\mathrm { F e - N _ { 3 } }$ sites were calculated to be $- 0 . 5 3$ , $^ { - 1 . 1 0 }$ , and $- 2 . 7 1 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, suggesting that atomic Fe sites are most prone to bond with NB. Based on these results, it can be concluded that during NB oxidation, Fe clusters preferentially adsorb and activate PMS, while NB, bonded to Fe- $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { 3 } }$ sites, is degraded by reactive species. For BPA oxidation, PMS is forced to adsorb on Fe- $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { 3 } }$ sites due to the competitive occupation of Fe clusters by BPA. Significantly, the electron transfer directions of PMS and BPA on Fe sites are opposite. Therefore, when PMS and BPA are adsorbed on adjacent $\mathrm { F e - N _ { 3 } }$ sites and Fe clusters, respectively, the electron transfer process is greatly enhanced.

Based on these findings, the mechanism of targeted contaminant oriented adsorption-dependent oxidation is proposed as described in Fig. 4a. Due to the competitive occupation of Fe clusters by low IP pollutants, PMS is preferentially adsorbed on adjacent $\mathrm { F e - N _ { 3 } }$ sites. Subsequently, electrons from pollutants are transferred to the PMS molecule through $\pi$ -electron recombination on carbon substrates, leading to the oxidation of pollutants. However, for pollutants that are resistant to the electron transfer process, electron-deficient structures make them trend to coordinate with $\mathrm { F e } { \cdot } \mathrm { N } _ { 3 }$ unsaturated sites. Meanwhile, exposed Fe clusters can rapidly activate PMS through the HaberWeiss reaction to generate radicals for the oxidation of pollutants. However, it is not scientifically accurate for the catalytic system to directly remove pollutants in the aqueous phase through any oxidation mechanism, as clear for heterogeneous catalysis and process in involving short-lived reactive species. We believe that pollutants are first adsorbed onto the corresponding active sites, then oxidized and detached from these sites through either radical or nonradical pathways. The idle adsorption sites can then re-enrich pollutants in the aqueous phase, thus facilitating the removal of pollutants.

The performance of Fe-SACAs/PMS system in different actual water body further verified the different mechanisms. As shown in Figure S16, low IP pollutants were degraded through electron transfer process, while this nonradical process was usually less affected by the water matrix. For high IP pollutants, natural organic compounds and inorganic anions in actual water might quench reactive species and inhibit the performance of Fe-SACAs/PMS system.

# 4. Conclusion

In this study, an adsorption-dependent oxidation mechanism, which may be overlooked in single atom catalyst mediated Fenton-like reactions, was revealed. Notably, this process not only depends on the active sites of the single atom catalyst but is also closely related to the molecular properties of the target pollutants. Briefly, pollutants with a low IP value preempt Fe clusters, causing PMS to adsorb onto atomic Fe sites, thereby facilitating the polymerization deposition of pollutants via the electron transfer process. Conversely, pollutants with a high IP value tend to bond with atomic Fe sites, while exposed Fe cluster sites enhance PMS activation via the Haber-Weiss reaction, with the generated radicals oxidizing the pollutants. These findings urge a re-examination of the mechanism of single atom catalysis, which has focused mainly on coordination structure previously. Importantly, optimizing the treatment process based on this specific mechanism theory is expected to achieve zero oxidant residue and pollutant emission simultaneously, thereby avoiding the chemical waste associated with conventional AOPs. It should be noted that the mechanism proposed in this work may be universal in single atom catalysis, as according to the energy minimum principle, even pure single atom catalysts will inevitably aggregate into metal clusters during Fenton-like reactions.

It must be rigorously pointed out that the adsorption-dependent oxidation process may be related to the type of metal, the size of the metal clusters, the single atom coordination numbers, and even the physical properties of the carbon substrate. All these possibilities deserve further consideration and investigation.

# Environmental implication

In this study, iron cluster decorated single atom iron catalyst (FeSACAs) were synthesized to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) for the removal of organic pollutants, and a differential PMS activation mechanism induced by selective adsorption of pollutants was observed for the first time. More importantly, an optimized treatment process was proposed based on the adsorption-dependent oxidation mechanism, which achieved zero oxidant residue and pollutant emission simultaneously. This study highlights the critical role of adsorption in Fenton-like reactions and offer valuable insights for the development of more sustainable catalytic systems for environmental remediation.
