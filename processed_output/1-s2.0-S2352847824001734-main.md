# a b s t r a c t

Achieving efficient photocatalytic activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) degradation of pollutants through the regulation strategy of surface microstructure in catalysts remains a challenge. Herein, $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } 0 _ { 4 }$ nanorods (CBO NRs) and $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } 0 _ { 4 }$ microspheres (CBO Ms) were synthesized by simply regulating the alkalinity of the reaction solvent. Under fullespectrum irradiation, CBO Ms exhibited remarkable photocatalytic performance, removing $9 2 . 4 8 \%$ of tetracycline (TC) within $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ , with the reaction rate constant reaching $0 . 2 1 3 5 \mathrm { \ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , which is approximately 2.7 times that of CBO NRs $( 0 . 0 7 9 8 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ). The exposure of oxygen vacancies on the surface of CBO Ms significantly promoted the generation and migration of photogenerated carriers internally, accelerated charge accumulation at the Cu active sites on the surface, and thereby enhanced the adsorption of CBO Ms on PMS. The charge density difference results confirmed the rapid transference of surfaceeenriched electrons to the PMS, facilitating further activation of PMS. Radical quenching experiment and EPR testing verified that both radical $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , OH) and noneradical $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ pathways were involved in the reaction system. This study offers novel insights into the design of catalysts for the photocatalytic activation of PMS to efficiently degrade environmental pollutants.

# 1. Introduction

Antibiotics are extensively utilized as medications, additives in livestock and aquaculture, and for treating infectious illnesses in humans [1]. Surveys have identified the livestock and poultry farming industry, antibiotic production enterprises, and urban sewage treatment systems as the primary sources of antibiotic release into the aquatic environment [2e4]. Tetracycline (TC) rank among the most extensively employed antibiotics globally. Due to their poor absorption by organisms, resistance to environmental degradation, and challenges in complete removal by conventional sewage treatment methods, they pose a serious threat to ecosystems and human health. Consequently, there is an urgent need to explore degradation and removal techniques for TC antibiotics

# [5e7].

Advanced oxidation technologies can improve reaction efficiency and effective mineral pollutants by producing strong oxidizing active free radicals such as OH, $0 \mathbf { \dot { 2 } } ^ { - }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , etc., and have been widely reported for tetracycline degradation [8e11]. Photocatalytic oxidation of sunlight is a relatively green way to drive photocatalytic materials to degrade pollutants by redox reaction. However, it has been found that the photocatalytic oxidation technology has some problems such as low reaction efficiency, easy recombination of photogenerated carriers and slow kinetics [12e14]. In contrast, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ has a greater oxidation potential, a longer lifespan, and a broader pH adaptability than OH, which explains why the reaction efficiency of sulfateebased advanced oxidation processes is comparatively high. However, both homogeneous and heterogeneous activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) by transition metals or transition metal complexes is subject to metal ion leaching, which is prone to secondary contamination [15e17]. Therefore, it is considered that the photogenerated electrons generated in the photocatalyst can directly activate PMS to degrade organic pollutants, which can greatly reduce secondary pollution.

Reasonable design of photocatalytic materials to achieve expanded light utilization and efficient mineralization of organic pollutants is the primary task.

At present, a large number of semiconductors photocatalysts such as mineralebased, Biebased and Feebased materials have been developed for photoedriven PMS activation and degradation of organic pollutants [18e20]. In order to further enhance the light absorption of photocatalyst and promote the migration of photogenerated carriers, a variety of microestructure regulation strategies have been adopted, such as doping engineering [21], defect control [22], single atom photocatalyst and heterostructure construction [23e25]. The morphological control of the catalyst can affect the interfacial effect between the reactant and the catalyst through the surface area, pore structure, crystal surface exposure and other factors to change the reaction rate [26,27]. Four $\mathrm { { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { { O } _ { 4 } } }$ samples with urchin-like, rod-like, capsule-like, and lamella-like morphologies were successfully synthesized by Zhang et al. [28]. The structure-activity relationship between the microstructure of $\mathrm { { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { { O } _ { 4 } } }$ and the degradation of ofloxacin was revealed. Our team previously reported a magnetic recoverable greigite $\left( \mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { S } _ { 4 } \right)$ with an exposed {011} surface (FSe011) [29], it was confirmed that the values of metronidazole and chromium (VI), which activated oxalic acid to remove pollutants under visible light irradiation of FSe011, were 2.02 and 1.88 times that of $\mathsf { F e } _ { 3 } \mathsf { S } _ { 4 }$ exposed {112} surface, respectively. The more significant adsorption and activation effects of oxalic acid on the surface of FSe011 were the main source of enhancing its catalytic performance. In addition, the rapid electron transfer between the active site and the PMS can further inhibit the photogenerated carrier recombination. Cuebased photocatalysts have received much attention due to the fast cycle of $\mathsf { C u } ^ { 2 + } / \mathsf { \bar { C } } \mathsf { u } ^ { + }$ which is considered to be the key to the activation of PMS [30e32]. Therefore, it is of great significance to realize the efficient PMS activation of Cu-based catalytic materials to degrade organic pollutants through the synergistic effect of macroscopic morphology and microscopic active sites.

Herein, $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ microspheres (CBO Ms) were prepared by oneestep hydrothermal method under simple solvent pH environment control, the degradation rate of TC can reach $9 2 . 4 8 \%$ within $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ in the presence of PMS, and the apparent rate constant is $0 . 2 1 3 5 \mathrm { \ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , which is approximately 2.7 times that of CBO NRs $\cdot 0 . 0 7 9 8 ~ \mathrm { \ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ). The effect of pH on the morphological, chemical, and optical properties of $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ microspheres was investigated by multiple characterization results. The experiments results showed that CBO Ms had excellent performance for activating PMS mineralized TC molecules, and CBO Ms had good cyclic catalytic performance and structural stability after 5 cycles of degradation experiments. The results of density functional theory (DFT) calculation confirmed that the oxygen vacancy induced electron enrichment in the center of $\mathrm { c u - O }$ tetrahedron. The oxygen vacancy not only enhanced charge separation, but also promoted the joint adsorption of Cu and Bi sites on the surface of PMS, which provided favorable conditions for further activation of PMS to produce free radicals. Finally, the intermediate of TC degradation process was tested by LCeMS, and the possible degradation pathway was proposed. These results have certain guiding significance for the development and application of the catalyst in the actual wastewater treatment.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Synthesis of photocatalysts

$\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ was fabricated by a hydrothermal method. Firstly, $0 . 3 0 \textrm { g C u } ( \mathrm { N O _ { 3 } } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 3 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ was added after $1 . 2 1 \textrm { g B i } ( \mathrm { N O _ { 3 } } ) _ { 3 } { \cdot } 5 \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ had been dissolved in $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m l }$ deionized water and mixed for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ using a magnetic mixer. Subsequently, adjust the pH value of the mixed solution to 10 with $2 \ m o l / \mathrm { L }$ NaOH solution under constant stirring. Ultimately, the mixture was poured into a $5 0 ~ \mathrm { ~ m L ~ }$ Teflonelined autoclave and heated at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . After the autoclave naturally cooled down to room temperature, the separated sample was washed alternately with deionized water and alcohol for 6 times. The precipitation after centrifugation then was dried at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C$ for $^ { 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ in a vacuum drying oven. The blackebrown powder was eventually obtained. Samples were named as CBO NRs.

Changed the pH of the solution to 14.0 with 2 mol/L NaOH, the remaining steps were the same as the preparation method of CBO NRs. Finally, the blackegrey powder sample obtained by drying was the $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ microspheres. Samples were named as CBO Ms.

# 2.2. Characterization

The structural attributes of the synthesized samples were elucidated using a D/MaxeB Xeray diffractometer (XRD) for crystallography analyses. The investigation into the vibrational modes encompassed Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTeIR) on a Nexus (Thermo Nicolet, U.S.A.), and Raman scattering was examined through a Raman spectrometer (InVia, Renishaw, Gloucestershire, U.K.). Sample morphologies were surveyed via scanning electronic microscopy (SEM, Hitachi Se8010), while detailed imaging was accomplished with a higheresolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM, J.E.M. 2100F). Optical properties, specifically UVeVis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) of the catalysts, were measured using an ultravioletevisible spectrophotometer (UVe4100). Surface elemental composition and states on the catalysts were analyzed by Xeray photoelectron spectra (XPS) using a PHI Quantera II system spectrometer. Photoluminescence characteristics, including spectra and timeeresolved Photoluminescence Spectroscopy (PL) analyses, were determined with a fluorescence spectrometer (Fe380). Electrochemical properties were probed through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and transient photocurrent response, employing an electrochemical workstation (CHI660C). The BET method, conducted on Autosorbe1 (Quantachrome Instruments), was employed for quantification of specific surface areas. Active species identification was performed via electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrometry on a Bruker ER200eSRC spectrometer. Additionally, intermediates formed during TC degradation under visible light exposure were characterized using Liquid chromatographyemass spectrometry (LCeMS, U.S.A.).

# 2.3. Photocatalytic properties measurement

The degradation of TC in water was used to gauge the catalyst's photocatalytic activity. The following were the particular experiments: To counteract the effect of adsorption, $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g }$ catalyst was evenly dispersed in $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m l }$ TC solution $( 2 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m g / L } )$ , and the adsorptionedesorption equilibrium was reached after stirring in a dark for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . Following the completion of the dark reaction, $2 0 \mathrm { m g }$ PMS was added to the suspension, and a 300W Xe lamp was used as a visible light source for the photodegradation experiment. $4 \mathrm { m L }$ suspension was taken at a fixed interval $/ 2 \mathrm { m i n } \mathrm { . }$ , centrifuged $( 5 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { r / m i n } , \ 3 \ \mathrm { m i n } )$ , and then filtered through a $0 . 2 2 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ filter. Ultimately, the absorbance change was measured by UVeVis spectrophotometer at the maximum absorption wavelength of TC $( 3 5 6 \mathrm { n m } )$ ) to evaluate the photodegradation activity of the catalyst.

To identify the types of free radicals participating in pollutant degradation, capture experiments were performed on the active species. Substances such as Isopropanol (IPA), ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { C } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ (AO), Benzoquinone (BQ), MeOH (MA), and Furfuryl Alcohol (FFA) were introduced respectively into the solution as scavengers for OH, $\mathrm { h ^ { + } }$ ,

$0 \bar { 2 } ^ { - }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . Subsequently, the aforementioned photocatalytic experimental steps were replicated to ascertain the active components involved in the photocatalytic degradation process.

# 2.4. DFT calculations

The electron characteristics of $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ crystal structures were computationally modeled using the Vienna Abeinitio Simulation Package (VASP). Preceding the computations, an optimization of the crystal lattices was conducted to ascertain the energetically favored configurations. To deduce the density of states (DOS) and the electronic band structure pertaining to the $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ crystal lattice, the HSE06 hybrid functional was employed. The established specific parameters for the DOS and band structure determinations were noted for their high precision, with an electronic iteration convergence criterion set at $1 0 ^ { - 6 } \mathrm { e V } / \mathrm { c e l l }$ . The charge density variation within the $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ crystal lattice was estimated using the GGAePBE functional, with specified parameters involving a kepoint mesh of $2 \times 1 \times 1$ and a standard precision level [33,34].

# 3.2. Assessments of photocatalytic properties

TC was chosen as the target organic pollutant to evaluate the photocatalytic performance of $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ . First, we tested the performance of the catalyst in activating PMS for the degradation of TC under different light conditions (Fig. 3a). A blank experiment was conducted under conditions where only PMS was added without light, resulting in a TC removal rate of $2 0 . 1 1 \%$ . It is noteworthy that under full spectrum light irradiation the TC degradation efficiency of CBO NRs was only $6 2 . 8 8 \%$ in $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ , whereas the degradation rate of TC by CBO Ms may reach $9 2 . 4 8 \%$ . Specifically, even in the presence of VISeNIR light irradiation, the degradation efficiency of CBO Ms for TC still reached $7 2 . 9 0 \%$ , while CBO NRs only attained $5 3 . 1 7 \%$ . In addition, to quantify and compare the photocatalytic activity of CBO MS nanospheres and CBO NRS nanorods with different morphological profiles, the experimental degradation curves were fitted using the LangmuireHinshelwood model [47], and the corresponding kinetic constants were calculated. The photocatalytic degradation of TC on both catalysts adheres to a pseudoefirsteorder reaction kinetic equation: ln $( C / C _ { 0 } ) = - k t$ . The firsteorder apparent rate constant $( k )$ of each photocatalyst is shown in Fig. 3b. Under the conditions of full spectrum irradiation and visible light irradiation, the $k$ value of CBO Ms reached 0.2135 $\operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . { \dot { 1 } } 0 0 7 \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , which are 2.7 times and 1.6 times greater than those for CBO NRs $( 0 . 0 7 9 8 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and $0 . 0 6 2 7 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ). Additionally, various experimental conditions were tested to evaluate the photodegradation behavior of TC on $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ . As depicted in Fig. 3c and d, the efficacy of TC selfedegradation under full spectrum light radiation is negligible, with only $1 6 . 4 4 \%$ efficiency observed. This could be attributed to the photoesensitivity of TC, which leads to hydrolysis. However, in systems without light or PMS, there was almost no TC degradation, which demonstrated the excellent photodegradation performance of Cat./light/PMS systems.

The degradation performance of the system is affected by the pH of the initial solution. The solution's pH value is an important factor in the degradation process as it can alter the form of tetracycline, the surface charge of the catalyst, and the formation and stability of the active intermediates that are responsible for decomposition [48]. The pH value of medical wastewater typically ranges between 5.5 and 8.5 [49]. According to Fig. 3e, the degradation efficiency of the wastewater remains above $80 \%$ within this range, despite a decrease in efficiency. However, when the pH level is extremely acidic or basic, the efficiency of degradation significantly decreases. This could be attributed to the fact that in highly acidic environments, $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ can clear the active sites on the catalyst surface, leading to a scavenging effect on OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ . In a highly alkaline environment, if the reaction time is prolonged, the $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ generated by the selfedecomposition of PMS may react with OH and $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ , leading to an adverse impact on the degradation of TC [50e52]. The zeta potential of CBO Ms samples was measured to determine their surface charge properties. Fig. S5 shows that the $\mathsf { p H } _ { \mathsf { z p c } }$ value of CBO Ms is close to 6. Acidic environments result in electrostatic repulsion between the catalyst and the target degradation molecules, leading to poor removal efficiency of TC. In neutral solution, the positive charges on the surface of CBO Ms can effectively interact with ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ generated by PMS, resulting in a strong interaction with TC molecules. However, under strong alkaline conditions, this electrostatic interaction is inhibited. Since surface water and medical wastewater usually contain background inorganic anions, such as chloride, nitrate, etc., many studies have evaluated the effect of the presence of inorganic anions on the degradation of organic pollutants [53,54]. For this experiment, the pH value of the TC solution was not adjusted, and NaCl, $\mathrm { N a } _ { 3 } \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 }$ , ${ \tt N a } _ { 2 } { \tt S O } _ { 4 }$ , and ${ \mathsf { N a N O } } _ { 3 }$ were added to it respectively as the corresponding inorganic anion sources to evaluate the influence of inorganic anions on the photocatalytic performance. As shown in Fig. 3f, four common anions all have a certain degree of influence on TC degradation efficiency, and the inhibition degree is $5 0 _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } > \mathsf { C l } ^ { - } > \mathsf { P O } _ { 4 } ^ { 3 - } > \mathsf { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ . The possible reasons for this inhibition are as follows: (1) The inorganic anion and the catalyst for competitive adsorption on the surface of the target pollutant, which makes the active site on the surface of the photocatalyst occupied and reduces the efficiency of the catalyst to degrade the pollutant. (2) Due to the low potential, these inorganic anions can react with OH, $\mathrm { h ^ { + } }$ and other free radicals as a free radical scavenger to generate inorganic free radicals with low activity, thus reducing the reaction efficiency [55]. However, in general, inorganic anions have little influence on the degradation effect of TC, and the degradation efficiency of TC can still reach about $7 5 \%$ .

# 3.3. Mechanism exploration

# 3.3.1. Identification of active species

The main active substances produced by the reaction were determined by quenching experiment. As shown in Fig. $_ { 3 \mathbf { g } }$ and Fig. S6, IPA, AO, $\mathsf { B Q } ,$ MA and FFA are respectively used as scavengers of OH, $\mathrm { h ^ { + } }$ , $0 \bar { 2 } ^ { - }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , among which, after adding IPA, the degradation efficiency of TC decreased significantly, only $2 6 . 1 \%$ indicating that OH played a major role in the photocatalytic degradation process. After the addition of MA and FFA, the degradation efficiency decreased to $3 4 . 0 \%$ and $4 4 . 9 \%$ , which proved that $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , and ${ } ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 }$ were secondary active substances. However, after adding BQ and AO, the degradation efficiency can still reach $8 1 . 3 \%$ and $7 8 . 9 \%$ , that is, the photocatalytic activity is not significantly inhibited, indicating that large amounts of $0 \mathbf { \dot { 2 } } ^ { - }$ and $\mathrm { h ^ { + } }$ are not induced during the catalytic process. In a solution with fixed TC concentration, we added different amounts of BQ (0.0, 0.1, 0.2, $0 . 3 \mathrm { m m o l / L } )$ to detect absorbance at $3 5 6 \mathrm { n m }$ wavelength to evaluate its effect on TC concentration detection. As shown in Fig. S7, compared with the control group, there was no significant change in the absorbance of TC solution under different BQ addition amounts. Therefore, it can be considered that the effect of BQ on the detection of TC concentration in the range of $0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { { \ m m o l / L } }$ to $0 . 3 \mathrm { \ m m o l / L }$ is negligible. In order to further confirm the active species in the process of TC degradation by CBO Ms activated PMS, EPR tests were conducted using DMPO and TEMP as trapping agents. Fig. 3h shows the obvious peak type of OH radical, and the characteristic peak belonging to $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ is accompanied by the 1:2:2:1 peak, which confirms the role of OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ in the reaction system, and the intensity of the signal peak increases with the extension of the illumination time, reflecting the accumulation of the concentration of these two free radicals. In the EPR spectrum test results of DMPOe $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , a strong signal peak of 1:1:1 belonging to ${ { 0 } _ { 2 } }$ was observed, and the changing trend of peak intensity reflected that $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ always maintained a high concentration in the reaction system (Fig. 3i). Through capture experiment and EPR test results, it was confirmed that OH, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and ${ \mathrm { { ^ { 1 } } O } } _ { 2 }$ were involved in the reaction system of CBO Ms.

# 3.3.2. Photoelectric property

The experimental results confirmed that light can significantly enhance the catalytic activation of PMS to degrade pollutants, so the photoelectric properties of the catalyst have a positive role in exploring the reaction mechanism. Ultravioletevisible Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy (DRS) is very useful for studying the optical properties and band structure of materials. The results of Fig. 4a show that the catalyst has strong absorption in both the ultraviolet and visible regions, and the absorption capacity of CBO Ms is less improved than that of CBO NRs. The insertion diagram of Fig. 4a is obtained by using the Tauc curve, and the results show that the band gap values $( E _ { \mathrm { g } } )$ of CBO NRs and CBO Ms are $2 . 0 \mathrm { e V }$ and $1 . 9 \mathrm { e V } $ , respectively. In order to further obtain the band structure of CBO NRs and CBO Ms, the flateband potential $\left( E _ { \mathrm { f b } } \right)$ value was obtained through MotteSchottky test. Fig. 4b and c shows that the flateband potential of CBO NRs and CBO Ms is $0 . 7 9 \mathrm { V }$ and $0 . 7 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ verse $\mathsf { A g } / \mathsf { A g C l }$ , respectively. Using the form: $E _ { \mathrm { N H E } } = E _ { \mathrm { A g / A g C l } } - E _ { \theta } +$ $0 . 0 5 9 ~ \mathrm { p H }$ , where $E _ { \theta } = 0 . 1 9 7 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ $( \mathsf { A g } / \mathsf { A g C l }$ , $\mathrm { p H } = 7$ ), the flateband potential is converted to $0 . 5 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $0 . 5 5 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ verse NHE. As the petype semiconductor has the property that its $E _ { \mathrm { f b } }$ is more negative (0.1 eV) compared with valence band $E _ { \mathrm { v b } }$ , so the $E _ { \mathrm { v b } }$ could be calculated as $0 . 6 9 \ \mathrm { e V }$ and $0 . 6 5 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , the corresponding conduction band $( E _ { \mathrm { c b } } )$ potential of CBO NRs and CBO Ms is calculated as $1 . 3 1 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and $1 . 2 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ . The contraction of the band structure may be caused by the presence of oxygen vacancy changing the interatomic interaction potential inside the crystal, which affects the local electronic structure and thus affects the band structure of the whole semiconductor.

In order to further determine the generation and migration of charge on the catalyst surface, the corresponding results were obtained by steadyestate PL spectrum, photocurrent test and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. The signal intensity of PL spectrum is positively correlated with the recombination rate of electronehole pairs. Fig. 4d shows that the catalyst has an obvious signal peak at $4 5 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ and the peak intensity of CBO Ms is significantly lower than that of CBO NRs, indicating that carriers have a lower recombination rate on the surface of CBO Ms, which is conducive to improving its catalytic performance. When a semiconductor material is illuminated, the successful separation of electrons and holes can generate a photocurrent inside the material. Therefore, the intensity of the photocurrent directly reflects the effective carrier separation and migration efficiency. Fig. 4e shows that the photocurrent density of CBO Ms is significantly higher than that of CBO NRs during each light cycle, further confirming the excellent carrier separation and transfer efficiency within CBO Ms. The semicircle in the Nyquist curve usually represents the charge transfer impedance. The smaller the diameter of the semicircle, the lower the resistance to charge transfer, which may imply a high carrier separation efficiency (Fig. 4f). This is because efficient carrier separation leads to faster charge transfer, reducing recombination at the interface. Obviously, the electrical impedance of CBO Ms is lower than that of CBO NRs, which provides favorable conditions for carrier separation and migration. The above results confirm the high efficiency of electronehole pair separation and mobility in CBO Ms, which is consistent with the test results of catalytic performance.

# 3.3.3. DFT calculation

The electronic properties of the catalyst and the trend of interfacial electron transfer during the activation of PMS were calculated by DFT. The original cell of $\mathrm { C u B i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ was optimized (Fig. S8). According to TEM results, CBO NRs and CBO Ms with exposed (1 1 0) and (011) crystal faces were constructed, respectively. Cause the work function refers to the minimum energy required to release negatively charged electrons from the surface of a solid material, and its level directly affects whether electrons can escape from the surface of the material. We found that the work function of CBO Ms $( 4 . 3 3 \mathrm { e V } )$ is lower than that of CBO NRs (4.81 eV), indicating that the electron migration on the surface of CBO Ms is easier (Fig. 5a and b), which is consistent with the results of the photoelectric properties of the catalyst. By calculating the charge distribution density on different exposed crystal surfaces, it is found that more charge is enriched at Cu site on (01 1) CBO Ms surface due to the presence of oxygen vacancy, while the charge density on $( 1 \ \overline { { 1 } } \ 0 )$ NRs surface is lower (Fig. 5c and d). Further, we constructed the adsorption of PMS molecules at Cu sites on the catalyst surface. For CBO NRs, the adsorption energy of PMS on its surface is $- 2 . 6 \mathrm { e V }$ (Fig. 5e). Interestingly, after structural optimization, due to the presence of surface oxygen vacancies on the CBO Ms surface, the oxygeneligated Cu site and Bi site have simultaneous effects on O on the PMS surface, and the adsorption energy $E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } = - 3 . 9$ eV (Fig. 5f), confirming the strong interaction between the surface of CBO Ms and PMS (01 1). Further, by calculating the surface differential charges of adsorbed PMS and (01 1) CBO Ms, it is found that there is a strong charge transfer between the adsorption interface (blue represents charge consumption, yellow represents charge accumulation), which confirms that (01 1) CBO Ms can effectively activate PMS (Fig. 5f). The above DFT results show that the electron transfer on the surface of CBO Ms and the adsorption and activation of PMS are more favorable, which is consistent with the results of catalytic performance and characterization compared with CBO NRs.

Intermediates and possible degradation pathways of TC The intermediate product in TC degradation process was detected by liquid chromatographyemass spectrometry and the possible degradation mechanism of tetracycline was analyzed. Fig. 6 summarizes the intermediate products of the TC target solution before and after the reaction, and the mineralization process is mainly divided into three paths. In Pathway I, The TC molecule produces T1 $( m / z = 4 1 5 )$ ) by breaking two methyl groups on the N atom, and T2 $( m / z = 3 8 7$ ) by removing the two hydroxyl groups in a dehydration reaction. Further through ringeopening and deamination, the product T3eT5 $( m / z = 2 2 8$ , 274 and 270) is obtained, which is mineralized into small molecules with $m / z$ approaching 100 under the action of strong oxidizing free radicals and finally converted into $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ and ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ . For pathway II, the product T6 $( m / z$ 433) may be derived from the demethylation of tetracycline molecules, and the two methyl groups break to form a resonanceestable carbon center radical. Due to the low bond energy of $\Nu { - } C ,$ , the product T7 $( m / z = 4 0 4 )$ can be formed by further oxidation of two amino groups by T6 through free radical action. After deamidation and oxidation ringeopening reaction, intermediate products such as T8eT10 $( m / z = 2 7 1$ , 192 and 194) are obtained. After further ringeopening reaction, small molecule products are obtained. Pathway III provides the reaction path for TC molecule to generate carbonyl group on benzene (T16, $m / z = 3 5 1$ ) ring molecule through acylation reaction after deamination reaction, and the products after acylation reaction form small molecule compounds (T17, $m / z = 3 3 2 { \mathrm { . } }$ ) through ring opening and further cracking to finally realize TC mineralization [56]. In order to evaluate the degree of mineralization of TC molecules after the reaction, TOC test was conducted by collecting the solution before and after the reaction. The TOC test results showed that CBO NRs realized about $1 9 . 5 \%$ TC mineralization, while the TOC removal rate of CBO Ms reached $4 1 . 8 \%$ , which was able to mineralization TC molecules more effectively (Fig. S9).

# 3.4. Reusability analysis

The stability of photocatalyst is of great significance to the popularization of photocatalysis technology. Fig. S10 shows that after five cycles, the removal rate of TC in the catalytic system remains at $8 1 . 0 \%$ , which confirms the excellent cyclic catalytic performance of the catalyst. XRD (Fig. S11) and SEM images (Fig. S12) were further used to characterize the phase structure and surface morphology of the catalyst before and after the reaction. The XRD signal peak of the catalyst did not change significantly, and no impurities were generated. Meanwhile, SEM images also showed that CBO Ms always maintained a flower spherical structure assembled by nanorods. The large specific surface area provides favorable conditions for the reaction. The results above confirm that the catalyst has good cyclic stability and provide a possibility for further application.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, we successfully prepared (1 1 0) CBO NRs and (011) CBO Ms by adjusting the growth environment of the material through changing pH. The specific surface area of CBO Ms is significantly increased, providing more favorable conditions for the adsorption of catalyst surface and PMS molecules. XPS results show that CBO Ms has a higher concentration of surface oxygen vacancies, which not only changes the electron cloud density around Cu atoms, but also promotes the charge separation efficiency and helps the adsorption and activation of PMS molecules. Using the removal efficiency of TC to evaluate the performance of CBO activated PMS to degrade pollutants, it was found that CBO Ms could remove $9 2 . 4 8 \%$ of TC within $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ of full spectrum illumination, while CBO NRs only achieved a removal rate of $6 2 . 8 8 \%$ . The catalytic performance of CBO Ms is still better than that of CBO NRs under the condition of full spectrum irradiation even under the condition of controllable light. Through characterization and analysis of DFT calculation results, it is found that compared with CBO NRs, CBO Ms has an explosively enhanced photogenerated charge separation and transfer efficiency. The formation of oxygen vacancy and the difference of surface charge density promote CBO Ms to achieve efficient adsorption and activation of PMS using photogenerated carriers perfectly, creating favorable conditions for further mineralization of TC molecules. Finally, the intermediate products of the reaction were detected by LCeMS, and three possible pathways for TC molecular degradation were proposed. This work provides effective theoretical guidance and application ideas for the application of Cuebased catalyst in water restoration.
