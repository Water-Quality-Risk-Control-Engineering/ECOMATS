# Abstract

A natural, ferruginous soil (HRS) was used as a catalyst for decontamination and disinfection via PMS activation. The HRS was fully characterized (morphological, structural, chemical properties) and was mainly comprised of $a / - F e _ { 2 } 0 _ { 3 }$ . Complete degradation and $8 8 . 6 \%$ mineralization of Diclofenac (DCF) was achieved in the HRS/PMS process (10 mg/L HRS, 75 mg/L PMS) within 8 min at neutral pH, while sulfate and chloride did not inhibit the process. E. coli and Enterococcus sp. were rapidly inactivated in shorter timeframe than DCF. The rapid purification was attributed to $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , less to $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and almost insignificant HO• participation; the degradation mechanisms and transformation pathways were fully elucidated by scavenging tests/EPR, and LC/MS, respectively. With a view in potential applications, HRS was supported on alginate beads (HRSB), maintaining high activity and high reusability (x5 reuse). Finally, a continuousflow, packed-bed process with HRSB/PMS was assessed, where DCF degradation and mineralization was reached within 2 min HRT.

Keywords: Natural mineral; iron oxide; PMS activation; singlet oxygen; emerging contaminants.

# 1. Introduction

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are efficient processes for the degradation of potentially toxic and biologically recalcitrant organic contaminants. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as hydroxyl radical (HO●), superoxide radical $( \mathsf { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet \bullet } )$ , hydroperoxyl radical $( \mathsf { H O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet } )$ and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ which can be generated during AOPs treatment are capable of preferentially or non-selectively oxidize and mineralize various organic contaminants (Ma et al., 2019). Besides the aforementioned classic AOPs, sulfate radical AOPs have also gained considerable merit due to their effectiveness. The use of organic peroxides including peroxydisulfate (PDS) and peroxymonosulfate (PMS) as oxidants can induce the generation of SO4 •‒ . PMS and PDS have intrinsically low reaction rates, but can be activated by various methods including microwave, ultrasound, ultraviolet, heat, alkalis, transition metals and their oxides, electron conduction and carbon-based catalysts (Matzek and Carter, 2016; Wang and Wang, 2018).

An efficient application variation is the activation of PMS/PDS by heterogeneous catalysts, due to the ease in their recovery before discharge or reuse. For this purpose, different noble and non-noble metal oxides such as iron oxides, ${ \sf A l } _ { 2 } { \sf O } _ { 3 } .$ , ${ \mathrm { T i O } } _ { 2 }$ , CuO, ZnO, ${ \mathsf { C e O } } _ { 2 }$ , etc. have been synthesized and investigated for the heterogeneous activation of PMS/PDS for the degradation of environmental contaminants (Yan et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2020; Li et al., 2019). Heterogeneous activation of PMS by transition metal(oxides), compared to the homogeneous analogues, are promising alternatives due to the advantageous properties of the oxides, such as low concentration of metal leaching, effective recycling, and stability of their structure during reaction (Zhou et al., 2020). Several reactive species might be generated through PMS activation with heterogeneous metal(oxides) catalysts, including sulfate radical $( \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ , hydroxyl radicals (HO• ), and other reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide $( 0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ . Considerable attention has been recently paid on the degradation of organic contaminants in the AOPs using non-radical reactive oxygen species (ROSs) such as $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , although the precise reaction mechanism is evidently complicated and has not established yet (Liu et al., 2018). $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ is a non-radical ROS with electrophilic nature, which has been largely applied for the oxidation of organic pollutants (Cheng et al., 2017). The non-radical mechanisms may display substrate-specific reactivity but seems uninvolved to undesired reactions with background compounds such as natural organic matters (Zhang et al., 2014). Hence the interest lies in using this pathway to treat wastewaters that may present an inhibition of radical pathways. However, although factors such as oxygen vacancies lead to non-radical pathways, the composition of materials may affect the resulting pathways of contaminant elimination. For instance, Deng et al. (2018) investigated the degradation of BPA using activation of PMS by MnFe2O4 and suggested that the main mechanism is a $\mathrm { S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } / H O ^ { \bullet } }$ radical pathway, however Fu et al. (2019)

suggested the induction of further non-radical pathways, due to the porous biochar in the composite. As such, the different PMS activation efficiencies and subsequent degradation driving force obtained by different metal components clearly indicates that the catalytic performance and contaminants’ degradation mechanism depend on the source and nature of the catalyst. Also, the need to combine an effective PMS activation, high degradation potential, effectiveness in complex water matrices (natural or wastewater) and easy recovery of a catalyst, are among the key aspects to a successful process. Although some of the materials in Table 1 had notable catalytic activity, due to the complexity, energyand cost-intensiveness of the synthesizing process, as well as the secondary chemical contamination, the preparation and the application of synthesized materials and nanoparticles raises environmental concerns (Zhang et al., 2020). Using natural materials is an interesting, economically competitive and environmentally benign alternative catalyst choice for the activation of PMS/PDS (Guan et al., 2014). For instance, hematite $( { \mathsf { a } } { - } { \mathsf { F } } { \mathsf { e } } _ { 2 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 3 } )$ is a natural and environmentally friendly catalyst with proven photocatalytic activity (Demarchis et al., 2015), which is widely distributed and abundant in nature, readily extractable at very low cost. Additionally, it has been shown that doping the iron oxides with the transition metals of Ti, Cr, Mn and Co increased their catalytic activity (Liang et al., 2010); the environmental samples of iron oxides often naturally contain similar “impurities” that lead to enhanced efficacy, albeit in an un-controlled manner.

Natural iron (hydr)oxides and minerals, like goethite FeO(OH), maghemite $\left( \mathsf { V } ^ { - } \mathsf { F } \mathsf { e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 } \right)$ , ilmenite (FeTiO3), pyrite (FeS2) and mackinawite (FeS) are abundant in the Earth’s crust. Some of these iron oxides have already been tested as natural catalysts for efficient activation of PDS or PMS for organic contaminants degradation and bacterial inactivation (Xia et al., 2018; Su et al., 2019; Chenxu et al., 2018). For instance, Zhang et al. (2020) investigated the role of a natural bornite (Cu5FeS4) as an efficient catalyst for PDS activation for the degradation of tetracycline via $\mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ , $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ . Peng et al. (2020) used a natural chalcopyrite to degrade Bisphenol S, a process that was effectuated via $\mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ and $0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , while Lai and Lai (2020) degraded atrazine via PMS activation by natural molybdenite to generate HO• and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ . Natural materials have also been used in more complex applications such as ultrasound-mediated processes of PMS activation or bio-electro-activation (Diao et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2018).

Hence, if the target application is the removal of organic compounds and microorganisms in water, the best option would be i) to use PMS, as a more effective reactive species generator than PDS, ii) apply it in heterogeneous mode in order to avoid metallic sludge and difficulties in catalyst recovery, and iii) intend to use a natural material to minimize the costs associated by synthesizing a material mimicking the natural oxides.

In order to meet this multi-level challenge, we considered the use of a natural iron source, Hormuz Red Soil (HRS) as a cheap, available, and environmentally friendly catalyst, used for the first time for the activation of PMS for the degradation of emerging water contaminants and microorganisms. Diclofenac (DCF) was used as a model organic contaminant, while E. coli and Enterococcus sp. as the bacterial contamination indicators. A full characterization of the morphological, textural and electrochemical properties of HRS was determined, followed by evaluation of the determination of its catalytic activity under different experimental variables, namely the solution pH, catalyst and PMS concentrations, contact time, and water anions in different experimental runs. The reusability and stability of the catalyst was also tested. The type of oxidative species involved in the degradation of DCF in the developed catalytic process was investigated and the degradation mechanism was proposed using the various scavengers. With a view to a potential large-scale application, alginate beads were prepared supporting

133 the HRS powder and the catalytic performance of the system in activating PMS for the degradation of   
134 DCF was assessed in batch and in a continuous (flow through) mode with a packed bed column reactor.

# 2.1. Chemicals and reagents

Hormuz Red Soil (HRS) was a naturally fine powder, with an abundant source available, extracted from the Hormuz Island (Iran) and used as received without any further purification or processing. The HRS powder was stored in a dark glass and kept in ambient temperature. DCF was obtained from a local pharmaceutical manufacturer as diclofenac sodium powder. OxoneTM $2 \mathsf { K H S O } _ { 5 } { \cdot } \mathsf { K H S O } _ { 4 } { \cdot } \mathsf { K } _ { 2 } \mathsf { S O } _ { 4 } )$ ) was used as the PMS source. The amount of PMS added to the reaction solutions was calculated based on $2 H S O _ { 5 } + H S O _ { 4 }$ . All the other chemicals used in the present study were of analytical grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Inc. The solvents used for DCF chromatographic analysis were HPLC-grade. All solutions were prepared with deionized water.

# 2.2. Preparation of the catalyst beads

The catalyst beads were prepared by using sodium alginate following a modified preparation method summarized as follows: 100 g alginic acid sodium salt was dissolved in $1 0 0 ~ \mathsf { m L }$ of distilled water and stirred for 30 min at 150 rpm. A 0.2-M CaCl2 solution was prepared by dissolving a known amount of ${ \mathsf { C a C l } } _ { 2 } . 2 { \mathsf { H } } _ { 2 } { \mathsf { O } }$ in distilled a water and used as the beads’ hardener solution. A $2 0 \cdot \mathsf { m l }$ suspension of $0 . 2 { \ g } / { \mathsf { L } }$ HRS was also prepared and sonicated for 30 min at ${ 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C } .$ The HRS suspension was then added to the alginate solution and the mixture was stirred for $^ { 6 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The mixture was finally drop-wise added to the hardener solution using a 5-mL plastic syringe and prepare the spherical beads containing the catalyst. The formed beads were kept in the hardener solution for $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ for proper aging and completing $\mathsf { N a } ^ { + } / \mathsf { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ ion exchange reaction (Wang and Wang, 2018). The beads were then recovered from the solution, washed three times with distilled water and stored in a capped container until used for the treatment experiments. The same procedure was undertaken to prepare the bare beads, during which no catalyst was added to the alginate solution. Supplementary Fig. S1 demonstrates the differences between the prepared beads, i.e. with or without the catalyst. A characterization of the bead morphology showed a spherical shape with an average diameter of $4 { \pm } 0 . 3 ~ \mathsf { m m }$ .

# 2.3. Material characterization methods and apparatus

The HRS was fully characterized prior to use for its morphological, textural, elemental, and electrochemical properties. The surface morphology was determined by the field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) analysis (FESEM; MIRA3 TESCAN) and the structure of the particles was determined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL-2100). The elemental analysis of the catalysts was performed by energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) elemental mapping analysis. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Malvern Instruments, Ltd) using water as dispersant was used to measure the size of the catalyst particles. The structural composition was analyzed by X-ray fluorescence (XRF) method on a Philips-X’PERT-PW 2404 instrument. Fourier Transmission Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIRThermo Nicolet-Nexus 670) at the wavenumbers ranging from 4000 to $4 0 0 \ c m ^ { - 1 }$ was used to determine the functional groups on surface of the catalyst.

The specific surface area of the catalysts was determined based on Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) model from the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms derived at −196 $^ \circ \mathsf { C }$ using a Micromeritics/Gemini-2372 analyzer. The sample was degassed at $200 \text{‰}$ under vacuum for $^ { 2 \mathfrak { h } }$ before analysis. The crystallinity and phase structure of the powder were analyzed by recording the X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD) at 2θ between ${ 1 0 ^ { \circ } }$ and $80 ^ { \circ }$ on a Philips X'Pert-MPD X-ray diffractometer operated at $4 0 \ k \ V$ and 0.04 A with Cu Kα radiation at the wavelength of 1.54056 Å. The average crystallite size (d) of iron oxide in the sample was calculated using Debye–Scherrer equation $\begin{array} { r } { \langle d = \frac { 0 . 9 \lambda } { \beta C o s \Theta } ) } \end{array}$ in which λ, β, and Ɵ are the X-ray wavelength, width at half-maximum of the peak, and the position of the peak, respectively. Finally, the binding energies of elements and surface composition of the catalyst were analyzed by X-ray photo-electron spectroscopy (XPS) using a Nicolet spectrometer with an Al-K x-ray source of 1486.6 eV.

# 2.4. Experimental process

The catalytic experiments were conducted in two modes: i) batch experiments for the activation of PMS by HRS (in powder form) or ii) supported HRS by alginate beads in flow-through reactors. In mode I, the activation experiments were conducted using the HRS catalyst in powder form in a glass Erlenmeyer flask as reactor, using a working solution of $1 0 0 ~ \mathsf { m L }$ . In a typical reaction, $1 0 0 ~ \mathsf { m L }$ of fresh DCF solution was poured into the reactor and mixed by a magnetic stirrer (150 rpm), the required amounts of PMS and HRS were added to the reactor, indicating the beginning of the experiment. The solution was sampled at the time intervals (otherwise noted at a fixed time) and the catalyst was immediately separated from the solution by centrifuging at 5000 rpm during 5 min; the supernatant was filtered through a $0 . 2 ~ { \mu \mathsf { m } }$ cellulose acetate syringe filter, and the filtrate was transferred to labeled vials for target analyses (HPLC). Control tests (blanks) were also conducted under the same procedure in the absence of PMS, as well as of HRS in order to determine the distinct effects of HRS and PMS and the contribution of DCF removal by adsorption to HRS surface or the oxidation with PMS, respectively. Furthermore, the changes in DCF removal in the absence and presence of different scavenging compounds was assessed: tert-butanol (TBA), methanol (MeOH), and Sodium Azide were used as hydroxyl radical (HO• ), sulfate radical $( \mathrm { S 0 } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ , and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ scavengers, were used to investigate the driving force of DCF degradation. In mode II, a set of experiments was conducted using the catalyst beads under both batch and continuous column test. For the batch tests, 5 spheres of the prepared HRS beads (HRSB) were added to $5 0 ~ \mathsf { m l }$ of DCF solution $( 5 0 \mathrm { \ m g / L } )$ and the process was run in the presence of 75 mg/L PMS (HRSB/PMS process). The solution was sampled over pre-defined time intervals and the samples were analyzed for their residual DCF. The control experiment was also conducted using the bare beads under similar conditions (bare alginate beads). Continuous flow column tests were also conducted to evaluate the field applicability of the HRSB/PMS process. The schematic setup of the packed bed reactor is given in Supplementary Fig. S2. The total volume of the column was $5 \mathsf { m l }$ and HRSB packed bed volume was 4 mL (15 spheres). The fresh DCF solution $\{ 5 0 \mathrm { m g / L } \}$ ) supplemented with $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m g / L } }$ PMS was transferred to the inlet tank. The solution was pumped to the reactor at the bottom at different flow rates, resulting to distinct hydraulic retention times (HRT) ranging from 0.5 to 2 min. At each HRT, the effluent was recovered, and the samples were analyzed for their residual DCF content.

# 2.5. Microbial methods

The Escherichia coli and Enterococcus sp. used for this study are wildtype strains, natural isolates from secondary wastewater, via selection by Tergitol and Slanetz-Bartley selective Agars, respectively. The use of the specific two model isolates provides a good simulation for the actual bacterial gram-negative and gram-positive pathogens in natural waters/wastewater. Following a successive selection process in proper culture media, the isolated strains were grown overnight in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and cryogenically stored at $\mathfrak { - 8 0 ^ { \circ } C }$ in a 80:20 glycerol:water solution. The process of bacterial growth and strain preparation was published elsewhere in detail (Giannakis et al., 2013). Briefly, a bacterial colony was grown overnight in $1 0 ~ \mathsf { m L }$ of LB solution under aerobic conditions and agitation (200 rpm) at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C ,$ , in order to reach stationary phase $\mathsf { O D } _ { 6 0 0 } { = } 3 { - } 5 . 5$ ). Prior to experimentation, the grown culture was centrifuged (2 min at 10.000 rpm) and washed with a sterile saline solution ( $0 . 8 \%$ NaCl, $0 . 0 8 \%$ KCl). The $0 D _ { 6 0 0 }$ of the spiked solution was set to 0.002-0.003 that corresponds to approx. $1 0 ^ { 6 } C F \mathsf { U / m L }$ . At pre-defined time intervals, 1-mL was recovered from the experiment solution and immediately plated in 9-cm petri dishes containing Plate Count Agar (PCA, Sigma-Aldrich) for E. coli or Slanetz-Bartley Agar (SBA, Panreac) for Enterococcus sp. Enumeration was performed by applying the spread plate method; $0 . 1 ~ \mathsf { m L }$ of sample was uniformly distributed by 4-mm glass beads, and the plates were incubated for 24 or $4 8 \ \mathsf { h }$ at $3 7 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C }$ before enumeration of E. coli and Enterococcus sp., respectively. Experiments were performed at least two times (biological replicates), minimum in duplicate (statistical replicates) and plating at least two consecutive aliquots (technical replicates). The presented values represent the average of all the aforementioned tests and the vertical bars indicate standard deviation from the mean.

# 2.6. Analytical methods

The concentration of DCF was determined by a high-performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC, Agilent: 1260 infinity Co.) with a UV detector coupled with a reversed-phase C18 separation column (Eclipse plus C18 column; $3 . 5 ~ { \mu \mathsf { m } }$ , $4 . 6 ~ \mathsf { m m } \times 2 5 0 ~ \mathsf { m m } _ { \cdot }$ ). The HPLC was run under the following conditions: sample injection volume $= 3 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ , mobile phase $=$ methanol/phosphate buffer (70/30) mixture, flow rate of 1 mL/min, detection wavelength $= 2 6 4 \mathsf { n m }$ , resulting in a retention time $=$ within 15 min. The concentration of total organic carbon (TOC) was measured with a TOC analyzer (Shimadzu, TOC analyzer –VCSH model). The appropriate standard methods (APHA, 2017) were used for measuring the concentrations of nitrogen species, including ammonium (4500-NH3-F), nitrite $\left( 4 5 0 0  – \mathrm { N O _ { 2 } } ^ { - } \right) .$ -B), nitrate $( 4 5 0 0 - \mathrm { N O _ { 3 } } ^ { - } { \cdot } \mathsf { B }$ ), and organic nitrogen (4500-Norg-C). The LC/MS instrument was equipped with an Atlantis T3-C18 $3 \mu$ column $( 2 . 1 { \times } 1 0 0 ~ \mathsf { m m } )$ ). For the bacterial tests, the logarithm of the colony forming units per mL $( \mathsf { I o g C F U / m L } )$ are plotted over time. Finally, the calculation of synergy within the involved processes was calculated according to the following equation (Eq. 15):

$$
\begin{array} { r } { S = \frac { k _ { 1 2 } } { k _ { 1 } + k _ { 2 } } } \end{array}
$$

Values of synergy $\mathsf { S } { \mathsf { 2 1 } }$ indicate a synergy among any actions 1 and 2, while $\mathsf { S } { < } 1$ shows an antagonistic effect.

The removal of Diclofenac (DCF) from an aqueous solution using 5, 10 and 20 mg/L HRS in the presence of various concentrations of PMS between 0 and $7 5 ~ \mathsf { m g / L }$ was investigated and the results are shown in Fig. 4. For 5 mg/L HRS, the DCF increased from 21.9 to $9 2 . 8 \%$ when the PMS added to the suspension was increased from 15 to $7 5 ~ \mathsf { m g / L }$ . The same trend without a significantly higher increase when increasing HRS concentrations from 10 to $2 0 ~ { \mathrm { m g / L } }$ ; under these conditions the complete removal of DCF was determined at the PMS concentrations of $7 5 ~ \mathsf { m g / L }$ . Accordingly, $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ was chosen as the optimal concentration for HRS to efficiently activate PMS and achieve complete removal of DCF in the HRS/PMS process.

Indeed, a high degree of synergy $( { \mathsf { S } } { \mathsf { > } } 3 )$ , calculated from Eq. (4), was achieved by combining HRS and PMS for the removal of DCF under the selected conditions.

# 3.2.2. Effect of solution pH

The effect of solution pH on the removal of DCF in the HRS/PMS system after 10 min was evaluated between 4 and 10. Fig. 5 indicates that the DCF removal in the developed process is independent of the solution pH up to the neutral conditions $\mathrm { [ p H \leq 7 }$ ), where complete removal of DCF was achieved. This suggests that the HRS/PMS process can be efficiently applied for treating contaminated natural waters without needing to adjust the pH to acidic values. The DCF removal was, however, dropped to 84.6, 23.3 and $1 6 . 3 \%$ at the solution pH of 8, 9 and 10, respectively. A similar decrease in DCF removal with the increase in solution pH was also reported for the PMS activated with BiFeO3 (Han et al., 2020), Co3O4-g${ \mathsf { C } } _ { 3 } { \mathsf { N } } _ { 4 }$ (Shao et al., 2017) and cobalt-manganese complex (Wu et al., 2019). In comparison, the highest DCF removal was observed in the LaFeO3/PMS process at pH 7.0 and it decreased under acidic and alkaline conditions (Rao et al., 2019). Therefore, the influence of solution pH on the performance of a catalyst-activated PMS process is greatly depends on the catalyst properties in the interaction with the solution pH.

Another factor to take into account should be the pKa of DCF; with a pKa of 4.15, at ${ \mathsf { p H } } { \mathsf { > } } 4 . 1 5$ , the anionic form of DCF- will prevail. However, in terms of the degradation mechanism, PMS could firstly be activated by HRS through the adsorption of PMS onto the surface of the catalyst, followed by the charge transfer between PMS and the catalyst (Othman et al., 2020). Accordingly, the effect of solution pH on the activation of PMS by the HRS catalyst can be explained considering the pKa of PMS and the pHpzc of the catalyst (ca. 8.0) which affected the first step of the activation process. ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ is the dominant form of PMS species at the acidic to mild-alkaline pHs $\leq 9 . 4$ (Wu et al., 2019; Rao et al., 2019). On the other hand, the HRS has a pHpzc of 8.0 meaning that its surface is positively and negatively charged at the solution pH below and above of 8.0, respectively (Alahabadi and Moussavi, 2017). Therefore, at the acidic to neutral solution pH, the surface of the catalyst had a positive charge, electrostatically attracting ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ which resulted in its activation and the formation of oxidative species. However, at the alkaline pH the negatively charged catalyst repulsed the PMS anions $\left( \mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - } \right.$ and ${ \mathsf { S } } { 0 _ { 5 } } ^ { 2 - } )$ thus limited the activation process in an increasing manner, and thereby the DCF removal (Wu et al., 2019; Rao et al., 2019). In a nutshell, there are many opposing forces and areas of overlap whose effects seem to peak around 6-7 pH.

# 3.2.3. Effect of water anions on DCF degradation performance by HRS/PMS

Natural waters and wastewaters contain different anions which may affect the performance of an AOP, due to the fact that they can react with $\mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ to form radicals with lower oxidative potential and thus resulting in decreasing the degradation of the target contaminants (Luo et al., 2019). The effect of main water anions on the performance of the HRS/PMS is illustrated in Fig. 5b. We report that the DCF removal decreased from $100 \%$ in the absence of anions down to $30 \%$ in the presence of $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m g / L } }$ phosphate and $34 \%$ in the presence of $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m g / L } }$ bicarbonate, indicating the strong inhibition of the process in the presence of these anions. Phosphate ions can bind with the active sites on the surface of the catalyst thus inhibiting the interaction between PMS and the catalyst and decrease the activation efficacy (Rao et al., 2019). This fact indirectly suggests that the activation of PMS occurred on the surface of the HRS particles. Similar result was observed for DCF degradation in the LaFeO3-catalysed PMS process (Rao et al., 2019). On the other hand, the reduction in the DCF degradation in the presence of bicarbonate can be attributed to the point that $\mathrm { C O } _ { 3 } ^ { \bullet - }$ with lower oxidation potential might be generated from the reaction between bicarbonate and the reactive species formed in the process (Rommozzi et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020).

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { \mathrm { H 0 ^ { * } + H C O _ { 3 } ^ { - }  C O _ { 3 } ^ { * - } + \Delta H _ { 2 } O } } \\ & { } \\ & { \mathrm { H 0 ^ { * } + C O _ { 3 } ^ { 2 - }  C O _ { 3 } ^ { * - } + \Delta O H ^ { - } } } \end{array}
$$

Where the speciation between $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ and $\mathsf { C O } _ { 3 } ^ { 2 - }$ depends on the solution pH. Chloride and sulfate (Fig. 5b) did not considerably affected the DCF removal in the HRS/PMS process. 3.4. Simultaneous disinfection and decontamination: Escherichia coli and Enterococcus sp.

inactivation by the batch HRS/PMS process in presence/absence of DCF

In order to assess the disinfection capacity of the HRS soil as a PMS activator, E. coli and Enterococcus sp. inactivation tests were performed, as representative bacteria of the Gram-negative and Grampositive categories, and the results are summarized in Fig. 7. Since the HRS/PMS process was found to be very synergic, the different actions taking place were attempted to be dissociated, in the elimination of the new, microbial targets. The first set of experiments took place in absence of DCF, in distilled water (Fig. 7a). The addition of $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ HRS did not affect cultivability, and the adsorption of HRS to E. coli cells did not reduce the viable count. HRS at $\mathsf { p } \mathsf { H } = 6 . 5$ is positively charged and bacteria carry a negative charge. However, HRS has a mean diameter of $2 1 1 { \mathsf { n m } }$ , while bacteria are $\mu \mathsf { m }$ sized microorganisms. Hence, the HRS adsorption did not cause aggregates that affected the bacterial concentration. The addition of 75 mg/L PMS caused rapid inactivation (within $2 \mathsf { m i n }$ ), which indicated the sensitivity of this target against PMS. PMS could abstract electrons from cell wall components (e.g. carboxylic acids), oxidize them and generate $S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals, which inactivate bacteria by themselves, or be a precursor of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ generation (Eqs. 4,5). Naturally, with the addition of HRS for PMS activation bacteria were also inactivated in less than 2 min, with the induction of radicals’ generation, albeit to fast to be measured. Following, addition of DCF in the bacterial dispersion was assayed. Although the presence of DCF did not affect E. coli adsorption, it presented an antagonism for the direct oxidation by PMS; DCF competes with E. coli, hence a delay was presented, and bacteria were inactivated within 10 min. Notably, the addition of HRS led to a modest increase in E. coli inactivation kinetics. This effect indicates that either $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ HRS does not enhance the generation of bactericidal species (not enough catalyst), or that the ones generated by PMS are already germicidal enough. Most likely, it could be a kinetic limitation driven by the adsorption of HRS onto bacteria.

In order to further investigate the germicidal species generated by the HRS/PMS process, we increased the bacterial concentration 10-fold, still in presence of DCF, and the results are given in figure 7b. # 3.5. DCF degradation mechanism in the HRS/PMS process: action modes, radical vs. non-radical

# degradation mechanisms and transformation pathways

From the results so far, we can verify that the notable activity of HRS for the removal of DCF and microorganisms in water is the net sum of a number of processes. In this part, in order to fully elucidate the full path towards elimination, we focus on the four distinct steps: analysis of the (theoretically)

involved processes, examination of the physicochemical properties of the catalyst, scavenging tests and radical determination, and finally, DCF degradation pathway.

# 3.5.1. Dissociating the complex catalytic process

First, we attempt to dissociate the occurring processes in order to further elucidate the mechanism of DCF removal and bacterial inactivation. The occurring actions can be separated to:

1. Adsorption. Adsorption of the contaminant (biological or chemical) onto HRS would be the first step of the complex degradation process since PMS and HRS need to come in contact, in order to achieve activation and generate the reactive species responsible for disinfection/decontamination. HRS at pH 6.5 presents a positive charge which in conjunction with the negative charge of DCF $( \mathsf { p K a } = 4 . 1 5$ ) or the overall negative charge of bacteria (due to their teichoic acids and peptidoglycans), present an electrostatic attraction that enhance the adsorption to HRS. We have measured a ${ \sim } 1 5 \%$ of DCF removal only by HRS, that could be attributed to the adsorbed DCF, but insignificant bacterial reduction.

2. Direct, PMS-driven oxidation, without activation. PMS is a stable oxidant with a 1.81 V oxidation potential, that can directly react with organic contaminants, including DCF (Yang et al., 2018), as well as bacteria, as seen in our results and other studies (Rodríguez-Chueca et al., 2019). Its effects are noticeable even without activation in high concentrations that overcome the kinetic limitation. In the present study, we have measured a $\sim 1 0 \%$ DCF reduction due to direct PMS oxidation and a 6-logU bacterial reduction, at $7 5 ~ \mathsf { m g / L }$ . Hence, the direct PMS action has to be accounted for, via its $S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - } / { } ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 }$ generation, reaction with DCF and the direct oxidation of membrane components (cell wall potential ${ \sim } 0 . 7 \lor )$ ), also possibly via the aforementioned routes.

3. PMS Activation by HRS: The generation of oxidative species passes through the activation of PMS and then results to the oxidation of the targets. Depending on the reaction localization, the activation could be either:

Homogeneous PMS activation (leached Fe-HRS/PMS system): We have measured a small but existent amount of dissolved Fe in the bulk of the medium after reaction (at $\mathsf { p H } = 7$ ). Because of the iron leaching measured, an effective Fe-mediated catalytic cycle takes place, which results in the generation of $\mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ , $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals. As a result, although at more acidic conditions this contribution is expected to be higher, we suggest its importance at the studied conditions, i.e. that this pathway of activation is actively contributing to disinfection and decontamination.

Heterogeneous PMS activation (interfacial HRS/PMS action): The reactions that take place in the interface between HRS and PMS are expected to further contribute in DCF degradation and bacterial inactivation. These reactions are governed by the pH, causing the electrostatic repulsion/attraction between catalyst and PMS or target (bacteria/DCF), and the type of radical that is generated. Generally, activation of PMS and generation of oxidative species take place with the Fe bound on the surface of the HRS catalyst and cycling must take place there, hence different $k$ rate constants need to be considered. Nevertheless, this pathway is not straightforward and in order to define the degradation pathway, we would need to check the oxidation state of the catalyst and the reactive species generated from the HRS/PMS process.

In order to begin the elucidation of the degradation processes, first we assessed the changes in the catalyst. After experimentation in the HRS/PMS system, the catalyst was recovered, and its characterization was performed anew; the results are summarized in Figure 8. The HRTEM after the catalytic reaction showed that the HRSB maintained a similar shape with the same size as the pristine HRSB, confirming the good structural stability of the prepared beads. Hence, the comparison of the rest of its properties is valid since the catalyst was not significantly altered.   
The XRD patterns revealed that the principal constituents of the HRS were $a \mathrm { - } \mathsf { F e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathsf { S i O } _ { 2 }$ . A part of $\mathsf { F e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ in the catalyst, after being used in the degradation of DCF, was reduced to $\mathsf { F e } _ { 3 } \mathsf { O } _ { 4 }$ , which could be verified by the diffraction peaks at 30.035°, 35.442°, 43.065°, 53.373°, 56.873°, $6 2 . 5 5 2 ^ { \circ }$ in the XRD pattern (Fig. 8) (Deng and Li, 2018), instead of 28.1, 38.7, 41.6, 47.83, 58.1, 63.7, 67.9, 74 and $7 5 . 9 ^ { \circ }$ of the pristine HRS catalyst.   
Furthermore, the fresh HRS contained 42.3 wt.% O, 40.5 wt.% Fe, and 11.8 wt.% Si. After using the catalyst for the activation of PMS, the wt.% of O, Fe and Si were 31.6, 59.6, and 5.7, respectively. This change in elemental composition is related to the minor formation of $\mathsf { F e } _ { 3 } \mathsf { O } _ { 4 }$ in the reduced hematite after the reaction (Wei et al., 2019) with PMS, proved by the XRD patterns of fresh and used HRS. The peak located at 106.5 eV in fresh HRS that has shifted to 107.4 eV in the used sample indicates that Si existed as ${ \mathsf { S i } } ^ { 4 + }$ in the $F e \mathrm { - } S i O _ { 2 }$ catalyst (Feng et al., 2017). The Fe peaks at binding energies at around 716 eV and 730 eV seen in the fresh HRS were attributed to the Fe $2 { \mathsf { p } } _ { 3 / 2 }$ and Fe $2 { \mathsf { p } } _ { 1 / 2 }$ , respectively, from the hematite $\mathsf { F e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ in the fresh samples. In the used sample the peaks at binding energies in the range of 715 eV and 729 eV are related to the Fe $2 { \mathsf { p } } _ { 3 / 2 }$ and Fe $2 { \mathsf { p } } _ { 1 / 2 }$ of $\mathsf { F e } _ { 3 } \mathsf { O } _ { 4 }$ for used HRS. Feng et al. (2017) reported that the shakeup satellite peak at 719.0 eV (Fe $2 { \mathsf { p } } _ { 3 / 2 }$ ) was fingerprint of the electronic structure of $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ .

Hence we have the first indication of the PMS activation mechanism, where electron abstraction from PMS to reduce $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ to $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ took place with simultaneous $S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ generation, as a first step, and the $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ activation of PMS as a second step, that generates $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ . The measured change in the percentage of Fe may also indicate a small loss in the bulk. Also, the shift in the peak of the XPS spectrum verifies the formation of magnetite and validate the proposed cycle.

# 3.5.3. Identifying the degradation mechanism: Radical scavenger testing and EPR measurements

Following, by employing EPR spectroscopy and the choice of proper scavenging tests, we will attempt to resolve the composite action found in the HRS/PMS system and unveil the pathways towards DCF degradation. Most likely, the same pathways will be true for bacterial inactivation, but the outcome of their inactivation is also subject to their reactivity with the generated transient species (Kohantorabi et al., 2019; Serna-Galvis et al., 2018, 2019).

The degradation of DCF in the heterogeneous activation of PMS might occur through radical $( \mathrm { H O ^ { bullet } } / \mathrm { S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } } )$ mechanism, according to Eqs. 1-3, or the singlet oxygenation and mediated electron transfer non-radical mechanism of Eqs. 4-5 (Othman et al., 2020). Furthermore, the secondary reactions 6-11 cannot be disregarded, hence the identification of the radical species $( \mathrm { H } 0 ^ { \bullet } / \mathrm { S } 0 _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } / { } ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ participating in DCF degradation is crucial.

EPR experiments employing TEMP $\{ 1 0 0 ~ \mathsf { m } \mathsf { M } _ { \ r { i } } ^ { \prime }$ ), a well-known radical spin trap of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ that results in the formation of a stable TEMPO radical with three-equal intensity pattern signal (REF), were conducted in the presence of PMS $\lvert 7 5 \mathrm { \ m g / L } \rvert$ and HRS $\{ 1 0 \mathrm { m g / L } \}$ in aerated media. Control experiments in which only TEMP was employed in the absence or in the presence of PMS or HRS were performed in parallel. All the experiments were carried out in ${ \sf D } _ { 2 } { \sf O }$ to enhance the prospects of TEMP to trap $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ since its lifetime is more than one order of magnitude larger than in $H _ { 2 } O$ (REF).

Results showed the formation of the typical TEMPO signal (Figure 9a). The highest amount of TEMPO was observed when PMS and HRS were present together in the aerated TEMP mixtures. However, while samples of TEMP alone and with HRS show the similar amounts of TEMPO, its generation increased in mixtures of TEMP and PMS. In the presence of PMS, TEMPO formation is produced by initial TEMP oxidation (TEMP.+ ), followed by subsequent deprotonation and reaction with $\mathsf { O } _ { 2 }$ . This process has been described for aerated TEMP mixtures in the presence of oxidants different from PMS (REF). According to the intensity of the TEMPO signal formed from TEMP-PMS mixtures in the absence and in the presence of HRS, more than $30 \%$ of the signal intensity can be attributed to the formation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ .

Experiments in the presence of DMPO were performed to confirm the presence of intermediates of radical nature such us HO• and/or $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ in the aqueous mixture of PMS and HRS. Interestingly, while control experiments in the absence of PMS or HRS did not showed any signal, several spin adducts were detected after a careful deconvolution of the signals obtained from the aqueous DMPO $( 1 0 0 \mathsf { m } \mathsf { M } )$ in the presence of PMS $7 5 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ and HRS $\{ 1 0 \mathrm { m g / L } \}$ (see Figure 9b). Thus, DMPO-OH, DMPO-H (which proves the participation of reaction 1) and a mixture of compounds associated to DMPO-degradation were identified, while the one corresponding to DMPO- ${ \sf S O } _ { 4 }$ was absent.

However, the absence of the DMPO-SO4 signal does not discard its generation upon reaction of PMS and HRS because the lifetime of the DMPO-SO4 adduct in $H _ { 2 } O$ is very short and quickly evolves to the formation of DMPO-OH (REF). In this context, additional experiments were carried out in order to confirm that the DMPO-OH signal could be attributed to the initial formation of HO• and/or $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , working under the assumption that the absence of DMPO- ${ \sf S O } _ { 4 }$ does not preclude its formation since once formed, DMPO-SO4 quickly evolves to the longer-lived DMPO-OH. In these tests, $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ was generated by homolytic rupture upon irradiation of a deaerated aqueous solution of $( N H _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 }$ $( 7 5 ~ \mathrm { m g / L } )$ in the presence of DMPO $( 1 0 ~ \mathsf { m M } )$ ) (see details in the Supplementary figures S3-S5). Only when a high concentration of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ was generated by keeping a constant irradiation, a mixture of two different signals could be observed attributed to DMPO- ${ \sf S O _ { 4 } }$ and DMPO-OH. Nevertheless, once the irradiation was stopped only DMPO-OH could be observed. Hence, the appearance of DMPO-OH as a result of the reaction between PMS and HRS does not provide an unambiguous piece of evidence for the HO• generation. Hence, from the EPR experiments, we can provisionally conclude that the reaction between PMS and HRS in aerated aqueous media gives rise to the formation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ,$ , as it was demonstrated when TEMP was used as spin-trap, while experiments in the presence of DMPO provide evidence for the participation of HO• and/or $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , but do not confer certainty over their participation and importance.

Afterwards, a series of selective intermediate quenching tests were implemented in order to determine the predominant reactive species for the DCF degradation in the PMS/HRS system. The quenching agents were MeOH, TBA and NaN3 (Figure 9c).

DCF degradation by PMS and HRS was initially performed in the absence of scavengers, and at the same concentration of the three quenchers (0.1 M). From the comparison of the experimental results, inhibition of the DCF degradation was only observed in the presence of ${ \mathsf { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ . This result can be easily understood because it has been reported that ${ \mathsf { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ traps $\mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ , $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , while MeOH and TBA only traps HO• and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ . Besides, NaN3 shows higher reaction rate constants $( \mathsf { k } _ { 0 \mathsf { H } } \cdot = 2 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathsf { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 } , \mathsf { k } _ { 5 0 4 } \cdot ^ { - } =$ $1 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 0 } \mathsf { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $\mathsf { k } ^ { 1 } \mathsf { \Lambda } _ { 0 2 } = 1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathsf { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ than those described for MeOH ( $\mathsf { k } _ { 0 \mathsf { H } ^ { \ast } } = 9 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathsf { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $k _ { 5 0 4 } \cdot \bar { \cdot } = 3 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } \mathsf { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ ) and for TBA $( \mathsf { k } _ { 0 \mathsf { H } } \cdot = 6 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathsf { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $k _ { 5 0 4 } \cdot \overline { { \cdot } } = 4 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathsf { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathsf { S } ^ { - 1 } )$ (REF, (Anipsitakis and Dionysiou, 2004)).

Interestingly, a deeper analysis of these results seems to indicate that $\mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ must not be the main intermediate involved in the DCF degradation, because the $\mathsf { k o H } ^ { \bullet }$ for the three quenchers has similar values and they were used at the same molar concentration (0.1 M) but degradation of DCF is only diminished by NaN3. Further support was found using 1 M concentrations of MeOH and TBA in this study, because if the main radical involved in this process was HO• , the presence of MeOH and TBA at 1 M (both have close $\mathsf { k o H } ^ { \bullet }$ .) would generate an effect similar between them and higher than that observed using NaN3 at 0.1 M. As this was not the case, consequently, the main reactive species should be $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ because the DCF degradation was diminished in the presence of 1 M MeOH but not by 1 M TBA in agreement with a value of the kSO4.- by MeOH of ca. one order of magnitude higher than that described for TBA (see values above).

Moreover, the role of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ as the main intermediate involved the DCF degradation is also in agreement with the high effect observed using ${ \mathsf { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ at 0.1 M because its kSO4.- is more than 1000 times higher than that of MeOH. Nevertheless, a minor effect of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ in the DCF degradation could not be disregarded because the in the absence of oxygen a lower degradation percentage was observed (see in Figure 9c, results under anaerobic conditions).

Hence we can conclude that the combination of the scavenger tests and the EPR experiments clearly confirm that the main intermediate of DCF degradation is $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and the participation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ in this process is not negligible, while HO• participation is significantly less important.

Finally, having identified the elements that participate in the reaction, its mode (primarily heterogeneous degradation) and the participating radicals, a proposition for DCF degradation would be as follows:

PMS produces $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ after activation by the HRS soil containing metal oxides such as α- Fe2O3/Fe3O4 through its adsorption onto the catalyst's surface, followed by the charge transfer contributed by the redox centers and oxygen defects present in the lattice of the $a \mathrm { - } \mathsf { F e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ surface between PMS molecules and the catalyst. (Huang et al., 2013) reported that high catalytic activity of α-Fe2O3 was related to the presence of active lattice oxygen in the structure of hematite. The $a \mathrm { - } \mathsf { F e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ in the HRS might also mediate transferring the electrons from DCF molecules to PMS (Tian et al., 2017; Ahn et al., 2016) which could be resulted in PMS reduction (with $S O _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ formation) and thus DCF oxidation. The co-existence of different transition metals of Mn, Ti, Cu, Mg, and Zn as well as Si might also improve the catalytic potential of HRS (Liang et al., 2010).

Another reason for the enhanced catalytic potential of the HRS might be the presence of sulfur (1 wt.%) and calcium (1.3 wt.%) in the structure of the catalyst which could enhance the electron transfer from $a \mathrm { - } \mathsf { F e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ to PMS thus accelerating acceleration of $S 0 _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radical and singlet oxygen generation (Zheng et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2020). In addition, the presence of the oxygen vacancies/defects in the surface of the catalyst, as a natural material, could decrease the reaction barrier of PMS and thereby improve its activation (Gao et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020). Therefore, the interaction with $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ rather than with $\mathrm { H O ^ { \bullet } }$ is proposed to be the main mechanism for the degradation of DCF in the developed HRS/PMS process.

Although small, the contribution of singlet oxygen is promising: despite its mild redox potential (0.64-0.81 V) (Diaz-Uribe et al., 2010), it has a long lifetime (up to ${ \sim } 4 \mu \mathsf { s }$ in water, Kohantorabi et al., 2019) and selectivity toward the electron-rich compounds (Chen et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018), and suggests that the developed process could be an efficient method of treating waters contaminated with compounds bearing electron-rich moieties such as DCF (Nihemaiti et al., 2018).

# 4. Conclusions

A naturally occurring ferruginous soil (Hormuz Red Soil-HRS) was characterized and used as a natural material for PMS activation, aimed at developing an efficient AOP for degradation and mineralization of an emerging water contaminant model, Diclofenac (DCF), and the inactivation of two model bacterial contaminants, E. coli and Enterococcus sp. The catalytic potential of HRS under different conditions of catalyst and oxidant concentrations, solution pH and water anions were evaluated, and in mild operational conditions, namely low HRS catalyst loading and modest PMS concentrations, we report effective disinfection and decontamination even in presence of high chloride or sulfate concentrations. Scavenging experiments in conjunction with EPR measurements suggest a dominant radical pathway of oxidation; a $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ based mechanism was found to be the dominant driving force in the efficient oxidation and mineralization of DCF, with parallel contribution from $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ .

From the engineering point of view, HRS supported on alginate beads was prepared (HRSB), and its potential and durability to activate PMS for the oxidation of DCF was confirmed over 5 catalytic cycles. The HRSB/PMS process in a continuous flow packed bed reactor was examined, and complete degradation of DCF was achieved at an HRT as low as 2 min. The LC/MS analysis of the effluent was indicated that DCF was degraded into less toxic, simple and biodegradable by-products after treatment by the developed process.

In conclusion, this work provided critical insights in the non-radical pathways of disinfection and decontamination by PMS activation. Also, it is proven that HRS is an active, easily separable, and stable natural catalyst to be used for efficient PMS activation, and the HRSB/PMS is deemed to be a cheap and promising technology of continuous flow treatment for disinfection and decontamination of emerging contaminants from water and wastewater streams.
