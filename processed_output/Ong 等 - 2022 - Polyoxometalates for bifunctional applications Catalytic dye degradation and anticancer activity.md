# 1. Introduction

In the past decades, the rapid industrial development has obvious deleterious effects on the environment. For example, the synthetic dye effluents from the textile production plant are known to be harmful and toxic. Direct contact with these hazardous dyes may cause skin and eye irritation, allergic conjunctivitis, asthma, nausea and even cell mutation (Carneiro et al., 2010; Tkaczyk et al., 2020). Several technologies such as membrane filtration, flocculation, adsorption, ozonation, photodegradation and biodegradation have been utilized to purify the dye-containing wastewater, which each method shows certain limitations (Katheresan et al., 2018; El Hassani et al., 2019; Gusain et al., 2019; Matos et al., 2019; Routoula and Patwardhan, 2020). Advanced oxidation process (AOP) such as Fenton reaction, has been proven to effectively decay hazardous pollutants by hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ .However, the major drawbacks are the risk of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ storage, extreme operating $\mathsf { p H }$ and the production of iron sludge, which have restrained its long-term application (Jain et al., 2018).

In contrast to the classical Fenton process, the sulfate radicals $( \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - } } )$ -based AOP not only displays stronger oxidation strength, higher redox potential, better stability, longer half-life, higher tolerance towards wider pH range, but also eliminates $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ storage issues (Nidheesh and Rajan, 2016). Heat treatment (Kordkandi and Forouzesh, 2014), ultrasonic (Cherifi et al., 2019), ultraviolet (UV) radiation (Rehman et al., 2018; Ding et al., 2020), alkaline activation (Qi et al., 2016) and catalyst activation (Nidheesh and Rajan, 2016; Li et al., 2020) are among the techniques broadly enacted to induce $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } { \cdot } ^ { - }$ radicals, with catalyst activation as the most favored method due to its energy-free system. (Tan et al., 2018) and (Mahdavianpour et al., 2020) illustrated the role of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ ions to incite the formation of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals from peroxymonosulphate (PMS) and peroxydisulphate (PDS) to decompose acid orange 7 and methylene blue respectively. Nevertheless, (Anipsitakis and Dionysiou, 2004) revealed that ruthenium- and cobalt-containing materials performed better than iron-based materials, yet the exorbitant cost of ruthenium is not economically viable. Thus, cobalt-based materials seem to be the auspicious candidates for PMS activation. Various type of dyes, such as rhodamine B, methylene blue, reactive black 5, methyl orange, acid yellow g, acid orange 7 and methyl violet have been successfully decomposed with PMS activated reaction (Yao et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018b; Wang et al., 2021).

Polyoxometalates (POMs), a large class of eco-friendly molecular inorganic compounds, impact the field of science and technology, in terms of their diversified structure and application. The field of catalysis is a prevalent application of POMs due to their excellent redox properties and non-toxic nature (Dolbecq et al., 2010). Particularly, in term of catalysis, POMs have been widely employed as an effective water oxidation catalyst (Du et al., 2015; Han et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2018d). In addition, POMs also have potential to act as a catalyst for wastewater treatment. (Hu and Xu, 2004) demonstrated the potential of POMs to act as a photocatalyst for the decomposition of brilliant red X3B dye under UV light condition. (Sheng et al., 2014) reported the aptitude of POMs to decompose dye contaminants upon solar irradiation. (Zeb et al., 2018) found that the synthesized iron-based POMs can eliminate rhodamine B dye via photo-Fenton reaction, a type of AOP. Nevertheless, photobased reaction is still prominent in POMs related research whereas negligible works have been conducted to interrogate the prospective application of POMs in non-photo reaction, such as catalyst activated AOP.

As discussed earlier, persistent exposure of synthetic dyes has the potential to cause cell mutation or cancer. Indeed, cancer is predicted to be the number two cause of death worldwide, overtaking infectious diseases by 2025, hence it is crucial to discover innovative solutions to cure cancer, such as by developing new anticancer materials (Olsen et al., 2008; Torre et al., 2016). In this regard, apart from catalysis, POMs have also emerged as a new class of anticancer agents. Although the exact mechanisms of anticancer activity of POMs are still not fully elucidated, POMs are found to have high affinity for growth-related enzymes such as actin, kinases and P-type ATPs (Gabas et al., 2016; Bijelic et al., 2019). Once internalized into the cell, the high oxidative potential of POMs can lead to significant increase in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) reducing the levels glutathione, a ROS scavenger, causing the dissipation of the mitochondrial membrane potential and ultimately leading to programmed cell death or apoptosis (Trembley et al., 2009). POMs are not newcomers in this arena of anticancer as numerous reports have well recognized their strong structure-dependent anticancer properties (Yamase et al., 1988). Furthermore, the existence of cobalt in POMs structure considerably improved the anticancer activity either in vitro (El Moll et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2016) or in vivo (Zhai et al., 2009; Guo et al., 2011). The potential of POMs is worth exploring in detail because of their sheer abundance, with different structures and working chemistries leading to unique combinatory properties.

Herein, the potential applications of cobalt-based POMs, $\mathrm { N a _ { 1 0 } [ C o _ { 4 } ( H _ { 2 } O ) _ { 2 } ( V W _ { 9 } O _ { 3 4 } ) _ { 2 } ] \cdot 3 4 H _ { 2 } O }$ were examined. The dye degradation experiments were carried out by as-synthesized POMs via PMS activation at room temperature and moderate $\mathrm { p H }$ The mild reaction condition is more feasible for future large-scale application. In this study, methylene blue (MB) and rhodamine B (RB) dye are chosen as the subject dyes for the degradation test because they are carcinogenic and used widely in the textile industry for the dyeing of fabric (Veisi et al., 2019). In addition, in vitro experiments were carried out to authenticate the anticancer activity of the nascent POMs. To the best of our knowledge, there is no report on the catalytic dye degradation using as-synthesized POMs as catalyst. This research provides new insight for the application of POMs for PMS activation to purify the dye-containing wastewater. This is also the first study to verify the bifunctional properties of the synthesized POMs, $\mathrm { N a _ { 1 0 } [ C o _ { 4 } ( H _ { 2 } O ) _ { 2 } ( V W , O _ { 3 4 } ) _ { 2 } ] \cdot 3 4 H _ { 2 } O , }$ particularly in environmental and biological applications.

# 2. Experimental methods

# 2.1 Materials synthesis

In this experiment, all chemicals are of analytical grade and used directly without purification unless otherwise stated. Deionized water (DI water, $1 8 . 2 \ M \Omega \cdot c m \ .$ )was used for the preparation of all reagents. The cobalt-based POMs, $\mathrm { N a _ { 1 0 } [ C o _ { 4 } ( H _ { 2 } O ) _ { 2 } ( V W _ { 9 } O _ { 3 4 } ) _ { 2 } ] \cdot _ { \it x H _ { 2 } O } }$ was synthesized according to the reported method with trivial modification (Li et al., 2009; Lv et al., 2014). $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { M }$ sodium acetate buffer $\mathrm { ( p H ~ 4 . 8 ) }$ was prepared from the mixture of sodium acetate $( \mathrm { C } _ { 2 } \mathrm { H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N a O } _ { 2 }$ ,Sigma-Aldrich) and glacial acetic acid ( $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C O O H }$ ,Merck). $5 . 9 4 \textrm { g }$ sodium tungsten dehydrate $( \mathrm { N a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { W O } _ { 4 } { \cdot } 2 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.24 g sodium metavanadate $\mathrm { ( N a V O } _ { 3 }$ Sigma-Aldrich) were then dissolved in $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { M }$ sodium acetate buffer solution, yielding a bright yellow solution as in Fig. S1 (a). The solution turned into light brown upon the addition of $1 . 1 7 \ \mathrm { g }$ cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate $( \mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ ,SigmaAldrich). It was then constantly stirred with a magnetic stirrer and heated at a temperature of $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 2 hours. The resulting dark brown solution as in Fig. S1 (b) was filtered and left aside for the crystallization process until the formation of dark brown crystals via selfassembly process. As validated from the characterization test, the chemical formula of the crystals produced as displayed in Fig. S2 was $\mathrm { N a _ { 1 0 } [ C o _ { 4 } ( H _ { 2 } O ) _ { 2 } ( V W , O _ { 3 4 } ) _ { 2 } ] \cdot 3 4 H _ { 2 } O }$ ,denoted as CoV-POMs throughout the study.

# 2.2 Characterization

PerkinElmer Frontier spectrometer was used to record the Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra while thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted by TA instrument TGA Q500 under nitrogen atmosphere with a temperature ramp of $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) test was effectuated with PerkinElmer Optima 8300. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was gathered by Bruker D8 advanced diffractometer with $\mathrm { C u K } \alpha$ radiation for the crystallographic study. Field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) techniques were employed to examine the morphology and elemental composition of the materials, in which the tests were performed by FESEM JEOL 7600F equipped with EDX Oxford Instruments detector. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were garnered by using AXIS SUPR with an Al Ka radiation source.

# 2.3 Dye degradation

The CoV-POMs catalytic performance towards dye degradation was evaluated based on the change in solution absorbance intensity. The initial $\mathrm { p H }$ of dye solution was regulated by either sulphuric acid $\mathrm { \Delta } \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ ,Honeywell) or sodium hydroxide (NaOH, Sigma-Aldrich). Conventionally, the catalyst and PMS (Sigma-Aldrich) were added simultaneously into 100 mL of dye solution with an initial dye concentration $\left( \mathbf { C } _ { 0 } \right)$ of $2 0 \ \mathrm { m g / L }$ . The tests were conducted in batch mode at room temperature and the solution was stirred with magnetic stirrer incessantly to ensure homogeneity. At a certain reaction interval, $3 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of the aliquot was pipetted into a quartz cuvette and its absorbance spectrum was measured by Shimadzu UV-2700 at $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = 6 6 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ and $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = 5 5 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for methylene blue (MB, Merck) and rhodamine B (RB, Sigma Aldrich) respectively. All the measurements were conducted at least in triplicates and presented as mean $\pm$ standard deviation for data precision. Solution decolorization and reduction in absorbance values signify the effectiveness of catalysts towards dye degradation. # 2.4 Anticancer property

The cell lines used in this study were of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells and A549 human lung cancer cells acquired from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cell lines were cultivated in Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with $10 \%$ fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone) and 1x penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in an incubator with $5 \%$ carbon dioxide $\left( \mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } \right)$ . The cell culture medium was replaced alternate days and the cells were passaged prior to the experiments. In this work,   
experiments were carried out in 96 well plate format, in which cells were seeded at a seeding density of $9 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { c e l l s } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ After initial seeding, the cells were treated with CoV-POMs at various concentrations for another 24 hours. The CoV-POMs were freshly pulverized, dispersed in DMEM and sonicated before each use. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the resazurin-based PrestoBlue (Invitrogen) cell viability assay and cell morphologies were observed using an inverted phase contrast microscope (PCM). As for the annexin-V apoptosis assay, the cells were cultured in 24 well plates at the same seeding density for 24 hours at 37 $^ \circ \mathrm C$ and $5 \% \mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ ,before using $0 . 2 5 ~ \%$ trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco) to detach the cells, and the assay was performed using the annexin-V apoptosis detection kit (InvitrogenTM 88-8007- 72). The stained cells were analyzed using the image based Arthur™M cell analyzer (NanoEnTekTM). All biological assays were conducted in triplicates.

# 3.2 Catalytic activity towards dye degradation

The catalytic performance tests were carried out to scrutinize the potential of CoV-POMs to activate PMS for dye putrefaction. In this study, invariable $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ PMS and $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ dye concentrations were adopted to inspect the relationship between catalyst dosage and dye degradation performance. Fig. 5 (a) and 5 (b) demonstrate that PMS alone was ineffective towards dye decomposition, in which meager degradation of less than $1 0 ~ \%$ was observed for

the control experiments with pure dye and PMS. The slight degradation was probably caused by PMS self-decomposition process, but the reaction was limited. Nevertheless, dye solution started to decolorize after the simultaneous addition of catalyst and PMS, illustrating the role of CoV-POMs in PMS activation to catalyze free radicals reactions (Wang and Wang, 2018). It was noticed that a trace amount of catalyst ( $1 0 ~ \mathrm { { m g / L } ) }$ was sufficient to attain a high MB removal efficiency of $8 7 . 8 ~ \%$ in $3 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . In the first 5 min of reaction, increment of catalyst dosage from $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ to $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ remarkably escalated the MB degradation efficiency from 4   
$7 3 . 4 ~ \%$ to $8 7 . 5 ~ \%$ . Meanwhile, it is worth to mention that by augmenting the amount of CoVPOMs catalyst to $4 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , a complete MB degradation was achieved within $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ with the corresponding rate constant of $0 . 0 8 5 \ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , yielding a clear solution. The apparent first-order rate constant value was closely 3.5 times than that of $0 . 0 2 3 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ for $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ catalyst, showing the efficacy of CoV-POMs catalyst to trigger the generation of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals from   
PMS for absolute dye decomposition.

Apart from MB dye, CoV-POMs also displayed positive outcomes towards RB dye degradation, in which the dye decaying trend was comparable to MB dye, butwith a lower reaction rate. For an instance, the batch reactor containing $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ CoV-POMs and 150 $\mathrm { m g / L }$ PMS could utterly degrade MB dye but only $9 3 . 3 ~ \%$ of RB dye was decomposed in 30 min. At the same reaction condition, the time required for a complete RB dye decomposition was almost doubled that of MB dye. In general, the RB dye degradation efficiency was improved by increasing CoV-POMs concentration. Remarkably, the pseudo first-order rate constant value was doubled up from $0 . 0 3 1 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ to $0 . 0 6 3 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ when the catalyst dosage raised from $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ to $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ .This indicates that at a higher catalyst dosage, more PMS was activated due to the existence of more reactive species to produce additional $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ radicals for dye decomposition. However, the dye decaying efficiency was almost similar for the system containing $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ and $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ CoV-POMs after $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction, indicating the possibility of PMS as the limiting reactant that control the overall reaction rate.

In Table S1, it is noticed that although higher rate constant of $0 . 0 9 3 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ was attained by further increasing the catalyst dosage to $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ ,compared to $0 . 0 8 5 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ of $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ catalyst, however the improvement in the MB dye removal efficiency was nearly imperceptible due to the PMS loading limitation, the similar observation as RB dye. Thus, the PMS loading was manipulated to probe its effect towards dye degradation. The dosage of CoV-POMs catalyst was fixed at $4 0 \mathrm { \ m g / L }$ , the concentration that led to a complete decolorization of MB dye in $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ Fig. 5 (c) depicts the degradation efficiency only hitting $9 1 . 7 ~ \%$ instead of $1 0 0 \%$ when the PMS loading was $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ , manifesting a low quantity of PMS may be inadequate to wholly decay MB dye. Assuredly, the raise in the PMS loading improved the MB dye degradation ability owing to the higher concentration of oxidant sources, which in turn producing more reactive $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals (Chen et al., 2019). In particular, the apparent rate-constant values rose from $0 . 0 8 5 \ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ to $0 . 1 0 4 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } }$ when the PMS loading increased from $1 5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ to $2 5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ in the MB dye degradation analysis.

In terms of RB dye, the effects of PMS loading towards dye degradation demonstrated an analogous pattern than that of MB dye. For example, in Fig. 5 (d), it was noticed that the RB dye degradation efficiency surged from $8 5 . 3 ~ \%$ to $9 3 . 6 \%$ with the increment in PMS loading from $5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ to $1 0 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ .The continuous increment of PMS loading to $2 5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ converted dye into a colorless solution within $4 5 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ ,with an apparent first-order rate constant of $0 . 0 7 3 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . All the experimental outcomes suggest the pivotal role of CoVPOMs catalyst in PMS activation to decompose both the MB and RB dye effectively. The imperceptibly lower RB degradation efficiency was explained by the steric hindrance effect by the fact that RB is more complex and much larger than MB in terms of molecule size. Based on the structural formula of RB dye in Fig. 1 (b), the presence of bulky carboxyphenyl group may obstruct the pathway for the incursion of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ free radicals towards dye (Liu et al., 2017). The plot in Fig. 5 (e) and 5 (f) present the summary of the pseudo first-order rate constant values for dye degradation and their dependency towards catalyst dosage and PMS loading.

The dye-containing wastewater typically has a wide pH fluctuation, hence a further investigation was carried out to study the effect of solution pH on the dye degradation performance (Yaseen and Scholz, 2019). The dye concentration, catalyst dosage and PMS loading were fixed at $2 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ and $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ respectively for the pH related study. In Fig. 5 (g), MB dye manifests the best degradation activity at neutral condition of $\mathrm { p H } ~ 7$ . following by acidic condition of pH 3. Meanwhile, the lowest MB dye degradation efficiency of $78 . 7 \%$ was attained in 30 min at basic condition of $\mathrm { p H } \ 1 1$ . An identical trend was observed in RB dye, but with a more prominent effect, in which the RB dye degradation efficiency plummeted from $9 3 . 3 ~ \%$ to $4 1 . 1 2 \%$ by increasing the solution pH from 7 to 11. At acidic condition of $\mathrm { p H } 3$ , the dye degradation efficiency of RB dye was almost similar to that of in neutral environment of $\mathrm { p H } 7$ It was noticed that CoV-POMs/PMS system worked well under acidic and neutral environment to decompose MB and RB dye effectively, but the reaction was inhibited at basic condition. This phenomenon could be elucidated by the fact that PMS existed mostly in a less reactive form of ${ \mathrm { S O } } _ { 5 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ under basic environment and impeded the overall dye degradation reaction rate. Furthermore, the self-decomposition of ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } { \mathrm { - } }$ could be initiated by OH presence in alkaline solution and reduced the lifetime of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals. The standard reduction potentials of ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } { \mathrm { - } }$ and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } { \cdot } ^ { - }$ radicals were also found to be lower at basic condition that tarnished the oxidizing ability of MS towards dye decomposition. On the other hand, the generation of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ radicals could be promoted by acid (Lei et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2020).

Apart from that, quenching tests with the addition of radical scavengers were further conducted to elucidate the principal radicals involve in MB and RB dyes degradation, as supported by the facts that catalyst activated PMS reaction will generate two major reactive radicals of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ and hydroxyl (OH·). The $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals produced from the PMS activation reaction may lead to the formation of OH radicals by the following pathway: $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } \mathrm { ^ - + O H ^ { - }  S O _ { 4 } \mathrm { ^ { 2 - } + O H ^ { . } } } }$

The radical scavengers of ethanol ( $C _ { 2 } H _ { 5 } O H$ , Merck) and tert-butanol ( $C _ { 4 } H _ { 1 0 } O$ , Sigma Aldrich) were used for quenching experiments. Ethanol with $\mathfrak { a }$ -hydrogen is an efficient scavenger for both OH· and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals with the high rate constant of $1 . 6 - 7 . 7 \mathrm { ~ x ~ } 1 0 ^ { 7 } \mathrm { ~ M ~ } ^ { \ - 1 }$ $\mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 . 2 - 2 . 8 \mathrm { ~ x ~ } 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { ~ s } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively. Although tert-butanol is an active OH scavengers, it shows negligible reactivity for $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ · radicals, whereby the rate constant of OH· radicals $( 3 . 8 - 7 . 6 \mathrm { ~ x ~ } 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { ~ M ~ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { ~ s } ^ { - 1 } )$ is almost 1000 times higher than that of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals $( 4 . 0 - 9 . 1 ~ \mathrm { x }$ $1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ (Huan et al. 2020 Wang et al., 2020).

Fig. 6 (a) reveals that MB dye was completely degraded within $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ in a non-inhibitor system, but after the introduction of $5 \ \mathrm { m L }$ of tert-butanol and ethanol, the dye removal efficiency decreased to $9 5 . 3 ~ \%$ and $8 6 . 5 ~ \%$ respectively. Similar trend was observed in Fig. 6 (b), in which RB dye degradation was inhibited by tert-butanol and ethanol, with a plunge of the removal efficiency from $9 3 . 5 \ \mathrm { ~ \% ~ }$ to $89 . 5 \ \%$ and $73 . 9 \%$ respectively. These results depicted the reduction of degradation efficiency upon the introduction of both scavengers, with more significant suppressive effect with ethanol containing system. Therefore, it is proposed that both OH·and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ radicals played a vital role in the dye degradation reaction, with $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ · radicals as the dominant radicals controlling the overall reaction rate. Nevertheless, it is interesting to note that the addition of radicals scavengers do not entirely inhibit the dye degradation reaction, indicating there are still others factor that affected the reaction. Various interaction could occur between CoV-POMs and dye molecules, such as the physical electrostatic force between them that promote the adsorption of dye molecules on CoV-POMs. In addition, there is a possibility that the hydrogen and covalent bonds are formed between dyes and CoV-POMs as reported by (Yahya et al., 2019). These bond formation side reactions could also explain the steep increment in the dye degradation efficiency at the start of reaction. Parts of the dye molecules have been transformed into other products prior undergo further degradation through $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ radicals reaction. Remarkably, in the end of reaction, both MB and RB dyes turned into a colorless solution as portrayed in Figure S4, confirming the effectiveness of CoV-POMs/PMS system towards a complete dye degradation.

# 3.3 POMs as Anticancer Agent

In addition to the excellent catalytic activity, POMs are also known for their unique antiproliferative property against cancer cells. Some POMs characteristics have been studied, including the induction of cellular apoptosis partly by inhibiting protein kinase CK2 (Wang et al., 2014), inhibition of ATP or DNA synthesis (Yamase, 2013), and disruption of tumor angiogenesis by interacting with angiogenic cytokines (Pu et al., 2013). Therefore, in this study, an in vitro cytotoxicity assay was conducted to investigate the anticancer activity of assynthesized CoV-POMs. This cell viability assay involved measuring the fluorescence of resazurin-based PrestoBlue dye, signifying the reducing power of living and actively metabolizing cells.

Fig. 7 (a) shows that CoV-POMs exhibited a dose-dependent cytotoxicity against A549 cells, a widely used lung cancer adenocarcinoma cell line and MDA-MB-231, a highly established breast cancer adenocarcinoma cell line. After exposure to $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ of CoV-POMs, only about $54 \%$ of MDA-MB-231 and $64 \%$ of A549 cells were viable, indicating a more effective killing against MDA-MB-231 cells. This suggests that the activity of CoV-POMs was celltype dependent. Interestingly, the synthesized CoV-POMs were found to be not as toxic to the normal cell line NCM460, in which the cells remained around $80 \%$ viable at the concentration effective against A549 cells, i.e., $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ . The cytotoxicity profile of CoVPOMs suggests a selectively higher toxicity to cancer cells than normal healthy cells, an important overarching criterion for successful anticancer drugs. In addition, the PCM images in Fig. S5 depicts that the cellular morphology of NCM460 cells still retained albeit a slight decrease in the cell number, whereas the original cellular morphologies of MDA-MB-231 and A549 cells was considerably destroyed by the same CoV-POMs concentration. These results constitute an initial step toward the development of safer advanced anticancer agents based on POMs.

According to the cytotoxicity results, it is hypothesized that the killing mechanism of CoVPOMs involves the induction of cellular apoptosis. Therefore, to probe into the extent of cellular apoptosis caused by CoV-POMs, the Annexin-V apoptosis assay was conducted on both A549 and MDA-MB231 cells. Fig. 7 (b) shows that approximately $6 8 \%$ of A549 cells and $72 \%$ of MDA-MB231 cells were in late apoptotic stage after 24 hours of treatment with $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ CoV-POMs. This observation is further supported by the image-based cytometer fluorescence micrograph in Fig. 7 (d) and 7 (e), in which $\mathrm { P I + }$ and annexin $\mathrm { . # 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, the mild synthesized CoV-POMs showed enhanced performance in both the dye degradation and anticancer experiments. The cationic dyes of MB and RB were decomposed with the simultaneous addition of CoV-POMs catalyst and PMS. The catalyst dosage and PMS loading were manipulated to elucidate their effect towards dye decolorization. This work suggests that CoV-POMs catalyst plays an important role in activating PMS to generate $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ · free radicals for dye degradation. Only negligible decomposition was detected without the presence of CoV-POMs catalyst. These constructive results propose the rational design of CoV-POMs/ PMS system to act as an efficient dye degradation medium. In addition, the effectiveness of CoV-POMs can also be extrapolated to the anticancer arena due to the preferential killing of cancer cells over normal cells, which will ultimately lead to developments into a ready-to-use anticancer agent in the future. Our comparative study shows that the killing efficacy of CoV-POMs at $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ is higher against breast cancer compared to lung cancer, highlighting the cell-type dependence of the cytotoxic activity. The findings from these experiments reveal the prospective of CoV-POMs to act as bifunctional materials to perform competently in both the environmental and biomedical applications.
