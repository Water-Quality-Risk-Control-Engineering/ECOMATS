# . Introduction

The increasing occurrence of micropollutants, notably pharmaceuticals, in wastewater has become a significant environmental and health issue [1]. Traditional wastewater treatment techniques face difficulties in completely removing these enduring organic compounds, prompting the need for inventive approaches [2]. The rapid expansion of the global population adds complexity to this issue, leading to elevated levels of micropollutants and intensifying their harmful impact on the water cycle. In response to this challenge, the European Parliament has suggested revisions of urban wastewater regulations [2], incorporating energy and circular economy considerations to manage associated cost and disposal impacts [3].

Among the different technologies used for micropollutant removal, Advanced Oxidation Processes (AOPs) have garnered substantial interest for their efficacy in their mineralising [4]. Specifically, sulphate radicals $( S O _ { 4 } { \cdot } )$ based AOPs are prioritised in this research due to the heightened reactivity of $S O _ { 4 }$ [5] compared to hydroxyl radicals (·OH)

[6], [7]. Catalysts play a pivotal role in the generation of oxidant radicals, being the iron cation a commonly used catalyst [8]. However, homogeneous catalysis involving free metal leads to challenges such as metal sludge generation, reusability, and recovery issues [9,10]. In contrast, heterogeneous catalytic activation, particularly with Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs), is gaining prominence [11]. MOFs are crystalline structures comprising organic ligands attached to metal clusters which have recently demonstrated utility as adsorbents and catalysts [12]. Nonetheless, some monometallic MOFs have exhibited limitations in structural and catalytic stability. To overcome these limitations, there is a growing interest in the development of bimetallic MOFs. Thus, in the field of wastewater treatment, recently bimetallic MOFs of Cu-Fe, have shown promising results in PeroxyMonoSulphate (PMS) activation [13]. Among different metals used for bimetallic synthesis, zinc has been also chosen as the second metal cluster because of its redox properties [14,15] and recoverability [16], along with low toxicity for human intake [17].

In this research, after MOF literature analysis, it was checked that there were several metal clusters, as zinc and iron in bimetallic MOFs, were used in photocatalysis but not explored or specifically synthesised as catalyst to be used in PMS activation AOPs. Moreover, further research revealed that zinc can enhance the iron catalyst activity. Thus, a novel bimetallic MOF, Zn-MIL-53(Fe) was synthesised via a one-step process in this research, intending to enhance MIL53(Fe) catalyst activity and stability performance, aligning with the evolving landscape of wastewater treatment demands.

For this purpose, deep physicochemical evaluation through several analyses of both synthesised MOFs were done. In the first instance, optimal dosages using a PMS-activated system were tested using model pollutant Rhodamine B (RhB) by surface response methodology, which involves various PMS concentrations, MOF dosages and time. Moreover, the optimal conditions were applied to degrade Sulfamethoxazole (SMX) and Fluoxetine (FLX). Finally, reuse capacities were analysed to verify its operational stability.

These techniques were used to establish and confirm MOFs' reactivity, structure, and properties. The Raman spectroscopy was carried out through Horiba Jobin Yvon HR800UV Spectrometer and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was accomplished by using CasaXPS.

# 2.3.5. Thermogravimetry analysis

Thermogravimetry analysis (TGA) indicates the loss of mass between temperature ranges and the literature reported a methodology to determine the exact composition of a presented MOF [18]. The analysis was done by using the TG-DSC Setsys evolution 16/18 (Setaram) analyser with temperature range conditions from 20 to $1 2 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ .

# 2.3.6. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller

Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis aims to explain the physical adsorption of gas molecules on a solid surface and serves as the basis for an important analytical technique for measuring the specific surface area of materials [19]. This is crucial for MOFs as they have a large specific surface area with multiple micropores which can adsorb high amounts of pollutants between them [20]. In this research specific surface area, pore size distributions and $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured by using TriStar II 3020 (V1.03) with nitrogen physisorption at $7 7 . 3 \ \mathrm { K } .$ The degasification was made at $9 5 \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ for 2 hours with $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ .

# 2.3.7. UV-Vis & Fluorimeter spectroscopy

To characterise the light absorption response of both MOFs, full spectrum UV-visible absorption spectra from $1 9 0 \mathrm { n m }$ to $8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ were made [21]. In addition, fluorimeter spectroscopy (PL) was accomplished to evaluate the changes in an organic ligand to form structures with metal clusters and characterise the material. Usually, the reagents do not have any significant signal in PL, however, when MOFs are synthesised, the changes produced in the aromatic structures with nitrogen groups in the ligand linked to the metal ions can show a signal in the fluorimeter as those present quantic yield. The quantum yield $( \Phi )$ is proportional to the ratio of emitted photons to absorbed photons [22].

# 2.3.8. Point of zero charge

The Point of Zero Charge (PZC) or isoelectric point is defined as the pH value at which the surface is electrically neutral. Six data points at different pHs (1.5, 2, 4, 6, 9 and 12) were used to establish a linear relationship for determining the PZC, following the referenced protocol [23].

# 2.3.9. Metal content and elemental analysis

An acid digestion method, following Danish standard DS250, was carried out for the synthesised MOF, and the metal concentration in liquid samples was analysed using Inductively Coupled Plasma ICP (Optima 4300 DV Perkin Elmer Instruments).

Elemental analysis of the total content of carbon (including different fractions of total organic and inorganic carbon), hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulphur (C, H, N, S) present in a wide range of MOF samples was performed using the Fisons Carlo Erba EA1108 elemental microanalyser with a detection limit of $1 0 \mathrm { p p m }$ .

# 2.4.4. Reuse and stability

Different cycles of adsorption-regeneration were accomplished to evaluate the stability of the Zn-MIL53(Fe). After completing the degradation assays, Zn-MIL53(Fe) was collected by centrifugation and dried for $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ at $5 0 \mathrm { { ^ \circ C } }$ . Subsequently, the dried MOF was then reused in a new beaker containing fresh $0 . 1 \mathrm { { L } }$ of pollutant solution (as described in section 2.4.2). To assess metal leaching at the end of each reuse cycle, samples were collected and measured through ICP (section 2.3.9). The MOF structural stability at the beginning and end of each reuse cycle was determined by SEM (section 2.3.1). Those analytical methods provided insights into the potential leaching of metal ions from the MOF material, and corroborated the maintenance of the structure, respectively, thereby evaluating its stability and performance over multiple reuse cycles.

# 2.4.5. Pollutant determination

For the RhB tests, sample aliquots (1 mL) were centrifuged at $1 0 , 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { r p m }$ in a microcentrifuge (miniSpin, Eppendorf) for 1 min to separate any residual MOF powder that could interfere with the UV spectra measure (UV-Vis Spectrophotometer, Thermo Scientific Genesys 150). Subsequently, the samples were measured at $5 5 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ and the concentration was determined by the calibration curve.

For SMX and FLX tests, samples were filtered using a syringe and a $0 . 2 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ pore size, $1 3 \mathrm { m m }$ diameter hydrophilic filter. Isopropanol (4 isopropanol:6 sample) was used as a scavenger to deplete the radicals involved in the oxidation process. The injected volume was $1 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ while the mobile phase flow rate was $1 \mathrm { m L / m i n }$ .

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. MOFs characterisation

It is pertinent to emphasise that multiple iterations of MOF synthesis were conducted, involving modifications to synthesis conditions. However, only those instances resulting in higher crystallinity and specific surface area were selected for presentation in this research. The properties of the synthesised MOFs, including crystallinity and specific surface area, exhibit variability contingent upon factors such as the molar ratio of reactants, solvent composition, temperature, and duration of the synthesis reaction.

The two synthesised materials, MIL-53(Fe) and Zn-MIL-53(Fe), obtained through solvothermal synthesis underwent thorough verification by various characterisation techniques.

# 3.1.1. XRD, FT-IR, ICP-OES & Raman Spectroscopy

The crystal structure of the synthesised materials was verified through XRD analysis, as depicted in Fig. 1. The XRD spectra exhibited a pattern of continuous diffraction peaks in the low-angle region, characteristic of carboxylic materials, particularly MOFs resulting from the reaction with the ligand. The diffraction pattern closely resembled that of MIL-53(Fe). Additionally, the peaks observed at 2Theta (0) values of $9 . 1 3 ^ { \mathrm { { o } } }$ ,10.4, ${ \bf 1 3 . 9 ^ { \circ } }$ ,and $1 9 . 7 ^ { \circ }$ in the XRD pattern of this research synthesised MIL53(Fe) were consistent with a crystal structure similar to MIL-53(Fe) reported in the literature by Nguyen et al., [24]. Intensity changes and fewer novel peaks observed at 2Theta (θ) values of $1 4 . 1 6 ^ { \circ }$ , and $1 7 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ , in the similar $\mathrm { Z n }$ -MIL53(Fe) pattern also indicated that ${ \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 + }$ had been successfully introduced into the pyrrolic ring doping MIL53 (Fe) through the solvothermal method, similarly as seen in the research by Nguyen et al., [24]. Notably, the higher peaks at $1 3 . 9 ^ { \mathrm { { o } } }$ , $1 9 . 7 ^ { \circ }$ ,and $2 5 . 6 ^ { \circ }$ , along with new peaks at $1 7 . 5 1 ^ { \circ }$ , $1 9 . 5 4 ^ { \circ }$ and $2 8 . 6 3 ^ { \circ }$ suggested a different crystal configuration in the synthesis. This variation in crystalline structure was further supported by the SEM results (section 3.1.7), demonstrating changes in the crystal morphology induced by the introduction of Zn during the reaction. The XRD analysis thus provided comprehensive insights into the structural integrity and composition of the synthesised Zn-MIL53(Fe) material, as high intensity in the lower part of the 2Theta showcases mainly the crystal conformation of MOFs.

The metal concentration of the two synthesised MOFs was evaluated after acid digestion by ICP (Table 1S) to determine their composition. Similar iron content, around $1 0 \%$ , was determined in both materials which demonstrates that the iron present in the crystalline structure of both MOFs is approximately the same. The zinc content in the bimetallic MOF, Zn-MIL53(Fe), was around $3 \%$ This fact confirms that the molar ratio of iron and zinc clusters in the synthesised Zn-MIL53(Fe) material was 1:4. Additionally, using $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } B D C }$ in synthesis in the presence of DMF molecules with iron and zinc ions, there exist diverse carboxylate coordination groups acting as ligand that can be found as a result of the synthesis conditions, which affect to distinct coordination environments within octahedral geometry [2527]. In this instance, $\scriptstyle { \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 + }$ clusters are linked through bridging BDC ligands, giving rise to a zinc cluster- centred 2D MOF [28] surrounded by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ clusters in the external part of the bridging BDC ligand. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 3S(a,b) and 4 S in the supplementary materials, the $\mathrm { Z n }$ addition altered the way the metal clusters joined to the ligand, as $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } B D C }$ , when deprotonated, can act as a 3 types of different form ligand. In this case, it acts mainly in bidentate form, with the Fe clusters joining the outer part of the chain and Zn clusters creating a core of them with iron surrounding them, while the one without Zn got a different core and ligand form acting that gives different shapes to the material.

Furthermore, the crystallinity and elemental analyses findings indicate that zinc is coordinated with two oxygen atoms from two carboxylate groups in the middle of the ligand allowing iron to join in outer space to a single oxygen atom, which is partially responsible for the different geometry between both MOFs.

Fig. 2 represents the FT-IR patterns that illustrate the distinctive features of Zn-MIL53(Fe) and MIL53(Fe), showcasing their respective chemical compositions. The appearance of the 1575 and $1 3 7 \bar { 6 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ bands indicates that the $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } B D C }$ ligand has deprotonated, forming a carboxylate that has coordinated with metal clusters appearing bindings type Fe-O and ${ \mathrm { Z n } } { \mathrm { - } } 0$ , which cannot be directly observed in FT-IR, as both bands, about $4 2 0 { - } 5 6 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ [26,27,29] are overlapping. In addition, the Raman spectra which are complementary to the FT-IR spectra, are shown in Fig. 2S and allow to analyse the different transversal modes of transition of the elements in the MOF the ${ \mathrm { Z n - O } }$ and Fe-O structures can be confirmed as linked to the ligand. The ${ \mathrm { Z n - O } }$ bonds band is found at 170 and $2 1 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ at the Raman spectra, while the Fe-O bonds band is located at 263 and $3 3 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ . It confirms the previous results from FT-IR and the correct linking between BDC as a ligand with the iron clusters of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { Z n ^ { 2 + } }$ .Thus, Raman spectra shown in Fig. 2 can confirm the correct conformation and presence of both metal clusters in the material. The $1 7 0 0 { - } 1 6 1 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ zones are assigned to the $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = C }$ modes of the benzene ring; the 814 and $6 3 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ correspond to out-of-plane ring C-H bending vibrations; the C-C ring stretching was observed at the $1 1 3 8 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ peak, while ${ \bf v } _ { s }$ (OCO) and $\mathbf { v _ { a s } }$ (OCO) are found at the 1444 and $1 4 9 7 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ peaks. Conversely, Zn-MIL53(Fe) displays a higher reactivity with FLX, leading to greater leaching of both ions. This observation suggests a potential correlation between leaching and pollutant degradation, where higher leaching may enhance the AOP and subsequently improve contaminant degradation. It is noteworthy, however, that the $1 2 \%$ leaching of Zn-MIL53(Fe) is lower and does not surpass the leaching observed in other MIL53-MOFs used for pharmaceutical treatment, which leach about $1 6 \%$ of the total iron content per use [54], indicating the stability of the MOF in water.

The reusability tests conducted with the synthesised Zn-MIL53(Fe) material underscore its promising potential as a heterogeneous catalyst. This bimetallic MOF exhibits remarkable stability, demonstrating the ability to be reused for at least three consecutive cycles in the SRAOP.

SEM analyses of synthesised Zn-MIL53(Fe) after three cycles for the oxidation of RhB, SMX and FLX, depicted a similar initial structure as shown in Fig. 11, except for some minor cracks or missing parts on the external layer of the MOF owed to the oxidant. Moreover, other small particles were detected on the surface, due to the adsorption capacity of the MOF, particularly noticeable in the RhB case wherein the adsorption was higher. Despite this, no significant morphological or structural changes were found; as depicted in Fig. 11, the rhomboid structure that represents this Zn-MIL53(Fe) was still robust.

Importantly, the material retains its catalytic efficiency without substantial loss or the need for washing between cycles. The consistent behaviour observed across the three organic pollutants demonstrated in Fig. 12, further emphasises the reliability and versatility of the Zn-MIL53 (Fe) as a reusable catalyst in the SR-AOP, similar to other metal-doped MIL53(Fe) [34]. These findings contribute to the material appeal for practical applications in water treatment processes, highlighting its potential for sustained and effective degradation of diverse organic pollutants.

# 4. Conclusions

A novel Zn-MIL53(Fe) MOF has been successfully synthesised, proving to be a suitable and effective catalyst for a sulphate radical advanced oxidation process using PMS. This bimetallic MOF demonstrates enhanced performance in adsorption and catalyst properties compared to its monometallic precursor, MIL53(Fe). The Zn-MIL53(Fe) exhibits both structural stability and robust catalyst capabilities. When combined with the sulphate radical advanced oxidation process utilising PMS, it achieves complete degradation of micropollutants. Specifically, it achieves ${ > } 9 9 \%$ decolouration for organic pollutants like RhB and ${ > } 8 0$ $\%$ degradation for pharmaceuticals such as FLX and SMX, within a short period. Compared to its precursor, the novel Zn-MIL53(Fe) improves 23 $\%$ the adsorption capacity and $4 1 \ \%$ the catalyst capacity overall micropollutants.

Importantly, the Zn-MIL53(Fe) displays a higher affinity for degrading cationic micropollutants such as FLX and RhB as they are found in monomeric states which facilitates the adsorption and contact with the MOF surface compared to the zwitterionic state or anionic micropollutants as SMX at work conditions pH of 2.3 naturally lowered by the MOF.

Additionally, it proves to be reusable, maintaining efficiency for at least three complete cycles without exceeding a $1 0 \ \%$ reduction in performance. This resilience is attributed to the minimal leaching of metals into the medium, emphasising the stability and potential applicability of the synthesised Zn-MIL53(Fe) as a catalyst for advanced oxidation processes in water treatment.

Finally, the novel synthesised Zn-MIL53(Fe) MOF improved the stability, reuse, and catalyst activity from the similar bimetallic reported MOFs Mn-MIL53(Fe) [34] and ZnFe-MOF [31] and is proposed as a suitable long-lasting catalyst for activate a PMS system to degrade micropollutants.
