# a b s t r a c t

A versatile and scalable preparation of hierarchically porous and highly active catalysts from a lignosulfonate biopolymer was proposed. The materials were systematically evaluated for catalytic oxidation of metformin, sulfamethoxazole, and phenol and reductive hydrogenation of 4-nitrophenol and 1- nitronaphthalene as model pharmaceutical and industrial pollutants. Temperature, background matrix, substrate, catalyst and chemical loading govern the conversion rates differently. Complete phenol and sulfamethoxazole removals were achieved in $9 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ at very low dosage of Oxone $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ $( 0 . 3 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ and catalyst $( 0 . 0 5 ~ \mathrm { g / L } )$ while mineralizing more than $8 0 \%$ (phenol and sulfamethoxazole) and $3 6 \%$ (metformin). Humic acid, gallic acid, sodium azide, chloride and bicarbonate ions at environmentally relevant levels possessed minor inhibitory effects on sulfamethoxazole degradation. Results from EPR spintrapping, radical quenching, and $\mathrm { D } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ exchange experiments support a dual-mode activation mechanism of Oxone®. In the catalytic reduction of the nitro compounds, excellent turnover frequencies of up to $3 . 7 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } / \mathrm { { m i n } }$ for 4-nitrophenol and $2 . 0 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } / \mathrm { { m i n } }$ for 4-nitronaphthalene were attained, outperforming the capacity of many previously reported catalysts. Furthermore, various solvent exchange studies reveal the significant role of structured surficial moieties in a “water assisted” catalytic mechanism and reduction pathway. The N, S co-doped samples exhibited superior performance, synergistically attributable to the active heteroatoms and $\mathrm { C o { - } N _ { x } / S _ { y } { - } C }$ clusters on porous $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ -conjugated framework. For both processes, the catalysts maintained their magnetic responsiveness, high activity and stability profile for at least five cycles, highlighting the practical applicability of this benign valorization route and the envisaged straightforward heterogeneous applications.

# 1. Introduction

Pharmaceutical and chemical production is imperative to maintaining a healthy world. Environmental contaminants such as drug precursor chemicals and pharmaceutical active compounds, however, pose risks to humans and ecological receptors (Kümmerer et al., 2019). The increasing discharges of anthropogenic pollutants into natural aquatic habitats represent major challenges to environmental remediation technologies (Grant et al., 2012; Shannon et al., 2008). These often bio-persistent and hazardous micropollutants are hardly mineralized into inert products (Klavarioti et al., 2009; von Gunten, 2018; Wilkinson et al., 2017).

Benign and effective redox organic degradations over carbonaceous catalysts have recently emerged as promising approaches to circumvent the described issues (Das et al., 2017; Duan et al., 2018b; Yang et al., 2018). Catalytic conversions activated by carbons (Chen et al., 2018; Daems et al., 2018; Gong et al., 2018; Kong et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016; N. Wang et al., 2018; Xi et al., 2018) or metal-carbon hybrids (Huang et al., 2018,

2014; Jagadeesh et al., 2017; Pi et al., 2018; Shao et al., 2017; Tian et al., 2018; L. Wang et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2017) have been widely demonstrated. Solid carbons are greener options considering the wide precursor alternatives and their physicochemical configurations (Antonietti et al., 2018; Antonietti and Oschatz, 2018). However, the main research and development challenge remains largely in finding sustainable or naturally occurring building blocks, simplifying synthesis procedures and increasing product yields (Samaddar et al., 2018).

Lignosulfonates (LS) are by-products of the sulfite pulping processes of a sustainable biopolymer: lignin. Valorizing LS into active solids would be a sensible approach towards cleaner production. To date, several LS-based value-added products have been realized (Konwar et al., 2018; Palazzolo et al., 2018; Pang et al., 2018, 2017; Zhang et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2019). Pyrolyzing Co/Mn-lignin with dicyandiamide gave $\mathrm { C o - M n / N } \varpi { \cal C }$ which is active catalyst for the aerobic oxidation of 5- hydroxymethylfurfural to 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (Zhou et al., 2019). In other work, glycerol was acetalized to bio-additives over metal-free and reusable LS-derived acidic carbons (Konwar et al., 2018). Therefore, the production of LS-based functional materials for heterogeneous advanced oxidation and reductive hydrogenation processes is feasible and might has promising industrial viability. However, the large scale practical application of these catalysts is still challenged by the potential negative environmental impacts of high leaching metal species and releasing tubular nanomaterials (Long et al., 2018; Minh et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2019). In this regard, tailoring the catalyst composition and morphology towards metal-less and non-noble composite-based remediation processes can be an alternative engineering option.

Herein, we report a direct and scalable carbonization of a Co(II)- LS coordination complex into Co-embedded N,S-self-doped carbons. The hierarchical porous microstructures comprised of holey layered graphitic stacks of large surface area and rich porosity. The $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ -dominated aromatic LS would enhance the conjugated bonding and diverged its resistance to oxidation and combust (Antonietti and Oschatz, 2018; Pang et al., 2018). The alkaline (Na, Ca) and heteroatoms (N, S, O) in the precursors would activate and dope the carbon lattices into reactive graphitized motifs, promoting their redox chemistry (Pang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2015). The coordination of LS with cobalt ions played a critical role in molecular organization and subsequent catalytic pyrolysis (Guo et al., 2014; Long et al., 2018). The materials are explored as activators for various peroxymonosulfate- and borohydridemediated redox processes. Metformin, sulfamethoxazole, phenol, 4-nitrophenol (4-Nip), and 1-nitronaphthalene (1-Nin) were selected as model compounds. The influence of synthesis conditions and operating parameters were evaluated in detail. The magnetic and durable composites allowed facile recovery and at multicycles use. Reaction pathways were proposed with evidences from various spin trapping, scavenging, solvent exchange, and isotope effect studies.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Material preparation and characterization

All the chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Typically, LS (5 g) and cobalt nitrate hexahydrate $( 0 . 2 4 7 ~ \mathrm { g } ,$ , equivalent $_ { 0 . 0 5 \mathrm { ~ g ~ } }$ Co) were dissolved in deionized water $\mathrm { 3 0 ~ m L } ,$ , followed by drying at $1 0 5 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C ( 2 \mathrm { h } )$ and pyrolyzing at 700, 800, or $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } C$ (2 h) under $\Nu _ { 2 }$ flow. The product was incubated in 1 M HCl $( 1 2 \mathrm { h } )$ then washed in ethanol (two times) and deionized water (three times) followed by drying in oven. The sample was labelled LSCo-X where X was the pyrolysis temperature. To explore the roles of heteroatoms, urea $( 0 . 1 \mathrm { g } )$ and thiourea $( 0 . 1 3 \mathrm { g } )$ was added to give NLSCo-X and SLSCoX, respectively. LSCo-X-1h and LSCo-X-3h were prepared with a corresponding pyrolysis holding time. The product yields are given in Table S1. For comparative tests, Zero-valent iron (ZVI, 325 mesh, Sigma-Aldrich) and $\mathsf { F e } _ { 3 } \mathsf { O } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles were also prepared (Tan et al., 2017).

The thermolytic behaviors were recorded on a PerkinElmer TGQ500 system with a heating rate of $5 ~ { \ ' C / \mathrm { m i n } }$ from 25 to $8 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under $\Nu _ { 2 }$ . The morphology was observed with a TEM (Hitachi 7700) and FE-SEM (Hitachi SU3500) with an EDS detector. The surface chemistry was inspected with a Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometry (FT-IR) and ESCALAB 250 X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Diffraction patterns were collected using PANalytical equipment. Raman spectroscopy was studied using a Horiba Jobin Yvon, Labram HR microscope equipped with a 513.4 nm laser. The textural properties were evaluated via $\Nu _ { 2 }$ sorption using a Micromeritics Tristar II Plus. The elemental composition (C, H, N, O, S) was determined by combustive analysis (Thermo Scientific Flash 2000). The Co content was quantified using an ICP-AES (Thermo Scientific iCAP 6000).

# 2.2. Catalytic experiment

Unless otherwise specified, the oxidative degradations were performed in a $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ aqueous solution of model compounds (30 $\mathrm { m g / L } ,$ no buffering) with catalyst (1 mg) and PMS (Oxone, $0 . 6 5 \mathrm { m M }$ ) at $2 5 \pm 2 ^ { \circ } { \mathsf { C } } .$ Periodically, $1 . 0 \ \mathrm { m L }$ filtered $( 0 . 2 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } )$ aliquot was sampled into a vial containing $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { \ m L }$ methanol for radical quenching. The organic concentrations were quantified using an HPLC (Shimadzu) and Kinetex $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ C18 column $5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ , $1 5 0 \times 4 . 6 )$ . The apparent rate constants were estimated by fitting kinetics data over a pseudo-first-order model.

The effects of operating conditions were studied by varying the corresponding parameter. Synthetic sewage was prepared and spiked with inorganic ions, humic- and gallic acid when needed. To avoid radical scavenging effect, pH buffer was not used. Nitrate was monitored using an ion chromatographer (Shimadzu) with a Shodex IC SI-50 4E column $5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ , $2 5 0 \times 4 . 0 \dot { \phantom { 0 } }$ ). Oxone was monitored colorimetrically. The extent of mineralization was evaluated as total organic content using a TOC analyzer (Shimadzu, $\mathrm { T O C - V _ { C P H / N } } .$ ). The isotope effect was studied by replacing deionized water by $\mathsf { D } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ . For reusability tests, spent catalysts were magnetically collected, washed with deionized water, ethanol and dried. Spin trapping tests were carried out using an EPR spectrometer (ADANI, CMS8400) with DMPO and TEMP as trappers. Potential reactive oxidizers were investigated via ethanol and sodium azide scavenging.

Reductive hydrogenation was performed in glass reactor containing nitro compounds $( 0 . 0 0 1 5 { - } 1 0 ~ \mathrm { m m o l } )$ , catalyst $\left( 1 { - } 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m g } \right)$ , and $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 } \left( 0 . 0 3 { - } 2 0 0 \mathrm { m m o l } \right)$ at room temperature. Aliquots $( 5 0 \mu \mathrm { L } )$ were removed at various times, diluted if required, and monitored spectrophotometrically. The solvent effects were tested using ethanol, methanol, $\mathsf { D } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ , and their mixtures. Spent carbons were also isolated using a magnet and were ready for reuse.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Catalyst characterization

The valorization process is illustrated in Scheme 1 showing ${ \mathsf { C o } } ( \mathrm { I I } )$ -LS coordination complex of lignosulfonate sodium and ${ \mathsf { C o } } ( \mathrm { I I } )$ $( 1 ~ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ underwent carbonization, followed by acid etching to give final $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ -hybridized carbons decorated with graphitic layersencapsulated cobalt species. Having different functional groups on flexible structural motifs, LS can make strong linkages with cobalt as evidenced by UV/Vis absorption spectrophotometry, FT-IR and XPS. The shifted and suppressed light absorbance of pristine LS compared to that of precursor mixtures suggests cobalt chelation and possible cross-linked organization with urea/thiourea (Fig. S1). The FT-IR spectra of the Co(II)-LS precursor showed peaks of varied intensities when compared with pristine LS, indicating that the functional groups were coordinated (Fig. S2). The O1s envelop (Fig. S3) indicated the characteristic HOeC peak $( 5 2 9 . 3 3 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ of lignosulfonate shifted to higher binding energies of 531.47 (Co(II)-LS precursor) and 531.50 eV (LSCo7), suggesting a LS-cobalt coordination. The self-contained sulfur, carbohydrates, and sodium (Table S2) would favor porosity development upon carbonization (Palazzolo et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2015). Thermal-gravimetric profile (Figs. 1a and S4) of the physically blended precursor had four weight losses: evaporation of moisture and low volatile impurities followed by the removal of $- S O _ { 3 } \mathrm { H }$ groups and unstable moieties (approx. $3 0 \%$ ); oxidation of non-graphitic carbon (approx. $5 5 \%$ loss) and total oxidation of crystalline domains (approx. $8 5 \%$ at $6 5 0 { - } 8 2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ (Eqs. S(1)e(6)) (Konwar et al., 2018; Pang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018). In contrast, derived samples showed one maximum loss around $5 1 5 { - } 5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C$ , echoing a catalyzed graphitization and transformation of carbonaceous matter. Half the initial mass was lost at $3 4 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for neat LS but at $5 2 0 { - } 5 5 2 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in other derivatives, suggesting improved chemical configurations.

Shown in Figs. 1b and S5 are the representative Raman spectra of the as-prepared samples. The observed defect-derived diamondoid D-band $( \sim 1 3 5 5 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ ) and carbonaceous graphitic G-band $( \sim 1 5 9 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 } .$ ) had intensities referable to the magnitudes of defect and $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ domain, respectively (Chen et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2016). Higher intensity ratios $\mathrm { ( I _ { D } / I _ { G } ) }$ in LS7, LSCo7, NLSCo7 and SLSCo7 indicate disorder and defectiveness. The shifted G-band between LSCo7, NLSCo7 and SLSCo7 suggests heteroatom allocation in carbon lattices (Chen et al., 2018; Fei et al., 2015). Narrower D-bands in NLSCo8 and lowest $\mathrm { { I _ { D } / I _ { G } } }$ (0.69) for NLSCo9 suggest successful annealing of the unstable defects into a highly graphited framework. Structural defects (edging, vacancies, dangling bonds) left on the stack after releasing thermal susceptible moieties probably contain structural $\mathsf { N } / \mathsf { S }$ bound only to carbon, which are critically active (Gong et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2017). Functional moieties and defects on the discrete amorphous or graphitic carbon, however, play differently in the catalytic activation pathway (Li et al., 2016; Shao et al., 2018; Yun et al., 2018).

Diffraction analysis (Figs. 1c and S6) showed a broad hum at 20- $3 2 ^ { \circ }$ for neat LS but a sharp peak at $4 8 \substack { - 5 2 ^ { \circ } }$ was ascribable to other samples. The cobalt-free carbons had more disorder than condensed polyaromatic fraction. In cobalt-coordinated derivatives, planes (002), (010) and (011) of graphite 2H (ICSD#3e1170) and planes (111), (002) and (022) of cubic Co- $\mathcal { A }$ (ICSD#4e4989) are detectable. Cobalt-boosted graphitization elevated peaks $6 3 . 6 ^ { \circ }$ and $9 3 . 3 ^ { \circ }$ in all carbons. Ascending pyrolytic temperature intensified the peaks, implying thermally-induced annealing and catalyzed $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 } / \mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ hybridization (Chen et al., 2018; Shao et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2018), essentially resulting in a more organized architecture.

Nitrogen sorptions indicated well-developed porosities in the composites, highlighting the capacity to enhance catalytic efficacy compared to the neat LS-derived microbeads. The developed pore spaces facilitate mass transport and the formation of surface-bound intermediates (Palazzolo et al., 2018; Pang et al., 2017). Similarly, hierarchical porosity and strong sequestering would expand the residence times of reactants at the interface, enabling better collision with the active centers in their vicinity (Huang et al., 2014; Jagadeesh et al., 2017). Fig. 1d depicts typical Type IV isotherms; a summary of textural properties is given in Table 1. Without cobalt, a one-hour pyrolysis produced no porosity $( 5 - 1 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ , whilst prolonging process gave low yields. After two-hour carbonization, the cobalt-free carbons developed very modest microporosity $\mathrm { \Delta } S _ { \mathrm { B E T } }$ $9 2 { - } 1 9 2 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } \rangle$ . Sodium species created mesopores and macro-voids (Pang et al., 2018, 2017) while additional dopants narrowed the hysteresis loop of LSCo7 (Figs. S7 and 8). In all series, surface areas reached maximum at $8 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ but declined significantly for those prepared at $9 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathsf C ,$ , probably due to structural collapse during catalytic graphitization- and alkaline metal-induced reactions (Pang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2018). The SLSCo samples had enlarged mesopores, likely because thiourea introduced more heterogeneity and promoted greater cross-linking. Owing to the excellent complexation of LS, cobalt could be dispersed homogeneously and formed the exfoliating centers. Then together with urea and thiourea, which acted as extra porogents, further improved the textural and chemical compositions. Broad pore size distribution was observed with maxima centers at micropores, followed by mesopore domains and expansive macropores. Annealing at $8 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ induced voluminous mesopores ${ < } 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , with the greatest extents by introducing thiourea (Fig. S9). These features are very important not only to sequestering and mobilizing species but also to the activation routes, which are rendered by the interactions mostly at surficial active sites and interfaces (Antonietti et al., 2018; Duan et al., 2018b). Overall, the observed rich hierarchical pore spaces are accorded with their thermal stability, which can stimulate good oxidation resistance.

Thus, exogenous doping enriched the frameworks with ${ \sf C o - N } _ { \mathrm { X } } / { \sf S } _ { \mathrm { y } }$ and functional moieties. As reported, intercalating p-block elements (N, S) could enhance electron transfer, atomic charge and spin density redistribution at the adjacent carbon atoms, and hence facilitate catalysis (Antonietti et al., 2018; Duan et al., 2018b; Kong et al., 2013; Yun et al., 2018).

Finally, the SEM and EDX analyses revealed that the complexderived catalysts are morphologically and chemically distinct from the neat LS microbeads (Fig. 3). NLSCo7 comprised holey layered sheet-like carbons, conductively linking catalytic sites and $\mathrm { C o { - } N _ { x } / S _ { y } { - } C }$ centers with reactants. EDX mapping indicated a homogeneous distribution of atoms, confirming cobalt-LS association under well-organized graphitic layers. Obviously, low Co/LS mass ratio (1 wt%) was sufficient to yield this lamellar architecture without any morphological impurities (Long et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2019). TEM imaging showed clear wrapping of cobalt by carbon layers (Fig. S10), locating Co- $\mathrm { \partial \cdot N _ { x } } / \mathrm { S _ { y } } { - C }$ species all over the stacks. As a result, not only may those cobalt-based active sites be effectively and chemically recycled, but a large part, if not all, of the catalyst samples may facilely be recovered using a magnetic field.

In summary, (N, S)-self- or exogenously-doped catalysts with tunable textural and chemical compositions were facilely prepared. The influences of these modulated intrinsic properties on the redox conversions of organic contaminants will be discussed below.

# 3.2. Catalytic oxidative degradation

# 3.3.1. Thermal agitation

Oxone can be thermally activated and the water temperature may also affects non-target reactions, making it important to complete abatement. Thermal agitation under high PMS dose $( 0 . 6 5 { - } 1 . 6 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ ) was applied. Positive influences were observed: NLSCo7 helped complete phenol decay ( $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $3 5 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ ) and $9 8 \%$ o f

SFM ( $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } C$ ) with $0 . 6 5 \ \mathrm { m M }$ PMS. From the Arrhenius correlation, the estimated activation energy for SFM, MET and phenol over NLSCo7 was \~18.58, 30.19 and $3 7 . 0 4 ~ \mathrm { k J / m o l }$ , respectively (Fig. S11d). Thus, beside free radical and substrate-catalyst interactions, surface-bound PMS complexes are more significant over NLSCo7 than hydrophobic NLSCo8 and NLSCo9.

# 3.3.2. Initial pH

Solution pH is a factor in determining the speciation of reactants and catalyst functionalities (Duan et al., 2018a; Oh et al., 2016; Wacławek et al., 2017). Particularly for SFM, the temporal degradation and leaching profiles (Fig. S11e) show that low initial alkaline condition favors fast initial kinetics, likely due to the enhanced evolution of free reactive species around the negatively charged pharmaceutical molecules. The degradation rate of SFM at $\mathsf { p H } 2$ slightly higher than that obtained at initial $\mathsf { p H } = 7$ whereas the detected concentration of $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { C } } \mathbf { \boldsymbol { o } } 2 +$ ions was double, eliminating the significant contribution of homogeneous catalysis (Oh et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2018). Singlet oxygen and superoxide radical, which are less interfered by water, can offer a stronger attack to electron-rich substrates and can be the principle oxidizers at high alkalinity (Wacławek et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2014). This tendency happened at $\mathsf { p H 9 }$ where Co species tend to remain stable. Comparing to near neutral $\mathsf { p H 7 } ,$ the conversion increased at $\mathsf { p H } 2$ , where anionic SFM may have stronger affinity to the positively charged NLSCo8. A 90- min treatment at $\mathsf { p H } 2$ over NLSCo8 degraded $9 5 \%$ of SFM and released $1 5 4 \mu \mathrm { g C o / L }$ . The evolution of acidic entities generated from the hydrolysis of PMS and by-products had a significant influence on the metal release (Table S7). This observation, together with the XPS and TEM data, support the hypothesis that both Co0 and Co$\mathrm { N x } / \mathrm { S y - C }$ sites were buried deeply under graphitic layers, favoring low leaching and negligible homogeneous $^ { \mathbf { C 0 2 + } }$ -mediated PMS activation (Wacławek et al., 2017).

# 3.3.3. Oxone dosage

Nonlinear behavior, regarding oxone $( 0 . 3 2 5 { - } 3 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ ) over Sseries catalysts (Fig. 4c), mirrored the self-scavenging effect of PMS. Fig. S11f illustrates the impact of PMS dose in the presence of ${ \mathsf { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ as a potential inhibitor. Low catalyst $\mathrm { ( 1 0 ~ m g / L ) }$ and high azide loading $1 9 . 5 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ were used to avoid mass transfer limitation and ineffective radical quenching. At low oxidant dose, either PMS loss through reaction with azide or hampered singlet oxygenation quenched by azide decreased $k _ { \tt a p p }$ . At a higher dose, rate constants improved in the first $1 5 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ then came tailings. A dose of $1 . 3 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ induced complete SFM removal in 5 min and $6 9 \%$ of mineralization, with accumulated TPs (Fig. S13a). Thus, in allowing sufficient reaction time, the NLSCo8/PMS process at a very low oxidant dose $( 0 . 3 2 5 \mathrm { m M } )$ ) could remove at least $8 0 \%$ of SFM $\mathrm { \Delta C _ { 0 } } = 3 0 \mathrm { m g / L ) }$ in the presence of excessive azide.

Ethanol and ${ \mathsf { N a N } } _ { 3 }$ retarded the degradations (Fig. $4 \mathrm { g } \mathrm { . }$ ) where MET removal was strongly hampered. Therefore, the electron states and spin density can be redistributed and spill all over the carbon surface, triggering dual-mode activation mechanism. All these action modes synergistically contributed to the observed catalytic potency (Eqs. (1)e(4)).

The effect of solvent on 4-Nip conversion rate decreases in the following order: $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } \gg \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } + \mathrm { M e O H } > \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } + \mathrm { E t O H } > \mathrm { D } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } > \mathrm { D } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ $+ \ \mathrm { M e O H } > \mathrm { D } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } \ + \ \mathrm { E t O H } \gg \ \mathrm { M e O H } > \ \mathrm { E t O H } .$ Replacing $5 0 \%$ of the alcohols with $\mathsf { D } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ allows hydrogenations but at much slower kinetics compared to solely in $\mathsf { D } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ or $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } .$ . Absolute alcohols $( 1 0 0 \% \mathrm { v } /$ v, water content $\leq 0 . 0 3 \%$ gave negligible yields $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ , ${ \mathrm { N a B H } } _ { 4 } / 4 \cdot$ - $\mathsf { N i p } = 1 0 0$ equiv.). $\mathsf { D } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ hydrolyzes $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ but $^ 1 \mathrm { H }$ or $^ 2 \mathrm { H }$ exchange is low at room temperature (Girardot and Parry, 2005; Jolly and Mesmer, 1961). Cobalt ions facilitated the evolution of ${ \sf D } _ { 2 }$ but not HD (Davis et al., 1964). Therefore, $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ was likely the hydrogen donor, whilst water was critical in the hydrolysis of $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ into reactive hydrogen (Scheme 2, step I). The fact that $_ { \mathrm { D } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ hydrolyzed only $1 0 { - } 2 5 \%$ of hydroborate (Davis et al., 1964) may explain the slower amination rates in ${ \sf D } _ { 2 } { \sf O } .$ .

Solvent exchanges by alcohols suggested water probably formed surface complexes surrounding the positively charged carbon atoms which can bind strongly to 4-Nip via the O atom of the hydroxyl group (Kong et al., 2013). These clusters likely favor the hydrogen transformation to nitro groups. The hetero dopants also induced surface polarization on the carbon sheets whereby these N, S, O-contained moieties can function as binding sites for nitro group (Song et al., 2018). Moreover, the flat orientation of the aromatic ring of substrates, especially in the case of 1-Nin, on graphitic surfaces via $\pi \cdot \pi$ interaction would be obviously dominant. The positively charged sites likely act as hydride acceptor sites, mediating electron flows and active hydrogen from and to adsorbed substrates. Due to the charge transfer effect, water quickly hydrolyzed $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ to form free molecular hydrogen. Adsorbed water at the carbon can also stabilize the negatively charged nitro compounds and promote charge-transfer interactions. This socalled “water assisted” activation pathway has been proposed (L. Wang et al., 2018). Finally, the layered graphitic architecture may also boost $_ \mathrm { H - H }$ bond cleavage via “frustrated Lewis acid-base pairs” (Antonietti et al., 2018), facilitating the transfer of active hydrogen to nitro groups on all three molecular alignments, giving 4-Aminophenol or 1-Naphthylamine the only product.

# 4. Conclusion

A series of N,S-doped hierarchical porous catalysts was prepared from a Co(II)-lignosulfonate. Cobalt cross-linked the biopolymer for an enhanced catalytic graphitization, yielding catalysts with large pore spaces and abundant active sites. The materials outperformed conventional activators in both peroxymonosulfate-based oxidative degradation and borohydride-mediated reductive amination of several pollutants. Effective mineralization of pharmaceutical contaminants and total hydrogenation of nitro compounds were demonstrated. The heteroatom-enriched sites and C $0 { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { N } _ { \mathrm { x } } / \mathsf { S } _ { \mathrm { y } } { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { C }$ interfaces on the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ -conjugated networks responsible for the enhanced catalysis. Increasing reaction temperature, catalyst loading and oxidant dose accelerated oxidative removal whilst inorganic anions (bicarbonate and chloride) and organic matter (humic and gallic acid) had minor inhibitory effects. Complete aminations of nitrophenol and nitronaphthalene were achieved at room temperature. EPR, scavenging and solvent isotope effect studies revealed a boosted evolution of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ , $\cdot { O H }$ , and $^ 1 O _ { 2 }$ via dualmode PMS activation. Solvent exchanges suggested water are essential to $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ hydrolysis and catalytic transfer of active hydrogen. Encouraging stability behavior with minor decay in activity, porosity and magnetic features was observed. This versatile valorization of lignosulfonate may facilitate further design and application of inexpensive functional materials for energy and environmental engineering.
