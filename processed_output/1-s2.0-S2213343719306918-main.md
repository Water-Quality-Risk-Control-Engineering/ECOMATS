Hydrocarbon contaminated sites are a critical issue. Soil is a complex substrate and its heterogeneity makes soil remediation a difficult task. Studies involving in situ chemical oxidation focusing on soil remediation are scarce in literature. Heterogeneous catalysts for oxidation process can be supported in clays. In this work, different claybased catalysts were synthesized. Concentrations of sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ferrous sulfate $\mathrm { ( F e S O _ { 4 } ) }$ , and ferric sulfate $\mathrm { ( F e } _ { 3 } ( \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 } )$ were varied in accordance with a $2 ^ { 3 }$ experimental design. Modified clay catalysts (MCC) were applied in oxidation assays of phenanthrene (PHE) contaminated soil to evaluate the activation of two oxidants: sodium persulfate (SP) and hydrogen peroxide (HP). The experimental design response was the PHE removal. Most of the catalysts proved to be efficient in activating both oxidants and achieved around $8 0 ~ \%$ of PHE removal. Control assays showed that around $2 0 ~ \%$ of PHE can be naturally transferred from soil to the liquid phase. PHE removal did not occur during oxidation with raw clay (RC). The highest PHE removal was obtained using MCC 16 with HP, reaching $8 3 ~ \%$ . XRF analysis of MCC 16 indicated an increase of $1 6 0 ~ \%$ in iron content and the Mössbauer spectroscopy showed higher values of relative $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ doublet area for MCC 16 than for RC. BET surface area measurements and scanning electron microscopy analysis suggested the impregnation of iron minerals on the MCC 16 surface. The catalyst proposed in this study can be applied in both in situ or ex situ remediation circumstances, reducing the environmental effects of organic contaminants.

# 1. Introduction

Soil remediation is a significant issue due to the complexity of this substrate. Human activities have enhanced the contamination of large areas of soil by different pollutants [1,2]. Release of hydrocarbons to the environment can occur during oil exploration, transport, and storage activities. Accidental leaks in underground tanks and pipelines cause severe pollution since the contaminants can reach groundwater, rivers, and oceans, with harmful effects for living organisms [3]. In addition, most of these contaminants are recalcitrant and persistent, being hardly removed by conventional treatment methods [2].

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) are the most important class of organic contaminants, which consist of hydrocarbons with two or more fused aromatic rings. They are generated from incomplete combustion and processing of fossil fuels, commonly related to petroleum, gas, and wood industries [2]. Approximately 130 types of PAH have already been identified by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA). This agency classified 16 PAHs as priority pollutants considering its significant toxicity, mutagenicity, carcinogenicity, and reproductive ability effects [4–7]. In addition, PAH have complex molecular structure, tend to adsorb on solid particles, beside presenting high hydrophobicity and low solubility in water, which instigates accumulation processes in the soil. Thus, PAH belong to the hazardous micropollutants group and are considered ubiquitous and highly resistant to degradation process [2,8,9].

The European Environment Agency (EEA) reports the presence of almost 250,000 contaminated sites throughout Europe, where $1 3 . 3 \%$ of the contamination is related to the PAH group [10]. According to the National Database of Contaminated Areas created by the Brazilian National Environmental Council (CONAMA), only São Paulo, Minas

Gerais, and Rio de Janeiro, among 27 Brazilian states, have management of contaminated areas. Based on contaminated and rehabilitated areas reports, 6110 areas were registered in Sao Paulo [11], 662 areas in Minas Gerais [12], and 328 areas in Rio de Janeiro [13]. The largest proportion by hydrocarbons contamination is related to fuel stations.

Several technologies have been studied in order to remediate PAHcontaminated areas, such as solvent extraction, thermal techniques, biological and electrokinetic remediation, phytoremediation, photocatalytic degradation, and chemical oxidation [14–16]. Advanced Oxidation Process (AOP) is a promising and efficient technique to remove organic pollutants from contaminated areas through chemical reactions involving radicals with high oxidative power [17–20]. In Situ Chemical Oxidation (ISCO) has emerged as an effective remediation technology among the AOP. ISCO is accomplished by injecting oxidants into contaminated site in order to convert pollutants into less harmful chemical compounds, instead of merely being transferred to a different phase [17,21–23]. However, ISCO efficiency depends on site characteristics, oxidant chosen, and pollutant properties [6,8,24].

The most commonly applied oxidants to soil and contaminated groundwater remediation are hydrogen peroxide, potassium or sodium permanganate, sodium persulfate, and ozone [19]. Each oxidant has its advantages and limitations. The oxidation process mostly used and studied is Fenton, when hydrogen peroxide is activated by iron ions as catalyst and generates hydroxyl radicals $( \mathrm { E } ^ { \circ } = 2 . 8 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ , which are highly reactive and can degrade organic compounds at high reaction rates [1,7,25]. Among the oxidants used in ISCO, persulfate has attracted special attention due to its significant characteristics. Persulfate anions, formed by the dissociation of persulfate salts in water, are able to spread at long distances and persist for weeks in subsurface [8,26]. Furthermore, persulfate activation generates sulfate radicals $( S O _ { 4 } { \bullet } ^ { - } )$ that are strong oxidants $( \mathrm { E } ^ { \circ } = 2 . 6 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ with longer lifetime, more stable and selective compared to hydroxyl radicals (HO•) [7,20,27,28]. $S O _ { 4 } { \bullet } ^ { - }$ reacts preferably with organic pollutants by electron-transfer reactions [20].

Conventional activation methods involving hydrogen peroxide and persulfate reagents require low initial pH, which makes the process disadvantageous due to the environmental impact [19,29]. Heterogeneous catalysis is an alternative to avoid medium acidification, since it can be applied in neutral conditions [1]. In addition, heterogeneous catalysts can be recovered and reused after the reaction depending on the in situ remediation technique [30]. In heterogeneous catalytic processes by iron oxyhydroxides, iron is stabilized within the catalyst porous structure and can effectively produce the radicals without pH adjustment [31]. Accordingly, iron oxides have been used in oxidation processes, resulting in an advantageous technology when compared to traditional oxidant activation methods. For petroleum hydrocarbons and other organic pollutants degradation through chemical oxidation, the catalyzed reaction by the mixture $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ oxides was found to be more effective in comparison with the reactions catalyzed only by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ oxides [26,32–36].

The possible reaction mechanisms involved in chemical oxidation processes with hydrogen peroxide and persulfate activated by ironbased heterogeneous catalyst are shown in Table 1. The mechanism of heterogeneous reactions has been investigated by researchers and are still under discussion [19,26,37–41]. The mechanisms already reported involve free radicals formed from surface iron species and radical formation by iron ions leached in the solution, i.e., homogeneous pathway [38]. For Fenton reactions, Adityosulindro, Julcour, and Barthe [37] and Pham et al. [38] reported a third mechanism with non-radical involving high valence iron species on the catalyst surface. The decomposition of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and $S _ { 2 } { \mathrm O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ into circumneutral pH values is a process that may involve surface iron reactions, since iron ions only remain dissolved under acidic conditions [38].

Clays are widely used in industry due to their high specific area, cation exchange capacity, and natural abundance, particularly in Brazil. Moreover, they are a low-cost material, eco-friendly, and present reactive feature. According to the literature, clay minerals are an efficient support for heterogeneous catalysts used in oxidation reactions, mostly applied in contaminated groundwater treatment [31,41,43–46]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no evaluation of heterogeneous oxidation processes with clay-based catalysts is so far available for PAH-contaminated soil remediation. Catalysts based on natural iron may be used in oxidation processes, but sometimes iron is not enough or is not accessible. Therefore, another approach is the iron active species incorporation, employing clay as a catalyst support [41,44]. Additionally, these solid catalysts with iron oxyhydroxides produce high recalcitrant pollutant degradation rates [31].

This work aimed to synthesize a clay-based catalyst capable of activating sodium persulfate (SP) and hydrogen peroxide (HP) oxidants for chemical oxidation of PAH contaminated soil. Thus, the design of experiments (DOE) tool was applied to evaluate the best catalyst synthesis condition, since this technique allows a multivariable analysis verifying the effects between the variables and the influences of the variables on the response. The produced catalysts, using iron ions and sodium hydroxide into smectite as support, were applied to heterogeneous oxidation processes to evaluate the activation efficiency of each catalyst. Phenanthrene (PHE) pollutant was chosen as a representative of the PAH group. Artificially contaminated soil with PHE was used, since preliminary studies involving synthetic samples are important to provide an accurate process evaluation.

# 2. Materials and methods

This section describes the synthesis procedure of the catalysts, their characterization and, finally, the application in chemical oxidation

experiments of phenanthrene contaminated soil.

# 2.1. Materials

The natural soil used in this study was collected from $3 0 \mathrm { c m }$ below the ground surface in Natal city, northeast of Brazil. Bentonite clay was supplied from Bentonisa - Bentonita do Nordeste S.A. The contaminant model was phenanthrene $9 8 \ \%$ , Fluka Analytical). Sodium persulfate $( \geq 9 8 ~ \%$ , Sigma-Aldrich) and hydrogen peroxide $( 3 0 ~ \% ~ \mathrm { w / w }$ , Fisher Chemical) were used as oxidants. Hexane $( 9 9 ~ \%$ , Sigma-Aldrich) and acetonitrile $( 9 9 . 8 ~ \%$ and HPLC gradient grade, Merck) were used as organic solvents for soil contamination and sample extraction and analysis methods, respectively. Other used chemicals were also of analytical grade and all solutions were prepared with high-purity water, i.e., distilled and dionized.

# 2.2. Catalysts synthesis

Clay-based catalysts were modified by the addition of different concentrations of sodium hydroxide $\left( \mathbf { x } _ { 1 } \cdot \mathbf { \left[ N a O H \right] } \right)$ ), ferrous sulfate $\left( \mathbf { X } _ { 2 } \cdot \mathbf { \right. }$ $[ \mathrm { F e S O } _ { 4 } ] )$ , and ferric sulfate $( \mathbf { x } _ { 3 } \cdot [ \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } ( \mathsf { S O } _ { 4 } ) _ { 3 } ] )$ , according to a $2 ^ { 3 }$ rotatable central composite design (RCCD) with three central points, resulting in 17 synthesized catalysts. Each catalyst was applied in batch tests to study the remediation of PHE artificially contaminated soil by oxidation process, in order to evaluate its activation capacity over HP and SP oxidants. The catalyst efficiency was quantified by PHE removal percent (response) from the contaminated soil. Table 2 presents the normalized values related to real values for the experimental design applied.

A $2 2 . 3 \mathrm { m L }$ total volume of ferric and ferrous sulfate mixture was prepared according to the concentrations shown in Table 2 and added to $2 0 \ g$ of the smectite. This suspension was homogenized and NaOH solution $\mathbf { 4 4 } \mathbf { m L }$ ) at its respective concentration for each assay (Table 2) was dripped on clay suspension for $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ under constant stirring. Each synthesized material was kept in an oven at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ during $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . After drying, the catalysts were ground by hand using a mortar and pestle until passing in $0 . 1 2 3 \mathrm { m m }$ sieve (145 Mesh), then they were stored for further use.

# 2.3. Characterization

Standard methods were carried out to characterize the raw clay and the modified clay catalyst (MCC). The characterization presented for modified clay are related to the selected catalyst (MCC 16), as presented in section 3.2, which obtained the best performance for the oxidant activation during batch application. Mineralogical analysis of samples was performed by Powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD), elemental chemical analysis by X-ray fluorescence (XRF), chemical nature of all chemically distinct Fe species by Mössbauer analysis, specific surface area analysis with BET method, and surface topography by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The XRF technique was also performed for chemical composition analysis of soil sample.

The XRD measurements were conducted in a D8 Advance X-ray diffraction instrument (Brucker) and monochromated CuK radiation at $4 0 \mathrm { k V } / 4 0 \mu \mathrm { A }$ . The diffractogram was recorded in $2 ^ { \circ } - 6 5 ^ { \circ } 2 \theta$ range with a $_ { 0 . 2 s }$ step size. Diffrac® software was used for data analysis. One way to identify smectite compared to another clay is based on the expandability of the basal layers after treatment with ethylene glycol and heating [47]. Hence, three X-ray diffraction patterns for raw clay samples were made: the first one was for regular raw clay, the second one after equilibrating with ethylene glycol vapor at room temperature overnight, and the other one after heating the sample at $4 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ (U. S. Geological Survey Open-File Report 01-041). X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometry was performed using a PANalytica Axios MAX advanced XRF.

# 2.4. Catalysts application in oxidation batch tests

# 2.4.1. Soil contamination

Soil samples were passed through a $2 . 3 6 \mathrm { m m }$ sieve (8 Mesh) prior to tests. According to the particle size distribution, the soil consists of 98.4 $\%$ of sand, $1 . 1 6 ~ \%$ of silt, and $0 . 4 4 ~ \%$ of clay thus configuring a sandy soil, characterized by the sieving method. Soil pH in water was 5.7. The soil organic matter content was $0 . 1 3 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { k g } ^ { - 1 }$ and it had $8 . 6 3 ~ \%$ of total iron. A water content of $1 . 4 5 ~ \%$ was considered in all the experiments. For the batch experiments, the soil was contaminated with phenanthrene solution in hexane to achieve a concentration of $2 0 0 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g }$ of PHE per $\mathbf { k } \mathbf { g }$ of dry soil. Then, the soil was kept in an open flask to dry the solvent, and finally stored. Artificially contaminated soil facilitates the process control allowing a better evaluation of mass balance and reaction efficiency.

# 2.4.2. Oxidation experiments

Batch oxidation tests were applied to each catalyst produced. $_ { 1 5 g }$ of contaminated soil were added to the reactor (Erlenmeyer $1 2 5 \mathrm { m L }$ ) with $_ { 1 . 5 g }$ of produced catalyst and $3 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of oxidant solution. Both oxidants, hydrogen peroxide and sodium persulfate at $2 . 4 \ g \ L ^ { - 1 }$ and $4 1 . 0  g \mathrm { ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, were evaluated. Control tests, water-soil (WS) and raw clay-oxidant-soil (COS), were made to assess the contaminant adsorption to soil particles and its natural transference to aqueous phase with WS control; COS control evaluates the raw clay capacity to activate the oxidant and contaminant adsorption to clay particles. All the experiments were performed without stirring and $\mathsf { p H }$ adjustment, at room temperature (around $2 8 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ for $^ { 7 2 \mathrm { h } }$ .

# 2.4.3. Extraction and analytical methods

In order to quantify the residual contamination, aliquots of $2 . 5 g$ of soil sample were taken from each reactor and added into $4 0 \mathrm { m L }$ vials along with $1 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of acetonitrile. These vials were exposed to ultrasound for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ for solid-liquid extraction, based on the 3550 Method US EPA [50] with some adaptations. Then, extracts were filtrated using $0 . 4 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ filters, added into $1 . 5 \mathrm { m L }$ vials, and analyzed by High-Performance Liquid Chromatography coupled with ultraviolet detection (HPLC-UV) (Shimadzu) under the following conditions: $\lambda = 2 5 4 \mathrm { n m }$ , flow rate $= 1 \mathrm { m L m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , and acetonitrile/water at a ratio $7 0 { : } 3 0 \ ( \mathrm { v / v } )$ as mobile phase.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Characterization analysis

# 3.1.1. X-ray analysis

The reflection angle variation was observed in the XRD results comparing raw clay pattern with patterns both for clay exposed to ethylene glycol vapor and for heated clay (Fig. 1). The smectite group has a diffraction pattern in the basal plane of $1 4 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ for the raw clay (Fig. 1a). After reaching equilibrium with ethylene glycol, the reflection expands to $1 7 \mathring { \mathrm { A } }$ (Fig. 1b), since the glycol addition to smectite generates an expansion of the basal planes. Moreover, when the clay is heated to $4 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , the reflection reduces to $1 0 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ (Fig. 1c). Then, the studied clay was classified as smectite clay, according to the literature [47].

Fig. 2 presents sample diffractograms of raw clay (RC) (Fig. 2a) and modified clay catalyst (MCC) (Fig. 2b). Initially, a reduction in the first reflection size of Fig. 2b can be observed when compared to the first reflection of Fig. 2a, which identifies the smectite. The reduction occurs due to a reduction in the particle size, which leads to a reduction in the crystallite. This effect indicates that the clay is less crystallized.

Besides, natrojarosite $\mathrm { ( N a F e _ { 3 } ^ { 3 + } ( S O _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 } ( O H ) _ { 6 } ) } .$ a mineral composed by an iron sulfate containing sodium, was formed in the MCC (Fig. 2b). This formation can be justified by the catalyst synthesis, in which there is an addition of ferric and ferrous sulfate and sodium hydroxide. Quartz can be noticed as the clay accessory mineral (Fig. 2a and b)

Smectites have negative charges on the surfaces of structural units, which are balanced by exchangeable cations. The compensating cations are alkaline and alkaline earth metals, mostly $\mathrm { N a } ^ { + }$ and $\mathrm { C a } ^ { + 2 }$ . The clay used in this study can be considered calcium smectite due to the XRF analysis, which presents $1 . 1 2 \%$ of calcium in its composition (Table 3), and by its lower swelling capacity in water compared to sodium smectite [51]. Table 3 shows the chemical composition from the XRF results. The main composition of the soil is silicon, as expected, and it presents low iron content, which indicates the need of adding iron for oxidant activation. Clay chemical composition is mainly based on silicon, followed by a considerable amount of aluminum and iron. Table 4 presents the results of chemical composition based on silicon oxide, calculated from the data provided in Table 3, so that they are on the same basis allowing the comparative analysis of the different samples. Regarding to the use of the same amount of each sample, a corresponding mass of $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ provides a reference basis for XRF analysis. A significant increase of $1 6 0 ~ \%$ in total iron content can be observed from RC to MCC 16, as shown in Table 4, due to the iron impregnation process.

# 3.1.2. Mössbauer spectroscopy

The Mössbauer spectroscopy data measured at $8 0 \mathrm { K }$ are shown in Figs. 3a for raw clay and b for modified clay catalyst 16 (MCC 16). The spectra consist of six-line pattern and also a two-line pattern that could be fitted with one hyperfine field and one doublet. The hyperfine fields, isomer shift, quadrupole splitting and relative areas for each spectrum are shown in Table 5. Thus, the three spectra revealed high amounts of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ doublet $( \mathsf { A R 2 } = 5 6 $ %and $8 7 \ \%$ ) and hyperfine field related to goethite $( \mathrm { A R 1 } = 4 4 \%$ and $1 3 \%$ ). Observing the results in Table 5, MCC 16 sample showed higher values of relative $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ doublet area than RC.

# 3.1.3. BET surface area

The BET surface area measurements indicated a significant decrease of the specific surface area from $1 4 3 . 2 6 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ (RC) to $2 5 . 5 9 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ (MCC 16).This area reduction for the modified clay suggests that there was an impregnation mechanism of iron minerals, produced during the catalyst synthesis, making covering layers on the clay grains surface. According to Dutta [46], montmorillonite, a clay mineral of the smectite group, has surface area of about $1 0 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ . Two natural clays from different cities of the Southeast of Brazil were studied from Acevedo et al. [52], who obtained BET surface areas (128.5 and $1 1 2 . 6 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ similar to the RC surface area $( 1 4 3 . 2 6 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { ~ g } ^ { - 1 } )$ . MCC 16 surface area $( 2 5 . 5 9 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ is in agreement with the Fe-imp sample result $( 2 2 \mathbf { m } ^ { 2 } \textbf { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ , made from a direct impregnation of iron oxide on clay grains [32,53]. The reduction in the surface area is an interesting result, since the modified clay catalyst has the function of the oxidants activation, not the adsorption of the organic compounds.

# 3.1.4. Scanning Electron microscopy

Fig. 4 shows the micrographs of raw clay and modified clay catalyst 16 (MCC 16) surfaces obtained from scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In general, a great amount of agglomerates of different sizes can be observed in both samples. However, for MCC 16 (Fig. 4b), there were small differences in morphology with an increase of agglomerates and crystals particles, probably because of Fe oxides and/or hydroxides deposition onto clay particle surfaces during catalyst synthesis.

# 3.2. Application in batch oxidation experiments

The experimental matrix for catalyst synthesis using $2 ^ { 3 } { \mathrm { ~ R C C D } }$ is presented in Table 6. Each assay represents a produced catalyst according to the experimental matrix conditions. Each catalyst was applied to the oxidation reaction to treat the PHE-contaminated soil in order to evaluate its efficiency inactivating SP or HP. The results for the PHE removal percentage are shown in Table 6. All the experiments were performed in random order to ensure statistical significance.

Around $2 0 ~ \%$ of PHE removal from the soil occurred in the watersoil control test (WS) probably because of the contaminant’s natural transference to water and a part that could not be extracted from the soil by the applied method. The transference to water may be related to the equilibrium expressed by the octanol/water partition coefficient for PHE $\mathrm { 1 o g ~ K o w = 4 . 6 2 ~ \pm ~ 0 . 0 1 }$ [54]. The raw clay-oxidant-soil control test (COS) demonstrated that the raw alone clay did not contribute to the oxidant activation, explaining the iron addition to the catalyst synthesis. Moreover, the effect of contaminant adsorption by the clay was not observed, probably due to its small amount added to the system.

The empirical model generated from PS was not considered significant and resulted in a regression coefficient of 0.61 with low degree of data fit, indicating that the equation could not explain the data properly. This can be justified by the uncertainties of the heterogeneous system involving the soil. Nevertheless, the influence of the independent variables on the response can be evaluated through the effects obtained. The most evident effects were quadratic $\mathbf { X } _ { 1 }$ in respect to [NaOH], quadratic $\mathbf { X } _ { 3 }$ for $[ \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ) _ { 3 } ]$ , linear $\mathbf { X } _ { 2 }$ for $[ \mathrm { F e S O _ { 4 } } ]$ , and interaction of factors $\mathbf { X _ { 1 } }$ and $\mathbf { x } _ { 3 }$ . The strong interaction between $\mathbf { X } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathbf { X } _ { 3 }$ (effect of 5.41) generates a requirement of joint interpretation of these variables; thus, an increase in $\mathbf { X _ { 1 } }$ only increases the PHE removal from the soil if there is an increase of $\mathbf { X } _ { 3 }$ . For example, if the variable $\mathbf { X } _ { 3 }$ is set to the minimum level in catalyst synthesis, the response will only increase if $\mathbf { X } _ { 1 }$ is also at the minimum level. This behavior can be observed when evaluating the data in Table 5 and explained by the positive and significant effect of the interaction. Variable $\mathbf { X } _ { 2 }$ (effect of 2.4) can be evaluated individually and an increase in its concentration can promote an increase in the PHE removal percentage from the soil.

For HP assays, it was possible to obtain an empirical model (Eq. (25)) considered significant and predictive with $9 0 ~ \%$ confidence, $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ of 0.83 and $\mathrm { R _ { a d j u s t e d } }$ of 0.76. Therefore, the proposed model presents a reasonable fit to experimental data. The variables $\mathbf { X } _ { 1 }$ ([NaOH]) and $\mathbf { X } _ { 3 }$ $\displaystyle \left( [ \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } ( \mathsf { S O } _ { 4 } ) _ { 3 } ] \right)$ were considered significant with remarkable effects. As the interactions were not significant, the effects were evaluated individually. Based on the effects of variables $\mathbf { X } _ { 1 }$ (linear effect of -4.14 and quadratic effect of -10.02) and $\mathbf { x } _ { 3 }$ (linear effect of -5.48 and quadratic effect of -6.78), it was concluded that the efficiency in PHE removal can be increased by using a catalyst with lower NaOH concentration and lower Fe (III) concentration in the synthesis.

# 4. Conclusions

The synthesis of a clay-based catalyst supporting iron minerals for chemical oxidation studies was efficient in creating a more reactive material. The raw clay (RC) used was classified as smectite clay by X

Ray analysis. The selected catalyst (MCC 16) was able to achieve around $7 5 \mathrm { - } 8 0 ~ \%$ Phenanthrene (PHE) removal from contaminated soil during the oxidation experiments with both hydrogen peroxide (HP) and persulfate (PS). The empirical model obtained from HP experimental data was significant and predictive. The multivariable analysis indicated that catalysts with lower sodium hydroxide and lower ferric sulfate can increase PHE removal. In SP experiments, the empirical model was not significant, but it is possible to infer that an increase of PHE removal can be obtained only by increasing both sodium hydroxide and ferric sulfate concentrations. So, both sodium hydroxide and ferric sulfate concentrations affected the design of experiments response (PHE removal) either with HP or with SP. Overall, HP presented slightly better results compared to SP.

The best condition found for catalyst synthesis was using sodium hydroxide concentration of $0 . 6 2 5 \mathrm { M }$ , ferrous sulfate concentration of $1 3 7 . 2 5 \mathrm { g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ and ferric sulfate concentration of $2 7 4 . 5 0 \mathrm { g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ . X-Ray analysis showed that natrojarosite mineral $\mathrm { ( N a F e _ { 3 } ^ { 3 + } ( S O _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 } ( O H ) _ { 6 } ) }$ was formed in MCC 16, and the total iron content of RC increased in $1 6 0 ~ \%$ after the modification. Mössbauer spectroscopy indicated that higher values of relative $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ doublet were present in MCC 16 thus proving that iron impregnation process occurred, as expected. There was a significant decrease in the specific surface area, from $1 4 3 . 2 6 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ (RC) to $2 5 . 5 9 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ (MCC 16), due to the cover layers that were formed on the RC grains surface. The surface area reduction hinders adsorption processes. Scanning electron microscopy exhibited an increase of agglomerates and crystals particles, confirming the Fe oxides and/or hydroxides deposition onto clay particle surfaces.

The catalyst produced in this work is suitable for both in situ or ex situ remediation processes and aims at reducing the organic contaminant mass in the media through oxidation reactions. Circumstances involving soil remediation with more contaminants will probably require higher reagents concentration for the catalyst synthesis. Although this work provides reference data for PHE oxidation design in soil, it may be extended for other PAH contaminants.
