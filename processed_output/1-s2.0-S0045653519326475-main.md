Keywords: Carbon nanofibers Electrospinning Sulfate radicals Peroxymonosulfate

Catalytic activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) to generate radicals has received considerable and increasing attention in the environmental catalysis for treatment of recalcitrant pollutants. In the current study, a series of highly porous, cobalt-loaded activated carbon nanofibers (Co/CNFs) were prepared by one-pot electrospinning followed by thermal treatment. Observations showed that the limited addition of Co $( \leq 8 \% )$ had no obvious effect on the morphology of the resulted CNFs, but it did affect the surface area and porosity of the CNFs as well as the carbon graphitic process during the carbonization. The applicability of this confined nanoreactor used in sulfate-radical based advanced oxidation processes (SRAOPs) was systematically investigated. The effect of pH on the radical generation and organics removal was examined. The oxygen species on the CNFs played an important role in the activation of PMS. The carbon layer encapsulated on the Co crystal surface inhibited the Co leaching during the reaction and increased the catalytic efficiency due to the enhanced interfacial charge transfer. Meanwhile, the carbon layer could synchronously function as the adsorptive active sites during the degradation of organics. Results showed that the Co/CNFs possessed the highest catalytic efficiency under neutral pH, corresponding to the sulfate radical generation. The Co leaching and XPS results showed that the Co served as the main active site in PMS activation.

# 1. Introduction

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have evolved as one of the most innovative water treatment technologies. Recently, sulfate radical based AOPs (SR-AOPs) have been widely applied for organics degradation via the generation of sulfate radical $( S 0 _ { 4 } { ^ { - } } )$ , which is more selective (Eberson, 1982; Oh et al., 2016) and stable over a wide pH range compared to hydroxyl radical (OH) (Hu et al., 2013; Neta et al., 1988). For $S O _ { 4 } { } ^ { \bullet ^ { - } }$ generation, the combination of transition metal ions and peroxymonosulfate (PMS) has been found to be a high efficient method. Among all the transition metals, cobalt-based catalysts have been discovered as a suitable activator for PMS (Anipsitakis and Dionysiou, 2004; Chen et al., 2008). Cobalt nanoparticles (Co-NPs) have shown high catalytic performance in PMS activation, however, aggregation as a result of poor chemical and thermal stabilities during operation lead to the decrease of the catalytic activity (Anipsitakis et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2008). Hence, to develop the active and stable heterogeneous Co-based catalysts, various supports (e.g. carbon material, ceramic membrane, and metal oxides) have been investigated to provide a much higher surface active sites (Bao et al., 2018a, 2018b; 2019a, 2019c; Shukla et al., 2010). Another problem faced by the catalyst is the separation and recovery after reaction. To solve this problem, one approach is to functionalize the catalyst with magnetic material. By incorporating iron into $\mathrm { { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { { O } _ { 4 } } }$ , the composite showed a magnetic property, therefore, it could be easily separated from the solution. Meanwhile, the composite showed a much higher catalytic activity due to the synergistic effect between iron and $\mathrm { { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { { O } _ { 4 } } }$ (Deng et al., 2019).

Fibrous carbon materials on the other hand, have attracted the attention of scientists and engineers since they could be produced from various carbon precursors via the melt-spinning process (Inagaki et al., 2012). Recently, electrospinning has been developed as a relatively simple and low-cost strategy to produce continuous nanofibers from a wide variety of materials (Li and Xia, 2004). Among all kinds of polymers, polyacrylonitrile (PAN) exhibits exceptional solvent resistance and thermosetting characteristic. Meanwhile, it shows good mechanical property and high degree of molecular orientation (Donnet and Bansal, 1998). The transformation process via stabilization and carbonization has been investigated in previous studies (Bao et al., 2019c; Ko and Chen, 1999; Watt, 1969). PAN-based carbon nanofibers (CNFs) have been used in water purification, recovery of solvent vapors and making humidity sensors (Bajaj and Dhawan, 1997), attributing to their unique electronic, physical and chemical properties (Zhu et al., 2017) and have been regarded as one kind of promising catalyst carriers. In the previous studies, many attempts have been made to load metal catalysts (i.e., Fe, Co, Zn) into activated carbon fibers for water treatment (Huang et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2014, 2018; Li et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2018). Results showed that the metal load carbon fibers processed a high catalytic activity as well as low metal leaching, which could be caused by the connection between carbon fibers and metal catalysts. However, most of the studies focused on the multi-step fabrication in the literatures, it is necessary to make the process simpler by using one-pot synthesis method.

Hence, in the current study, we reported the synthesis of CNFs containing a high dispersion of active cobalt crystals via a one-pot electrospinning of PAN precursors doped with cobalt nitrate, followed by thermal treatment. The main objectives of this work are 1) to arrange the sulfate radical generation into a confined nanoscale reactor to enhance the catalytic activity; 2) to synthesize such nanoscale reactor by a one-pot electrospinning method; 3) encapsulation of Co crystals into the CNFs to increase the stability and surface-active sites; and 4) to evaluate the nanoscale reactor performance on the degradation of organic pollutants. It is hypothesized that, the carbon layers encapsulated on the Co crystals would prevent the cobalt dissolution and enhance its catalytic activity because of interfacial charge transfer. In addition, CNFs may offer adsorptive domains for concentrating of reactants to the Cobased active sites and thus increase their catalytic efficiency. A systematic evaluation of the degradation of organics with the asprepared Co/CNFs nanoreactor was performed. Co/CNFs as a multifunctional material showed that such a system with good catalytic and adsorptive capabilities would process great promise for practical environmental applications.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Chemicals

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, $\mathsf { M w } = 1 5 0 , 0 0 0 \mathrm { ‰ }$ (Sigma-Aldrich), cobal$\operatorname { t } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ nitrate hexahydrate $( \mathsf { C o } ( \mathsf { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathsf { H } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } )$ (Sigma-Aldrich) and Dimethylformamide (DMF) (VWR) were used for the dope solution preparation. Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) (Alfa Aesar) was used as the oxidant and sulfamethoxazole (SMX) (Sigma-Aldrich) was chosen as the target organic pollutant in the catalytic degradation process. Glacial acetic acid $\left( \mathsf { C } _ { 2 } \mathsf { H } _ { 4 } \mathsf { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ and methanol $\mathrm { ( C H _ { 4 } O ) }$ were provided by Sigma-Aldrich and used for the mobile phase in SMX detection. All chemicals used in the current study were of reagent grade or higher and were used as received.

# 2.2. Preparation of Co/CNFs

PAN polymer powder with a concentration of 8 wt $\%$ was dissolved in DMF by stirring at room temperature to form a transparent dope solution. For the preparation of ${ \mathsf { C o } } / { \mathsf { P A N } }$ electrospinning solution, $\mathsf { C o } ( \mathsf { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathsf { H } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ with different concentrations were added to the mixture of PAN and DMF before the dissolution of PAN. The as-prepared dope solutions were named as $\chi C o / \mathrm { P A N }$ solution, where $\chi$ refers to the weight percentage of $\mathsf { C o } ( \mathsf { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathsf { H } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O }$ (0, 2, 5 and 8) with respect to PAN in the solution. The dope solution was fed to a spinneret (inner diameter $( \mathrm { I D } ) = 0 . 7 5 \ : \mathrm { m m }$ , out diameter $\mathrm { ( O D ) } = 1 . 5 9 \ : \mathrm { m m }$ ) via a syringe pump. In the electrospinning, the flow rate was set as $3 0 \mu \mathrm { L } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and the applied voltage was $2 1 \pm 2 { \mathrm { ~ k V } } .$ . The working distance was set at $1 2 \mathrm { c m }$ and the humidity in the chamber was maintained at $4 0 \pm 5 \%$ . The polymer dope was electrospun to form nanofibers on the polyethylene terephthalate (PET) non-woven support using an electrospinning apparatus (MJESPIN-08-01, SBTG, Inc, USA). The details on the equipment could be found in our previous work (Liao et al., 2013; Tian et al., 2013). The prepared $\chi C o / \mathrm { P A N }$ nanofibrous layer was then air-dried in a fume hood overnight for the removal of residual solvent.

All electrospun ${ \mathsf { C o } } / { \mathsf { P A N } }$ nanofibers were then peeled off from the non-woven substrate and were transferred into a furnace for the stabilization treatment at $2 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ with a heating rate of $2 \ ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C } \ \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . The stabilized nanofibers were cooled to room temperature and carbonized at $8 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in $\Nu _ { 2 }$ atmosphere with a heating rate of $5 ~ { } ^ { \circ } \mathsf { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . The resulting carbon nanofibers were named as $\chi C 0 / C \mathrm { N F s }$ $\boldsymbol { \cdot } \boldsymbol { \chi } = 0$ , 2, 5 and 8). The preparation schematic of Co/CNFs is shown in Fig. S1.

# 2.3. Physical-chemical characteristics of Co/CNFs

The characteristics of as-prepared $\chi C o /$ PAN dope solutions were analyzed at ambient conditions. The viscosity of dope solutions was measured via an Anton Paar MCR 102 rheometer and the measurement of solution electrical conductivities were conducted by a conductivity meter (Mettler Toledo). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on an SDT-Q600 instrument to investigate the Co/CNFs transformation mechanism (TA instrument).

The morphology and cobalt distribution of $\chi C 0 / C \mathrm { N F s }$ were investigated using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, JSM-7600 F, JEOL) combined with an energy-dispersive Xray spectroscopy (EDX). The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis of Co/CNFs were measured via nitrogen $( \mathsf { N } _ { 2 } )$ adsorption/desorption isotherms. The crystalline structure and elemental mapping analysis were further conducted by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL 2100 F) and X-rays diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8

Advance). Diameter distribution of the CNFs were determined using Adobe software measuring 100 nanofibers of each FESEM images in different regions. The surface roughness and force of $\chi C o /$ CNFs were measured by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Park XE100). The vibrational absorbance was examined by Fourier transformed infra-red spectrometer (FTIR, PerkinElmer) and the structural characteristics were investigated by and Raman spectrometer (HORIBA Scientific). The chemical status of elements was examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Shimadzu).

# 2.4. Catalytic performance evaluation followed by adsorption on Co/CNFs

The performance of the as-prepared Co/CNFs for removal of organics was evaluated in a series of batch experiments. The target organic pollutant used in the current study was sulfamethoxazole (SMX), which has been widely used and detected from various systems (Godfrey et al., 2007; Jansomboon et al., 2016; Segura et al., 2009). The uncontrolled usage of SMX induced the antibioticresistance of bacteria and gens, leading to an undesired chronic effect on the whole ecological system (Rizzo et al., 2013). A certain amount of Co/CNFs was firstly added into the working solution of SMX. The adsorption test was conducted for $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ with stirring followed by the addition of PMS, which initiated the catalytic process. The samples were then drawn at pre-determined intervals for the further test.

# 2.5. Analytical method

A liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu, LC-2030C-3D) was used to quantify SMX concentration. The detection conditions were set as follows: mobile phase: methanol and water $0 . 1 \%$ acetic acid) with a volume ratio of 60:40; flow rate: $0 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m L } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ; and wavelength: $2 6 4 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ . The quantification of the sulfate radical generated in the process was achieved with a spectrophotometric determination method. As described in the previous study, cerium (III) could be oxidised selectively by sulfate radical to form $\mathsf { C e } ( \mathrm { I V } )$ whereas other reactive oxygen species $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , OH and $0 _ { 2 } ^ { - } \cdot$ ) had shown a negligible effect (Chen et al., 2018). To avoid the interference of other intermediates, the radical generation experiment was conducted without the addition of organic pollutants. The generated $\mathsf { C e } ( \mathrm { I V } )$ was then quantified using a UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) and the detection wavelength was set at $3 2 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ .

# 3. Results and discussions

# 3.1. Growth mechanism and characterization of Co/CNFs

The morphologies of the electronspun $\chi C o / \mathrm { P A N }$ fibers were observed from the scanning electron micrographs (Fig. S2). All the as-prepared fibers exhibited a random orientation with a high aspect ratio, producing an intricately constructed threedimensional interconnected porous membrane. The obtained ${ \mathsf { C o } } /$ PAN possesses the similar nanofiber morphology and no obvious nanoparticles were observed on the fibers. Compared with the pure PAN nanofiber, the diameters of ${ \mathsf { C o } } / { \mathsf { P A N } }$ nanofibers decreased slightly due to the change of viscosity and electrical conductivity of the dope solution. As shown in Table 1, the viscosity increased with the initial addition of $\mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ . Further increment of ${ \mathsf { C o } } ( { \mathsf { N } } -$ $0 _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } 0$ only induce a small decrease of solution viscosity, while the electrical conductivity increased dramatically. The increased conductivity would lead to stronger repulsion during the electrospinning in the electric field because of the greater charge density on the nanofiber surface, resulting in a smaller nanofiber diameter.

It is worth mentioning that the dope solution destabilizes with any further increment of the cobalt dopant in the solution.

Analysis of morphology and diameter distribution of Co/CNFs are shown in Fig. 1. Results showed that CNFs became intertwined and adjacent fiber segments conglutinated to each other after the carbonization process (Fig. S3), while the remained aspect ratio could resolve the crystal aggregation (Zhang et al., 2018). All CNFs were constituted of an interwoven and open porous network, which provides accessible sites for effective mass transfer of organics in solution during catalytic degradation reaction. The composites contain an ultralow loading of Co and the structure of the underlying CNF substrate was unaffected based on the XRD results (Fig. S4).

The local structure of 5Co/CNF was further analyzed by TEM (Fig. 1b and Fig. S5). In agreement with the XRD pattern, no obvious crystalline phase of Co was observable. However, under the high magnification, the crystalline structure could be identified as Co crystals. The elemental mapping indicated that the nanofiber was composed of C, O and minute amount of Co elements (Fig. S5), substantiating the successful synthesis of 5Co/CNF.

The average diameter of the Co/CNFs slightly decreased with increasing Co content in the dope solution, which was consistent with the results for Co/PAN fibers. Interestingly, in a previous study, the diameter of carbon nanofibers increased with the addition of metal particles attributing to formation of porous structures under high metal content (Aykut, 2012). In the current study, the porous structure was not obvious as the Co loading amount (8 wt%) was much lower as compared with the previous study $( 3 0 \mathrm { ~ w t \% } )$ , however, the BET results showed that the addition of Co would increase the surface area of CNF (Table 1 and Fig. S6). During the heat treatment, the motion of cobalt ions in the carbon matrix would result in a porous structure due to the different phase behaviours of cobalt and carbon. Therefore, with the increased of Co concentration in the matric, the movement of ions enhanced, resulting in a more porous structure, and greater surface area (Aykut, 2012). Compared with the $0 C o / C \mathrm { N F }$ which processed a surface area of $2 6 5 . { \dot { 6 } } \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } ,$ , after the addition of $\mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ , the surface area increased dramatically to 436.4, 513.0 and $3 0 1 5 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ for $2 { \cal C } 0 /$ CNF, 5Co/CNF and $8 C { 0 } / { \mathrm { C N F } } ,$ respectively. The super high surface area might be an advantage for the pollutant adsorption and degradation. As shown in Table 1, the $0 C o / C \mathrm { N F }$ possessed a smooth surface with a uniform diameter $2 3 5 . 3 \pm 2 5 . 2 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ . With the addition of Co, the resultant Co/CNFs original fibrous structure was maintained, with only a minor decrease of the fiber diameter due to the shrinkage of fibers during the dehydration activation of Co. The actual Co loading amount in ${ \mathsf { C o } } / { \mathsf { C N F } }$ arrays was determined using SEM-EDX (Fig. S7), which was summarized in Table 1.

The TGA plots for the $\chi C o / \mathrm { P A N }$ nanofibers is shown in Fig. 1d. Compared with the pure PAN nanofibers, with the decomposition temperature of $3 0 0 { - } 4 0 0 ^ { \circ } { \mathsf C } ,$ , all Co/PAN fibers showed a continuous weight loss in the temperature range of $1 2 0 { - } 3 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C .$ . The much lower weight loss temperature range could be caused by the removal of volatile components as well as dehydrogenation and cyclization occurring during the stabilization process. It could be concluded that the addition of Co in the PAN nanofibers would initiate the chemical reactions in a lower temperature.

XPS analysis was conducted to investigate the surface chemistry of $\chi C 0 / C \mathrm { N F s }$ . Due to the detection limits of the instrument, the Co loading on 2Co/CNF was too low to obtain a detectable Co 2p spectra, while the 5Co/CNF and 8Co/CNF showed a resolved XPS spectrum. The survey spectrum shown in Fig. 2a revealed the presence of four peaks at 285, 398, 531 and 778 eV, which corresponds to carbon (C1s), nitrogen (N1s), oxygen (O1s) and cobalt (Co 2p) species, respectively. Table S1 summaries the quantified element concentrations in the cCo/CNFs. The C1s and N 1s concentration showed a slight decrease with the addition of Co, while O 1s levels were significantly higher in Co/CNFs $( 9 . 4 \% )$ than that of in the pure CNF $( 5 . 9 \% )$ .

The addition of Co could increase the pyridinic N and pyrrolic N in the final products. It is hypothesized that ${ \mathrm { C o O } } _ { \mathrm { x } }$ in the oxidised state would be reduced during the carbonization process, while the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Co2p indicating oxidised species on the CNF surface should be caused by the post-oxidation in the air, which is shown in Fig. S8. The co-existence of ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { C o O _ { x } }$ could be evidenced in the previous studies, which confirmed the reduction of Co in the carbonization process (Bao et al ., 2019c; Zhang et al., 2018).

The O 1s spectrum of CNF consisted of four multiplets including CeO, ${ \sf C } = { \sf O }$ , $0 { - } C { = } 0$ and COOH onto the CNF surface at 530.5, 531.8, 532.9 and $5 3 3 . 9 \ \mathrm { e V } ,$ , respectively. Generally, the O 1s peak can be deconvoluted into three kinds of peaks (e.g., lattice oxygen (529 eV), oxygen vacancies or defects (531e532 eV) and chemisorbed or dissociated oxygen $( 5 3 3 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ (Torrente-Murciano et al., 2013). Therefore, the addition peak at low binding energy $( 5 3 0 . 5 \mathrm { e V } )$ of the O1s peak from $1 4 . 9 \%$ to $2 0 . 9 \%$ could be ascribed to lattice oxygen in ${ \mathrm { C o O } } _ { \mathrm { x } }$ due to the post-oxidation. In the case of $5 0 /$ CNF, the concentration of oxygen vacancies could be as high as $7 4 \%$ with a quite limited chemisorbed oxygen $( 4 . 8 \% )$ , which could affect the catalytic activity. The oxygen vacancies may secure the cobalt active site for donating electrons to PMS for sulfate radical generation (Duan et al., 2018). Also, the presence of trace amount of hydroxyl groups on CNF could be generated by the reaction between highly reactive radicals formed during the carbonization and water vapor or oxygen when the CNFs were exposed to air postcarbonization. These hydroxyl groups would enhance the trapping of electrons as the electron-transfer mediator, resulting in a higher catalytic efficiency in the organic degradation process (Yang et al., 2015).

# 3.2. Catalytic performance evaluation of Co/CNFs

Prior to investigating the catalytic activities of Co/CNFs for the activation of PMS, the adsorption of SMX to ${ \mathsf { C o } } / { \mathsf { C N F } }$ was evaluated. The test was conducted for $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ without the addition of PMS. As mentioned before, Co/CNFs processed super high surface areas, therefore, they were expected to have an obvious adsorption effect. However, the results in Fig. 3 showed that the adsorption of SMX on all Co/CNFs was quite limited $( < 2 0 \% )$ , indicating that the adsorption was more likely influenced by other factors (i.e., the surface hydrophilicity and electronic property) rather than the surface area (Zhang et al., 2010).

However, the SMX could be completely removed in $4 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ for 5Co/CNF and 8Co/CNF with the addition of $0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ PMS. The Co leaching after 120 min reaction was detected as 0.034, 0.31 and $0 . 5 2 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ for 2Co/CNF, 5Co/CNF and 8Co/CNF, respectively. Based on the $_ { \mathrm { C o } / \mathrm { C } }$ ratio in the sample (Table 1), the theoretical Co amount added in the solution was calculated as 0.64, 4.69 and $6 . 8 3 ~ \mathrm { m g } ~ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for 2Co/CNF, 5Co/CNF and 8Co/CNF, respectively, therefore, the leached Co concentration was $5 . 3 \%$ , $6 . 6 \%$ and $7 . 6 \%$ based on mass balance equations. The limited Co leaching for $2 { \cal C } 0 /$ CNF could be caused by the homogeneous dispersion of cobalt crystals in the CNF matrix. When the Co concentration increased in the matrix, the dispersion could not be well achieved as well as the confinement effect, resulting in a higher porous structure and less attachment between Co and carbon. To further investigate the role of carbon layer, a pure Co crystal was prepared under the same condition without PAN addition and used for comparison. The performance of the pure Co crystal was tested at a catalyst loading amount of $5 0 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - \bar { 1 } }$ , and the results showed that the SMX removal was less than $3 0 \%$ with $0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ PMS addition in $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ (Fig. S9).

The Co/CNFs showed a much superior effect on PMS activation compared with the Co and CNF, which revealed that the spatial confinement effect increased its catalytic efficiency due to the internal electron transfer in the composites. The removal efficiencies of SMX in the presence of Co/CNFs and PMS increased with increasing cobalt loading from 0Co/CNF to 5Co/CNF. For pure CNF (0Co/CNF), the PMS activation sites could be the surface oxygen and nitrogen-containing functional groups (Bao et al ., 2019c). For ${ \mathsf { C o } } /$ CNFs, the main active sites were proposed as Co crystal $\mathrm { \dot { ~ } C o } ^ { 0 }$ in the internal part and ${ \mathrm { C o O } } _ { \mathrm { x } }$ on the surface). With increase of Co amount from $2 \%$ to $5 \%$ in the composites, the removal efficiency increased from $4 0 \%$ to $\sim 1 0 0 \%$ . The higher cobalt loading would provide more active sites for generating radicals, thereby enhancing the oxidation efficiencies and reaction kinetics. However, when the Co further increased to $8 \%$ in the dope solution, the resulted 8Co/CNF did not show a significant enhancement on SMX removal, which could be caused by the effect of oxygen vacancies in the composites as well as the limitation of PMS in the solution. Since Co/CNFs (adsorption) and PMS (Control) could not efficiently remove SMX separately, it could be concluded that SMX was decomposed mainly via a synergistic effect between Co/CNFs and PMS in the current system. Meanwhile, it was found that the pH after reaction slightly decreased from around 6 to 4.5, which could be caused by the formation of some intermediate organic acids in the degradation process (Bao et al., 2019b).

# 3.3. Sulfate radical generation via Co/CNFs

Sulfate radical has been recognized as the predominant radical species in the SR-AOP. And it is well acknowledged that pH is an important factor affecting the radical generation and distribution in water system (Guan et al., 2011). Generally, $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet } }$ would be dominant in the range of $\mathsf { p H } 1 { - } 9$ . At the acidity of $\mathsf { p H 9 }$ and higher, decomposition of $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet } }$ would occur and  OH would in turn be the predominant radical in the system (Fang et al., 2012). The effect of pH on SMX removal is shown in Fig. 4a. The overall pH effect on the SMX degradation would be dictated by the combination of all the following conditions: 1) rate of generation of radical species; 2) relative distribution of radical species in water; 3) ionization state of SMX.

The pH of solution can affect the SMX species and hydration of SMX molecules based on the its $\mathsf { p K } _ { \mathsf { a } }$ values $\mathsf { \Delta p K } _ { \mathsf { a 1 } } = 1 . 8$ and $\mathsf { p K } _ { \mathsf { a } 2 } = 5 . 6$ ) (Dodd and Huang, 2004). As shown in Fig. S10, SMX could exist as cationic SMX $( [ \mathsf { S M X } ^ { + } ] )$ , neutral SMX ([SMX]), and anionic SMX $( [ S \mathbf { M } X ^ { - } ] )$ under different solution pHs. At pH 1, SMX mainly exists in $[ { \mathsf { S } } { \mathsf { M } } X ^ { + } ]$ while at pH 11, SMX mainly exists in and $[ S \mathsf { M X } ^ { - } ]$ . When the solution pH is around 6, SMX is primarily in the isoelectric SMX form, the less charged molecular could reduce the intramolecular electrical repulsion, thus increasing the mass transfer coefficient. At $\mathsf { p H } ~ 1$ and 11, the homogeneity repulsion limited the adsorption of SMX on ${ \mathrm { C o / C N F } } ,$ while the neutral SMX molecular has a higher affinity to ${ \mathsf { C o } } / { \mathsf { C N F } }$ which could be verified from the higher adsorption (Fig. 4a). The SMX removal efficiency shows a positive relationship with the generation of $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet } }$ , which could be concluded that $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet } }$ played an important role in the SMX degradation. Results in Fig. 4b shows that enhancement in production rate of sulfate radical with an increase of $\mathsf { p H }$ from 1 to 6, while at $\mathsf { p H } 1 1$ , the radical generation decreased slightly which could be a result of the decomposition of $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet } }$ at high pH value. The highest SMX removal efficiency was achieved at pH 6. At pH 6, the isoelectric SMX has a higher affinity to the Co/CNF surface, resulting in the higher adsorption, meanwhile, the high generation rate of $S O _ { 4 } { } ^ { \bullet ^ { - } }$ at $\mathsf { p H } 6$ enhances the decomposition of SMX on the Co/CNF surface.

# 3.4. Reaction mechanism

In order to develop a better understanding of the reaction mechanism, the Co/CNFs were analyzed with XPS after reaction under different pHs. Table 3 summarizes the atomic composition of the elements in the fresh and used 5Co/CNF. The concentration of ${ \mathsf { C o } } \ 2 { \mathsf { p } }$ decreased after reaction for all the samples analyzed, indicating that self-regeneration of the material was not fully achieved and this could be further supported by the cobalt leaching analysis. Further attempts are still needed to enhance the stability of the materials. Interestingly, the Co 2p concentration decreased to $0 . 0 6 \%$ at $\mathsf { p H } 6$ which was much lower compared with $\mathsf { p H } 1$ and 11. The result corresponded to that of the SMX degradation efficiency, which indicated the SMX degradation was caused mainly by radical pathway via PMS activation at the scarification of Co active sites. At $\mathsf { p H } 1$ and 6, $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet } }$ would be the dominant reactive species, while at pH 11, $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet } }$ would decompose to generate  OH, which becomes the dominant reactive species. After the reaction, the O1s concentration increased for all cases, which could be attributed to the hydroxyl group in solution. Compared with the reaction at pH 1 and 11, the reaction under $\mathsf { p H } 6$ showed less increase in oxygen amount. Meanwhile, the addition of SMX did not show any significant influences on the elements concentrations at $\mathsf { p H } 6$ , meaning that the PMS was firstly activated by Co active sites on Co/CNFs to generate radicals, which could further decompose SMX (Fig. S11). However, when the oxygen peak was deconvoluted into $\mathrm { O - H }$ and O-M, it could be found that the ratio of different oxygen species changed (Table 3). The results showed that oxygen played a role in the radical generation process. After reaction, O-M concentration decreased for all samples due to the metal leaching effect in the composites.

# 4. Conclusions

A novel Co crystal loaded carbon nanofiber was prepared via a one-pot electrospinning followed by thermal treatment. The multifunctional Co/CNFs were used as PMS activator for enhanced organics degradation. The following conclusions can be drawn based on this study:

1) The Co/CNFs were successfully prepared by electrospinning followed by thermal treatment, and the Co concentration was limited by the viscosity and conductivity of the as-prepared dope solution.   
2) The addition of Co in the precursor influences the fiber diameter due to the electric-field effect in the electrospinning process, while it has no significant effect on the fiber morphology.   
3) The addition of Co increased the CNFs surface area from 265.6 to $5 1 3 . 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ and changed the $\mathrm { I _ { D } / I _ { G } }$ ratio from 3.73 to 3.13 at the addition Co concentration of $5 \%$ .   
4) The adsorption and degradation of organics in the presence of Co/CNFs was affected by $\mathsf { p H } ,$ , and the optimized performance was obtained under $\mathsf { p H } 6$ .   
5) The degradation of organics proceeded via a radical pathway, in which sulfate radical played a virtual role, while the oxygen species on the material surface also contributed to the reaction.   
6) The Co leaching test and XPS results for used Co/CNFs confirmed the main active site was Co $( C 0 0 _ { \mathrm { x } } )$ , and the material could not be fully self-generated.

Overall, the multifunctional Co/CNFs could be prepared via electrospinning followed by thermal treatment and used for PMS activation. The carbon nanofibers provided protection and dispersion of Co crystals over the entirety of the as-prepared fibers, while the enhanced interfacial charge transfer and increased adsorptive sites advanced the high catalytic efficiency of Co/CNFs. However, this material was proved not to be fully self-regenerated, the stability and life-time should be optimized in the following study.
