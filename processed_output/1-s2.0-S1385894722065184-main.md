# . Introduction

The environmental presence of refractory pollutants, including endocrine disrupters, pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and personal care products, threatens aquatic organisms and humans [13].

To date, a large number of studies have focused on the removal of recalcitrant organics from aqueous systems through the use of advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), which exhibit excellent degradation efficiencies [4]. Besides, it is complicated to remove them using conventional treatment methods due to the stable structure of these organic pollutants. At present, dozens of studies have evidenced that peroxymonosulfate (PMS)-advanced oxidation processes (PMS-AOPs) are productive in degrading organics [57]. However, metal-based catalysts cause metal leaching inevitably, although they have a good catalytic ability [8,9].

Fortunately, carbon materials such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), reduced graphene oxide (rGO), and nanodiamonds have been employed as non-metal catalysts for persulfate activation [10,11]. And those carbons' inert nature can be enhanced by non-metal heteroatom doping into the carbon framework to produce ROSs during PS-AOP.

Recently, biochar (BC) stands out as a promising low-cost and green carbon material with a tunable specific surface area (SSA) and abundant surface functional groups. Biochar can be derived from various waste biomass sources through thermochemical processes, including hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) and pyrolysis [12]. Indeed, it has been found that nitrogen doping significantly enhances the catalytic ability of biochar by adjusting the spin density and charge distribution [13,14]. Specifically, graphitic-N species have been reported to play a significant role in the activation of PMS due to the positive charge on the carbonaceous material leading to strong interactions with PMS [14]. Similarly, edge nitrogen species (e.g., pyridinic-N) were found to be the key reactive sites in plant-derived biochars [15,16]. As mentioned above, various conclusions regarding the activation mechanism of the PMS/ carbon catalyst system have been derived due to the structural complexity of the carbon material. Mainly, in-situ treatment has been used to prepare $N \mathrm { . }$ doped biochars $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { N } }$ -biochars, NBCs), wherein organic nitrogen-containing compounds, such as urea, thiourea, and melamine, were widely used as nitrogen precursors with a high mass ratio between the dopant and the biomass [1719].

The hydrothermal treatment (HTT) route is a thermochemical process that employs high-moisture biomass sources, such as food waste, sewage sludge, and algae, without the requirement for pre-drying steps [20]. In this context, [21] used the HTT approach to produce $N \cdot$ carbonaceous materials as supercapacitors with high nitrogen contents $( 6 . 8 \mathrm { - } 9 . 0 ~ \% )$ . They found that higher temperatures led to increased C contents due to a loss of oxygen during the dehydration process; however, no specific nitrogen elimination was observed, imply that the N atoms incorporated in the HT carbon structure are bound relatively strongly. It has also been reported that N atoms can be doped into carbon structures under HT conditions by the reaction between $\mathrm { N H } _ { 3 }$ (released from an N dopant) and the O-containing groups of the carbon material [22]. It was therefore considered that post-synthetic pyrolysis could lead to increased SSA and higher degrees of aromatization, thereby tailoring the introduction of optimal pyridine-like N and quaternary N atoms into such carbon species at elevated pyrolysis temperatures [21]. However, to the best of our knowledge, HT-based nitrogen doping of sludgederived biochar $\mathrm { ( ^ { H T } N B C ) }$ using ammonium nitrate as the nitrogen source has yet to be reported.

Thus, based on the above considerations, we herein report the preparation of a high-quality NBC catalyst via HT co-carbonization from sewage sludge and $\mathrm { N H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 }$ , and the subsequent application of the desired ${ \mathrm { H T } } _ { \mathrm { N B C } }$ for the activation of PMS to degrade BPA. First, we scrutinize synthesizing conditions thoroughly to develop a promising NBC catalyst and unveil the catalytic center of N-BCs to PMS activation by the structure  activity relationship. Second, we explored the effects of pH, PMS, the water matrix, and scavengers on the removal of BPA in the HTNBC/PMS system. Moreover, degradation products and reactive species were identified by mass spectrometry and electron spin resonance (ESR), respectively, and the catalytic mechanism in the $^ \mathrm { H T } \mathrm { N B C } /$ PMS system was investigated. Finally, we evaluated the material's behavior by comparing fresh and spent catalyst structures, the ability to remove different organics and the PMS utilization efficiency. This work provided detailed insights into designing an effective NBC for PMS activation with a nonradical mechanism.

# .3.Evaluation of the catalytic performance

The batch BPA degradation experiments were conducted in $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ Pyrex vials covered with tinfoil at room temperature $( 2 2 \pm 2 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } )$ . More specifically, the catalyst $_ { ( 0 . 2 g / \mathrm { L } ) }$ was added to $1 0 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of BPA $( 0 . 1 \mathrm { m M } )$ at a constant stirring rate $( 5 0 0 \mathrm { r p m } )$ , as controlled by a magnetic stirrer. The desired volume of a PMS stock solution $( 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m M } ) }$ was then added dropwise to the reaction mixture to initiate the reaction. The reaction was conducted without pH adjustment; the initial $\mathsf { p H }$ of the reaction solution was $\sim 7 . 1$ , and this dropped to $\sim 3 . 2$ during the reaction. Aliquots (1 mL) were removed from the reaction mixture at specific time intervals and immediately quenched with a $0 . 2 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ sodium thiosulfate solution (1 mL). After filtering the obtained solutions through a $0 . 4 5 \mu \mathrm { M }$ PTFE syringe filter, they were collected immediately in amber HPLC vials. In addition, to determine the main reactive species responsible for the degradation of BPA, quenching experiments were conducted by adding an excess of the scavenger prior to the addition of PMS to the reaction mixture. As scavengers, methanol (MeOH), tert-butanol (TBA), sodium azide $\mathrm { ( N a N _ { 3 } ) }$ , and potassium iodide (KI) were employed. Furthermore, the evidence in the formation of reactive radical species was monitored using electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis and spintrapping agents, namely 5,5-dimethyl-pyrroline $N \mathrm { . }$ oxide (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidinol (TEMP) [23].

To test the reusability of the $^ { 1 8 0 } \mathrm { N _ { 2 } B C } { \cdot } 8 0 0$ catalyst, cyclic BPA degradation experiments were carried out under the optimal conditions. At the end of each cycle, the catalyst was collected by centrifugation $( 5 0 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ , $1 0 \mathrm { m i n } \dot { }$ without any additional regeneration steps. After the 3rd cycle, the $^ { 1 8 0 } \mathrm { N _ { 2 } B C } { \cdot } 8 0 0$ catalyst was regenerated by heat treatment $( 3 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ according to the method reported by Kang et al. [24].

All batch tests were performed in triplicate, and the average values and standard deviations are reported.

# 4. Conclusion

We herein reported the use of ammonium nitrate as a nitrogen doping source in sludge waste to obtain a superior $N$ doped biochar (NBC) catalyst by means of hydrothermal treatment coupled with pyrolysis for the first time. Following optimization of the synthetic conditions, $^ { 1 8 0 } \mathrm { N _ { 2 } B C } { \cdot } 8 0 0$ ( y $1 8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ pyrolyzed at $8 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , and prepared using a dopant/sludge mass ratio of 2:1) was found to be the preferred catalyst owing to its favorable structure and high catalytic activity. Thus, following the application of this catalyst to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS), $0 . 1 \mathrm { m M }$ BPA was removed within 10 min at a biochar dosage of $0 . 2 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ and over a wide $\mathtt { p H }$ range. It was found that the more graphitic-N, pyridinic N species and the adequate carbonyl groups in the $^ { 1 8 0 } \mathrm { N _ { 2 } B C } { \cdot } 8 0 0$ structure were primarily responsible for the activation of PMS. These structural characteristics were achieved using the optimal hydrothermal conditions to induce carbonyl group introduction, and a short but high-temperature pyrolysis step to promote the formation of graphitic-N. Consequently, the optimal NBC demonstrated a superb catalytic performance owing to synergy between the various active species. Oxidation mechanism studies verified that BPA degradation in NBC/PMS system was achieved mainly through nonradical $^ { - 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and electron transfer) pathway, with the auxiliary contribution of radicals. The declined activity of material over cycles could be ascribed to the transformation of graphitic N and pyridinic N to pyrrolic N. Further, efforts can be put into improving the stability of carboncatalysts. We believe that this work provides a novel understanding of how the structures of carbon species can be modified with the desired $N \mathrm { . }$ .
