# . Introduction

Contamination of soils associated with the use of pesticides and fertilizers is an issue of growing concern because of the potential risk to the environment and human health [1]. Lindane $\dot { \boldsymbol { \cdot } } \boldsymbol { \gamma }$ hexachlorocyclohexane, $\boldsymbol { \gamma } { \mathrm { - H C H } }$ is an organochlorinated pesticide, persistent in water and soil systems [2]. In 2009, this compound was listed in Annex A of the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants [3] and identified as a carcinogen in 2017. Although many countries have banned lindane production, its extensive use in the last decades and the uncontrolled disposal of wastes generated during its manufacture have caused many HCHs-polluted sites [2]. Due to the characteristics of this compound (low aqueous solubility, high persistence, low biodegradability, high toxicity, etc.) and the relatively high concentrations in soils (from 30 to $3 0 0 0 0 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { k g } ^ { - 1 }$ , most commonly below $2 0 0 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g }$ $\mathbf { k g } ^ { - 1 }$ [4]), it is necessary to reduce the potential serious harm to human health and environmental safety by effective remediation processes.

Different techniques, such as electro-kinetic soil flushing [5,6], biodegradation [710], chemical reduction [1114], and chemical oxidation [1517], have been studied for remediating HCHscontaminated soils. Persulfate advanced oxidation processes (PSAOPs) have shown great potential due to their high efficiency and environmental friendliness [18,19]. PS advanced oxidation processes present essential advantages, such as convenient preservation and transport of solid PS, stronger standard oxidation potential than other oxidants, wider pH range and higher stability than hydroxyl radicals, more selective and longer contact with pollutants, complete reaction, economic efficiency, simplicity and feasibility, and multifarious activation ways, which can effectively avoid the shortcomings of traditional oxidation technology [2022]. Moreover, it has been proved that the effects of activated persulfate on soil microorganisms are less damaging than those of hydrogen peroxide [23]. PS activation methods, including heat, ultraviolet light, ultrasonic wave, and the addition of alkali and transition metals, are commonly used to increase the generation of radicals, species with higher oxidising capacity than PS. The thermal activation of PS (PS/T) (Eq. (1), [20]) has shown promising results when treating HCHs-polluted soils [24]. At the optimal conditions $( 5 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ $\mathrm { C _ { P S } }$ $= 8 0 ~ \mathrm { g / L }$ , $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { L } } / \mathrm { W } _ { \mathrm { S } } = 2$ $1 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ , 9 days), $8 3 ~ \%$ of HCHs degradation was achieved after 9 days, with no chlorinated intermediate compounds.

The alkaline activation of PS $\mathrm { ( p H > 1 2 }$ intensified by temperature (PS/ NaOH/T) has emerged as a promising alternative [15,25]. In this system, sulfate, hydroxyl, and superoxide radicals are generated (Eqs. (2) and (3) [2628]), and temperature highly improves the kinetics of the process. At $\mathrm { p H } > 1 2$ , HCHs are hydrodechlorinated to trichlorobenzenes (TCBs), compounds with higher aqueous solubility and reactivity with the radicals than the parent pollutants [16]. The synergetic effect of alkali and temperature PS activation resulted in $9 4 ~ \%$ pollutant depletion in only 3 days [25].

HCHs' degradation and dechlorination on PS/T and PS/NaOH/T treatments have been thoroughly evaluated, and both showed high efficiency in terms of pollutant degradation [15,24,25]. However, the disappearance of organic pollutants does not mean the disappearance of soil toxicity [29]. On the other hand, although the impact of PS-remediation processes on the soil quality (pH decrease, salinity, and conductivity increase, oxidation and changes in the composition of soil organic matter, mobilization of heavy metals, changes in microbial communities, soil surface morphology, etc.) has been carefully reviewed [18,30,31], some aspects related to soil ecotoxicity after remediation processes remain unclear. Thus, studying the effect of oxidizers and activators on soil toxicity and biodegradability before implementing the processes would be very convenient.

Due to the easy operation and short detection time, acute toxicity is the most widely used method for evaluating the toxicity change during AOPs. Among these techniques, Microtox $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ bioassay, based on the measurement of the natural luminescence emitted by the bacteria Vibrio fischeri, is the most used since it is simple, inexpensive, and allows the analysis of a large number of samples [29,3234]. To comprehensively evaluate sample toxicity, several toxicity assessment methods should be used simultaneously [29]. Artemia salina, a bioassay using microcrustaceans, is recognized as an outstanding model organism for acute toxicity testing [35]. It is also considered a simple, inexpensive, and reliable short-term routine test [36,37]. Brine shrimp present a high reproduction rate and sensitivity to toxins. Finally, biodegradability, a key parameter to evaluate the biological risk of different samples, can be assessed by a rapid test using a real sludge from a wastewater treatment plant. This method provides results equivalent to those of the ZahnWellens test (OECD 302B 1992), significantly reducing the time required [3840].

Assessing the toxicity of aqueous phases (wastewater) during and after AOPs is generally well documented [29]. Regrettably, its relevance in soil remediation is poor. Recently, some studies have attempted to assess the toxicity of PS-treated soils using simulating software [41,42] (Table SM 4). These softwares (TOPKAT, TEST, and ECOSAR) are easy and valuable tools. However, they only consider the toxicity of the initial pollutants and the intermediates generated, a key aspect, without considering the interactions between the soil matrix and reagents.

On the other hand, soil remediation works involving experimental toxicity measures have usually focused on the reaction supernatant and not on the soil [43,44] (Table SM 4). The acute toxicity of soils contaminated with HCHs (Microtox® bioassay of soils, elutriates, and organic extracts) has been exhaustively determined in a recent work [45], but scarce information on the toxicity of remediated soils was reported [46,47]. To our knowledge, the Artemia salina test has also not been applied to assess soil toxicity. Finally, the rapid biodegradability test was developed for aqueous samples, but its application to solid samples has also not been described in the literature.

In this context, this work studies the feasibility of PS processes (PS/T and PS/NaOH/T) for the remediation of HCH-contaminated soils, ensuring the safety of the treated soils. For that purpose, the toxicity changes of HCHs-contaminated soils (including all the phases involved, i.e. soils, organic extracts, reaction supernatants, and soil elutriates) after PS-based treatment are considered using two acute toxicity assays (Vibrio fischeri and Artemia salina) and a rapid biodegradability test to realistically assess the potential effects of the chemical treatment on the soil. Our results can provide new insights into selecting efficient and green methods for treating HCHs-polluted soils.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Soil samples

The polluted soil sample was provided by SARGA (Sociedad Aragonesa de Gestión Agroambiental). It was taken at a depth between 0 and $0 . 3 \mathrm { m }$ in the most contaminated area of the Bailín landfill (surface soil). Soil samples were collected and milled in the landfill facilities and stored in plastic bags $( 4 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ darkness) until analysis (1530 days). The coarser particles of the soil (with low adsorption capacity) are usually removed by sieving, density, or gravity [48]. Thus, received soil samples (lots of at least $4 ~ \mathrm { k g } { \mathrm { . } }$ were sieved using an electromagnetic sieve shaker (BA200-N), and the fraction from 0.02 to $0 . 2 5 \mathrm { m m }$ (around $9 5 \%$ of the total soil sample) was selected for further experimentation. Once the sample is sieved and homogenized, a minimum of 3 subsamples were analyzed by gas chromatography to determine the concentration of HCHs and total organic carbon (following the protocols described in subsection 2.4). A standard deviation $< 1 0 ~ \%$ was found in these measurements. The values shown are the average of these measurements.

The distinction between physical interference and the effect of target pollutants on toxicity and biodegradability tests was performed using a reference soil equivalent in appearance and composition to the polluted sample but with a substantially lower concentration of organic pollutants [49]. This unpolluted soil was collected outside the perimeter of the contaminated sites at a depth similar to that used for the polluted sample.

# 2.3. Activated PS remediation experiments

Two types of PS activations were applied to remediate the HCHspolluted soil: PS activated by temperature (PS/T) [24] and PS activated by alkali addition and intensified by temperature (PS/NaOH/T) [25]. The experiments were conducted in $4 0 \mathrm { m L }$ -PTFE well-mixed batch reactors with PTFE screw caps. The mass relationship of the aqueous phase/soil $( \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { L } } / \mathrm { W } _ { \mathrm { S } } )$ was fixed at 2/1 [24]. Firstly, $_ { 1 5 g }$ of polluted soil was placed into the PTFE reactors. After that, the reagents (PS and $\mathrm { N a O H }$ , when appropriate) were simultaneously added from concentrated stock solutions (the rest of the volume up to $3 0 \mathrm { m L }$ was completed with milli-Q water), meaning the beginning of the experiment. The concentration of PS, temperature, and remediation time were chosen based on previous works in which the operating variables had been optimized [24,25]. The current work aims to evaluate the acute toxicity of soils after the remediation treatments. Therefore, under equivalent pollutant degradation conditions (with significant COCs conversions), different treatment times were selected for each process, considering the different degradation kinetics. In this sense, a treatment time of 17 days was selected for PS/T and 11 days for PS/NaOH/T. The NaOH to PS molar ratio used was 2 in PS/NaOH/T treatment, which ensures a pH above 12 [25,50].

The PS stoichiometric concentration $( \mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P S } , \mathrm { s t c } } )$ required for HCHs mineralization has been estimated considering: i) the moles of radicals generated in the thermal (Eq. (1)) and alkaline (Eqs. (2) and (3)) activations per mol of PS, i) the stoichiometry of COCs mineralization by the main radicals involved in each process (Eq. (4) in PS/T, and Eqs. (5) and (6) in PS/NaOH/T; in the last one, the oxidation of HCHs and TCBs is considered), ii) the aqueous to solid phase ratio $( \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { L } } / \mathrm { W } _ { \mathrm { S } } )$ , and iv) the initial pollutants concentration [24,25].

PS concentration $( 6 0 ~ g / \mathrm { L } )$ was significantly higher than the stoichiometric amount (Cps/CpSstoichiometric was 29.9 and 7.2 in PS/T and PS/ NaOH/T, respectively) to decrease the required reaction times, as usually found in the bibliography [15,5154].

The reactors were introduced in a constant-temperature bath (UNITRONIC-ORBITAL) equipped with a digital electronic temperature, time, and stirring rate regulator. Reactors were shaken at $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ and $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ At the final reaction time, 17 days in the case of PS/T and 11 days in the case of PS/NaOH/T days (according to the different rates of pollutant degradation associated with each process), the reactors were cooled in an ice water bath. Subsequently, solid and aqueous (reaction supernatant) phases were separated by centrifugation ( $\mathrm { 1 0 ~ m i n }$ ,9000 rpm, MEDTRONIC-BL-S, JP SELECTA®) and analyzed. Elutriates and organic extracts were also prepared from the treated soils. The remediation tests were conducted in duplicate, and the average value is represented in the figures (along with the standard deviation).

# 2.4. Analytical techniques

Investigating the leachability and mobility of residual pollutants is necessary to know the toxicity risk associated with the waters in contact with the treated soils (leachates). This aspect is particularly relevant when there are superficial waters near the contaminated site, as is the current case (the Gállego River surrounds the investigated landfill). For this reason, the polluted soil (3 subsamples, with a particle diameter from 0.02 to $0 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ and the corresponding elutriates (simulating a possible lixiviate of the landfill sediments) were characterized. Additionally, all the involved phases in the oxidation treatment were analysed, including the treated soils and the resulting aqueous phases (reaction supernatants and elutriates of the treated soils).

Soil elutriates (from initial and treated soils) were obtained by mixing $_ { 1 5 g }$ of soil with $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of milli-Q water in PTFE vials and stirred for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ $2 2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ $8 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ , rotary agitator, RR80, $\mathbf { L B X } ( \mathbf { \mathbb { R } } ) _ { . }$ ; time enough to reach equilibrium conditions [5557]. Finally, both phases (washed soil and elutriates) were separated by centrifugation.

Soil organic extracts (from initial and treated soils) were prepared by mixing $1 5 g$ of soil with $1 5 g$ of methanol in an ultrasound bath (Power Sonic 505) for $1 8 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ at $4 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ The vials were cooled until room temperature and centrifuged ( $\mathbf { \dot { 1 0 } } \mathbf { m i n }$ , $9 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ ). The resulting supernatant organic phase constitutes the soil organic extract.

The total organic carbon (TOC), inorganic carbon (IC), and metal concentrations of the soil were previously determined [15]. Soil pHs were measured from a soilwater suspension $( \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { L } } / \mathrm { W } _ { \mathrm { S } } = 2 )$ using a Basic 20-CRISON pH electrode. COCs concentration in the soil samples was determined by gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 6890) coupled with flame ionization (FID) and electron capture (ECD) detectors after extraction with methanol [24,25,56,58]. More details regarding the chromatographic method can be found in previous studies [15,50,59].

COCs concentration in the aqueous phases (reaction supernatants and soil elutriates) was determined by GC/FID/ECD after liquidliquid extraction with hexane (1/1 mass ratio) [24,25]. PS quantification was performed using a spectrophotometric method [60] by adding the sample to a prepared water, $/ \mathrm { K I } / \mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ solution. The iodine yellow colour formed was measured at $3 5 2 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ (BOECO S-20 UV-VIS spectrophotometer). The COCs' dechlorination was evaluated regarding chlorides released to the aqueous phase, quantified by ion chromatography (Metrohm 761 Compact IC) with chemical suppression coupled to a conductivity detector. A Metrosep A SUPP5 5250 column was used as the stationary phase, and $3 . 2 \ \mathrm { m m o l / L }$ of $\mathrm { N a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 }$ and $1 \ \mathrm { m m o l / L }$ of $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ were used as the mobile phase.

# 2.5.Toxicity and biodegradability evaluation

The acute toxicity (Microtox $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ and Artemina salina) and biodegradability (rapid biodegradability test) of the unpolluted and polluted soils and their corresponding elutriates were determined. The results were compared with those obtained after applying the remediation treatments (PS/T and PS/NaOH/T). All analyses were duplicated (error bars, standing for standard deviation, have been included in the figures).

# 2.5.1. Toxicity towards Vibrio fischeri

The Microtox $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ bioassay has been used to determine the acute toxicity of soils (unpolluted, polluted, and treated soils), elutriates $( \mathrm { V _ { L } } /$ ${ \sf W } _ { \mathrm { S } } = 2$ $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ ) and reaction supernatants. This test is based on measuring the natural luminescence emitted by the marine bacterium Vibrio fischeri. The exposure of these bacteria to toxic samples disrupts their respiratory processes, reducing the emitted light, which can be directly correlated with the samples toxicity [61]. Toxicity analyses were carried out in triplicate using a Microtox $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ M500 Analyzer (Microbics Corporation, USA). The guidelines of Doe, Jackman, Scroggins, McLeay and Wohlgeschaffen [32] were followed to evaluate the data quality obtained. The test was considered not valid if any of the following conditions were not fulfilled: i) monotonous decrease of the light loss as the test concentration decreases, ii) coefficient of variation of the light readings $< 1 2 \%$ in the controls and iii) coefficient of determination $( \mathtt { R } ^ { 2 } )$ $> 0 . 9 $ Considering the nature of the sample (soils, soil organic extracts, and aqueous phases), different sample preparation procedures and standard protocols have been employed.

The Microtox $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ Basic Test (BT) [62] was followed to determine the acute toxicity of aqueous phases (soil elutriates and reaction supernatants). The pH of the samples was adjusted (6.08.0) due to the bacterial reagent sensitivity to pH [62]. In the case of reaction supernatants, the remanent concentration of PS was removed (with thiosulfate TiS) to avoid interferences with the toxicity results (the stoichiometric amount of TiS for the complete PS removal was added and agitated for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ [63]). Subsequently, $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of each diluted sample was added to the next vial (concentration $5 0 ~ \%$ of the previous one), achieving a maximum sample concentration of $4 5 \ \%$ . The adapted Organic Solvent Sample Solubilization Test was performed considering the hydrophobic nature of soil pollutants (HCH isomers) and their limited bioavailability for soil toxicity determination [62]. # 2.5.2. Toxicity towards Artemia salina

The study of the acute toxicity of soils was completed with Artemia salina, which involves exposing brine shrimp to the tested substance and observing their mortality rate. The procedure followed was based on previous works [36,37,67]. Firstly, Artemia salina larvae were obtained. The egg-hatching process consisted of adding $_ { 0 . 5 ~ g }$ of eggs (Artemio $\mathrm { P u r @ }$ in a beaker containing $2 3 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of previously prepared synthetic seawater ${ 3 5 } \mathrm { g / L }$ of Marinium $^ { \mathrm { \textregistered } }$ salt). The beaker was placed on an IKC RCT Basic hot plate for temperature control $( 3 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ . A neutral pH value, uninterrupted oxygenation, and light exposure were maintained for the egg-hatching process. Under these conditions, the eggs hatched within $^ { 2 4 \mathrm { h } , }$ and the Artemia salina in the larval stage (1 to $2 \mathrm { m m }$ ) were used to determine the acute toxicity of samples.

The preparation of the samples to determine the acute toxicity was accomplished following standard conditions: $0 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of concentrated saline water $( 2 8 0 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ of Marinium $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ salt), $1 . 5 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of the sample $\mathrm { ( p H = }$ 7) at different initial concentrations and $0 . 2 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of the solution containing 20 Artemia salina nauplii were added to $2 \ \mathrm { m L }$ vials. Five serial dilutions are prepared with three replicates of each test concentration and the control. Vials were placed in a Julabo SW22 water bath at $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under dark conditions. A recent review has highlighted that the most promising protocols to standardize the use of Artemia salina are the mortality tests after $2 4 { \ - } 4 8 \ \mathrm { h }$ of exposure [68]. Therefore, in the present experiments, 24-hour-old nauplii were exposed to the polluted samples for $^ { 4 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The mortality of the microcrustacean was monitored by checking the number of dead individuals by visual inspection. Microscopic images of alive and dead brine shrimp are summarised in Figure SM 1, observing different colours and textures. To facilitate the results obtained by Vibrio fischeri and Artemia salina, the mortality results obtained in the different serial dilutions of each sample analysed have been subsequently treated, obtaining the corresponding lethal concentration values $\mathrm { ( L C _ { 5 0 } ) }$ (defined as the concentration of the compound that kills $5 0 ~ \%$ of the nauplii within $4 8 \mathrm { { h } }$ ).

The test has been considered valid if the following conditions were fulfilled: the percentage mortality in control did not exceed $1 0 \%$ ,and at least three data were situated in the mortality range of $5 \mathrm { - } 9 5 \%$ (if not the case, the test was repeated with additional dilutions) [36]. As an example, the results obtained from the count of live and dead brine shrimp for an elutriate sample analyzed (PS/T treatment) are shown in Table SM 1, and the graphical representation of the percentage of mortality as a function of sample concentration is shown in Figure SM 2. As can be seen, the requirements mentioned above are fulfilled, and the corresponding $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ value can be determined.

The toxicity of soils, soil organic extracts, and soil elutriates (prepared as indicated in subsection 2.4) was measured, and the following deviations from the standard method were accomplished. An aqueous soil (particle diameter $< 0 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ suspension (slurry, $7 . 0 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L } )$ was prepared for soil sample analysis. As in the case of the Microtox $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ analyses, the PS-treated soils were water-washed and neutralized (if necessary) before the measurements. For the soil organic extracts evaluation, preliminary tests were carried out to determine the maximum concentration of methanol at which no Artemia salina nauplii death was observed after $4 8 \mathrm { h }$ of exposure, obtaining a solvent concentration of 0.9 $\%$ (vol.), which is in the same range previously reported in the literature [68,69]. Thus, concentrations below this level will not interfere with the effects of the compound under study. Finally, in the case of soil elutriates, the remaining oxidant was removed as previously indicated. The $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ results obtained have been expressed in the following units: soil $( \mathrm { g } _ { \mathrm { s o i l } } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ , elutriate $( \mathbf { g _ { s o i l } \mathbf { m L } ^ { - 1 } } )$ , and organic extract $( \mathrm { g } _ { \mathrm { s o i l } } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ , equivalent to those used for the $\operatorname { E C } _ { 5 0 }$ values obtained in the Microtox® analyses.

# 2.5.3. Biodegradability

Rapid biodegradability assays [3840] were carried out to assess the short-term biodegradability of initial and treated samples (soil and elutriates). Soils elutriates were prepared following the procedures indicated in subsection 2.4. Before biodegradability analysis, soils were pretreated the same way as for acute toxicity assessment (removal of remaining PS and pH adjustment). Unacclimated activated sludge was taken from a local municipal wastewater treatment plant (WTTP) in Madrid (Spain). Before the rapid biodegradability assays, the collected sludge was aerated overnight without external substrate addition to eliminate residual organic matter and avoid oxygen limitation. The tests were conducted in glass reactors $\mathrm { ( 1 0 0 ~ m L ) }$ filled with $9 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of endogenous activated sludge and $5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of the target sample (a soilwater suspension, $2 0 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ for initial and treated soils, and soil elutriates) under a magnetic stirring. Subsequently, the dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration was monitored using an oximeter (HI9142, Hanna Instruments) as a function of the experimental time. Sample biodegradability $( \% )$ was calculated as a relation between the rapid biochemical oxygen demand $\mathrm { ( B O D _ { r a p i d } ) }$ and the chemical oxygen demand $\mathrm { ( C O D _ { s a m p l e } ) }$ ,considering the volume of the biological reactor $\left( 1 0 0 \mathrm { m L } \right)$ and the sample $\mathrm { ( 5 } \mathrm { m L ) }$ (Eq. (12)). The $\mathrm { \Delta B O D _ { r a p i d } }$ is estimated considering the difference, in terms of dissolved oxygen (DO) decay $( \mathbf { m g } \ 0 _ { 2 } \ \mathbf { d m } ^ { - 3 } )$ , between the slope of primary decay associated with endogenous consumption and the slope when sample is added into endogenous activated sludge. The sample addition usually provokes an increased DO consumption rate associated with the consumption of the organics contained.

$$
\mathrm { \cal { R a p i d b i o d e g r a d a b i l i t y } } ( \mathrm { \% } ) = \frac { \mathrm { \bf { B O D } } _ { r a p i d } \mathrm { \bf { \hat { A } } } { \cdot } { \bf V } _ { \mathrm { b i o l o g i c a l r e a c t o r } } } { \mathrm { \bf { C O D } } _ { \mathrm { s a m p l e } } \mathrm { \bf { \hat { A } } } { \cdot } { \bf V } _ { \mathrm { s a m p l e } } } 
$$

COD concentration was determined by acid oxidation with $\mathrm { K _ { 2 } C r _ { 2 } O _ { 7 } }$ using the COD closed reflux micro method according to Standard Methods for the Examination of Water and Wastewater [70]. The absorbance of the digestate was measured $( \lambda = 6 0 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ and compared with a standard solution of potassium hydrogen phthalate.

# 3.2. Oxidation treatments evaluation

The concentration of COCs in the treated soils and the aqueous phases (reaction supernatants and soil elutriates) has been included in Fig. 1. Both treatments significantly reduced pollutant concentration in the soil, but notable differences have been found between them.

As can be seen, the residual COCs concentration in the PS/NaOH/T treated soil $( 8 . 7 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { k g } ^ { - 1 } )$ was significantly lower than in the case of the PS/T system $( 4 4 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { k g } ^ { - 1 } )$ . Conversely, a COCs concentration of 3.1 and $1 . 8 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ was measured for the PS/NaOH/T and PS/T reaction supernatants, respectively. The nature of the pollutants or the intermediate compounds generated in both cases can explain this fact. In the case of the soil remediated by the thermal activation of PS, in which sulfate radicals are the main oxidizing species, only HCHs were detected at the end of the treatment, in agreement with previous results [24]. It was demonstrated that at temperatures $\geq 5 0 ^ { \mathrm { ~ \circ } } \mathrm { C } ,$ HCHs conversion and dechlorination profiles are almost coincident, indicating the absence of chlorinated organic compounds in significant amounts. Only 1,2-dichlorobenzene and chlorobenzene were identified in this system, showing a maximum concentration at 57 days and completely degraded from 9 treatment days [24]. On the other hand, it should be noted that the ratio $\alpha$ -HCH/βHCH decreased from 7.5 (polluted soil) to 3.3 after the remediation treatment, highlighting the higher refractoriness of $\beta$ -HCH [15,24,56]. Consequently, the elutriate of this treated soil was composed basically of ${ \mathfrak { X } } ^ { . }$ -HCH $\mathrm { 1 . 6 \ m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ , a value close to its solubility limit), with higher solubility than $\mathrm { \beta - H C H }$ .

In the case of the soil treated by PS/NaOH/T, the primary pollutants detected in the soil and the aqueous phases were trichlorobenzenes (TCBs), which agrees with the results previously published, as long as it is ensured that the pH of the solution remains above 12 [25]. These compounds are rapidly generated under alkaline conditions due to the hydrodechlorination of HCHs [15,25,56] and released to the aqueous phase (due to their relatively high solubility $\left( 3 1 - 3 9 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } \right.$ for 124-TCB, and $1 6 { - } 1 8 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ for 123-TCB) [15,57,71,72]). Thus, the concentration of HCHs in the treated soil was negligible, except for $\beta .$ HCH $_ { [ 0 . 9 \mathrm { ~ m g } }$ $\mathbf { k g } ^ { - 1 } )$ , highlighting again the refractoriness of this compound, also towards hydrolysis reaction [15,25,57]. Thus, the slightly higher COCs concentration in the supernatant of PS/NaOH/T $3 . 1 \mathrm { \ m g \ L ^ { - 1 } } .$ than PS/T $( 1 . 8 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ at the final reaction times of each treatment was explained by the higher solubility of TCBs than HCHs [71,73]. The low concentration of COCs in the treated soil elutriate $\left( 0 . 6 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ can be associated with the possible loss of TCBs (the only remaining pollutants) from the aqueous phase due to the high volatility of these compounds [7476]. In the case of PS-T treatment, $\alpha \cdot$ HCH (non-volatile compound) remained in the aqueous phase, with values close to its solubility limit.

Fig. 2 shows the conversion of $\alpha$ HCH and $\mathrm { \beta { - } H C H }$ isomers and total COCs in the soil phase and the conversion of PS and the dechlorination degree $\mathrm { ( C l ^ { - } / C l _ { 0 } ) }$ , determined in the aqueous phase for the PS/T and PS/ NaOH/T treatments at final reaction time.

As previously stated, COCs degradation was very high in both cases, demonstrating the high efficiency of the two treatments under the selected operating conditions. A slightly higher COCs removal was achieved with the alkaline activation of PS intensified by temperature than with the thermal activation: $9 7 . 8 \%$ vs $8 9 . 1 \%$ (Fig. 2). Moreover, it is worth noting that the reaction time was shorter in treatment PS/ NaOH/T than in PS/T (11 and 17 days, respectively). The higher conversion of pollutants in the case of the PS/NaOH/T system was associated with the alkaline conditions used $\left( \mathrm { p H } \geq 1 2 \right)$ , at which HCHs are hydrodechlorinated to TCBs [15,25,77]. Thus, the conversion of HCHs in this remediation treatment was almost complete ( $1 0 0 \%$ and $9 8 \%$ for $\alpha$ HCH and $\beta$ HCH, respectively). As previously stated, the intermediate compounds from HCHs hydrolysis (TCBs) have higher solubility and reactivity with the radical species than the parent pollutants [16].

# 3.3. Acute toxicity assessment

# 3.3.1. Vibrio fischeri

The selection of the remediation treatment should not be entirely based on the contaminant removal efficacy but also on the toxicity reduction of all the phases involved in the treatment. The acute toxicity of the polluted soil (soil phase, elutriate and organic extract) and after applying the oxidation treatments (treated soils, organic extracts, reaction supernatants, and soil elutriates, the last ones of great importance as they simulate superficial soil leachates) has been evaluated with the Vibrio fischeri bacteria.

The acute toxicity results obtained (in terms of $\mathrm { E C } _ { 5 0 . }$ have been grouped in Table SM 3. Likewise, the toxicity value corresponding to the unpolluted soil $( \mathbf { C } _ { \mathrm { C O C s } } < 1 \mathbf { m g } \mathbf { k g } ^ { - 1 } )$ has been included as a reference (Ref). The acute toxicity of $\mathrm { R e f } + \mathrm { N a O H }$ was equivalent to that of the PS/NaOH/T-treated soil. The $\mathrm { v } _ { \mathrm { L } } / \mathrm { w } _ { \mathrm { S } }$ ratio used in the remediation treatments (2) and the NaOH excess could also contribute to the acute toxicity increase in the alkaline reaction supernatant.

The concentration of COCs in the organic extracts of polluted and treated soils is collected in Fig. 1-a (coc,soil, mg $\mathbf { k g } ^ { - 1 } )$ . The results obtained (expressed as $1 / \mathrm { E C } _ { 5 0 , \mathrm { { s o i l } } } ,$ in $\operatorname { L } _ { \mathrm { M e O H } } \mathbf { g } _ { s \mathrm { o i l } } ^ { - 1 } ,$ Eq. (11), Microtox $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ aOsssT) before and after oxidation treatments are shown in Fig. 3-b. The toxicity of the reference soil, with low COCs concentration $( < 1 \mathrm { \ m g \ k g ^ { - 1 } } )$ , is negligible, whereas that of the polluted soil $( \mathrm { C _ { C O C s } } = 4 0 9 \mathrm { \ m g \ k g ^ { - 1 } ) }$ is significantly higher (it has not been feasible to classify these samples as there is no toxicity classification for organic extracts in the literature). The differences between polluted and reference soil toxicity are more noteworthy than those of the soil phase (Fig. 3-a). This finding can be explained as most of the COCs in soil are extracted to the organic phase, highlighting that the aOsssT method ensures the total availability of the pollutants [66]. Moreover, the absence of acute toxicity in the organic extract of the reference soil confirms that the residual toxicity of the soil (Fig. 3-a) is not attributed to the organic pollutants (they are not solubilized into the organic phase).

As can be seen, the trend obtained for soil toxicity reduction after the remediation treatments was similar to that of the soil phase (Fig. 3-a). Both treatments led to a significant toxicity reduction in the soil organic extracts (PS/NaOH/T $( 3 2 ~ \% )$ and PS/T $( 8 4 ~ \% )$ The higher toxicity value of PS/NaOH/T than PS/T treatment (Fig. 3-b) can be justified by the TCBs remaining in the treated soil $( \mathrm { C _ { T C B s } } = 7 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ k g _ { s o i l } ^ { - 1 } } ,$ Fig. 1-a) and, therefore, in the organic extract. In the case of PS/T, only HCHs $( \mathrm { C _ { H C H s } } = 4 4 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ k g _ { s o i l } ^ { - 1 } } )$ remained in the treated soil, with significantly lower acute toxicity towards Vibrio fischeri bacteria [45]. Zhang, Zhou, Liu, Zhang, Xu, Lin, He, and Ouyang [46] analyzed the organic extract of lindane-contaminated soil after CaO-ball milling treatment (Table SM 4). Although a significant decrease in lindane concentration in soils was obtained $( \mathrm { X } _ { \mathrm { l i n d a n e } } > 9 0 \ \% )$ , a slight decrease in acute toxicity was achieved.

Based on the acute toxicity results obtained towards Vibrio fischeri bacteria, PS/T treatment was preferable to PS/NaOH/T. This treatment reduced the soil toxicity to the levels of the reference sample. In addition, the aqueous reaction phase (reaction supernatants and soils elutriates) and organic extracts did not exhibit toxicity, facilitating their management. In addition, essential soil characteristics, such as pH and TOC, were unaffected after the remediation treatment. However, to realistically assess the potential effects of chemical oxidation treatment on soil, it is recommended to use multiple toxicity assays [30]. In this context, further toxicity tests have been applied to evaluate the toxicity after treatments.

# 3.3.2. Artemia salina

Artemia salina has been used to assess the acute toxicity of studied soils further. The high reproduction rate and sensitivity of brine shrimp to toxins make it a suitable organism for these types of samples. The acute toxicity results obtained (in terms of $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 . }$ after $4 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ of exposure have been grouped in Table SM 5. To facilitate the comparison between polluted and treated samples, Fig. 5 collects the toxicity values expressed as $1 / \mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ for a) soil, b) aqueous and c) organic phases.

The concentration of contaminated soil that produces a $5 0 ~ \%$ mortality is lower in the case of Artemia salina $( \mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 } .$ , Table SM 5) compared to the bioluminescence inhibition of Vibrio fischeri $[ \mathrm { E C } _ { 5 0 } .$ , Table SM 3) for the different phases involved, indicating that Artemia salina shows greater sensitivity to the studied contaminants (HCHs and chlorobenzenes) under the conditions tested.

Samples corresponding to polluted soil (soil, elutriate and organic extract, Fig. 5) exerted significant toxicity. Minimal information was found in the literature regarding the toxicity of these compounds to Artemia salina. Canton, Wegman, Vulto, Verhoef and van Esch [80] reported that the $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ of $\mathbf { \alpha { \mathrm { \alpha } d C H } }$ in the aqueous phase was $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ . However, no results have been found in the literature for polluted soils or organic extracts. Additionally, the limited results presented in the literature are not directly comparable because of the uniformity in the experimental conditions employed (no international standards for hatching, swimming, immobilization, mortality, exposure conditions and data presentation), highlighting that the variability in the bibliography limits the comparison and classification of the results obtained [68].

Unlike Vibrio fischeri toxicity results (Fig. 3-a), the soil matrix does not show acute toxicity towards Artemia salina (Fig. 5-a), as the toxicity value of the reference soil was negligible in this case. After applying both treatments (PS/T and PS/NaOH/T), toxicity decreases considerably in the soil phase (Fig. 5-a), achieving values equivalent to that of the reference soil. As for soil elutriates, toxicity was significantly reduced after oxidation treatments (Fig. 5-b). It is worth noting that a slightly higher toxicity value was detected in the case of PS/T-treated soil elutriate than that corresponding to PS/NaOH/T, unlike in the case of the Microtox bioassay (Fig. 4). The higher pollutant concentration of the PS/T treatment and different pollutant nature could explain this result: $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { C O C s } } = 1 . 6 \ \mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ , mainly ${ \mathfrak { X } } ^ { \mathfrak { - } }$ -HCH, vs $\mathrm { C _ { C O C s } } = 0 . 6 \ \mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ , mainly 1,2,4- TCB, Fig. 1-c. Thus, in contrast to Vibrio fischeri, it seems that increasing the number of Cl in the molecule increases acute toxicity [81], being HCHs more toxic than TCBs towards this organism (Artemia salina), as occurred with other crustaceans, such as Cladocera [82]. In addition, it should be noted that due to the high volatility of TCBs, the concentration of contaminants could not be guaranteed to remain constant during the relatively long test time $( 4 8 \mathrm { h } )$ . The higher sensitivity of Artemia salina towards HCHs than Vibrio fischeri could also be explained by the high water filtration rate of Artemia salina organisms, being, thus, more susceptible to pollution exposure than other aquatic species [35].

Finally, the toxicity of the soil organic extract (Fig. 5-c) was also significantly reduced after the application of both treatments, although they did not reach the reference value. A slightly more significant reduction in elutriates toxicity was obtained in the case of the alkaline than the thermal treatment, in agreement with the lower COCs concentration in that organic phase $( 8 . 7 \ \nu s \ 4 4 . 4 \ m g \ k g ^ { - 1 }$ ,Fig. 1-a).

Considering all the above, both oxidation treatments (PS/T and PS/ NaOH/T) successfully reduce the acute toxicity of the soils to levels close to those of the reference (unpolluted soil), with slightly better results in the case of the PS/NaOH/T.

# 3.4. Biodegradability evaluation

The biodegradability of samples is commonly evaluated regarding the BOD to COD ratio. In this work, short-term biodegradability tests were carried out by measuring the dissolved oxygen uptake of nonacclimated activated sludge when in contact with soils (Figure SM 5) and soils elutriates (Figure SM 6) before and after chemical treatments (PS/T and PS/NaOH). The results corresponding to the reference soil $( \mathrm { C O C s } < 1 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { k g } ^ { - 1 } \cdot$ have been included in these figures for comparison. The COD values of these samples have also been indicated.

The variability of sludge characteristics complicates the comparison with other works. Nevertheless, this method is adequate for comparing samples analyzed using the same activated sludge and under the same experimental conditions, as has been done in this study. Soil biodegradability results obtained and expressed as $\%$ (Eq. (12)) are represented in Fig. 6. As can be seen, the biodegradability calculated for the polluted soil $( 4 6 . 6 ~ \% )$ was lower than that of the reference soil $( 7 6 \% )$ due to the higher oxygen decay and lower COD values in the absence of pollutants (Figure SM 5). After the remediation treatments (PS/T and PS/NaOH/T), the biodegradability of the soil increases (61.6 and 66.5 $\%$ to values close to the reference sample. Even though the biodegradability differences found between soils are not especially significant (the biodegradability of reference soil is $3 0 ~ \%$ higher than that of polluted one) since they could be overcome during the long-term natural attenuation, it can be assured that the chemical treatments tested (PS/T and PS/NaOH/T) do not decrease the biodegradability of soils. Thus, it is highlighted that neither reagent residues remaining in the soil after oxidation treatments nor interactions of the reagents with the soil matrix are detrimental. Therefore, although more research is required, the results obtained here cast a promising potential for combining activated persulfate and aerobic biodegradation for soil and groundwater remediation.

# 4. Conclusions

The PS-activated treatments (PS/T and PS/NaOH/T) have proven effective in degrading HCHs in real soil systems. Under specific conditions, the PS/T treatment $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P S } } = 6 0 \ { \mathrm { g / L } }$ , $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { L } } / \mathrm { W } _ { \mathrm { S } } = 2$ , 17 days) removed over $9 0 \ \%$ of HCHs without generating chlorinated intermediates. The acute toxicity (Vibrio fischeri and Artemia salina) of both soil and organic extract decreased to the levels of uncontaminated soil, whereas the aqueous leachate did not exhibit toxicity. In contrast, the PS/NaOH/T treatment $[ \mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P S } } = 6 0 \mathrm { g / L }$ , molar $\mathrm { N a O H } { \mathrm { : P S } } = 2$ , $5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ $\mathrm { V _ { L } } / \mathrm { W _ { S } }$ $= 2$ , 11 days), with $9 8 \%$ of COCs removal, showed controversial results. While Vibrio fischeri's acute soil toxicity was not significantly reduced (a more thorough soil washing after alkaline treatment is necessary), the treated soil (and the corresponding aqueous and organic extracts) resulted non-toxic towards Artemia salina. Adding reagents (PS and NaOH) and by-products generation did not increase acute soil toxicity. Both chemical treatments (PS/T and PS/NaOH/T) increased the biodegradability of soils (and leachates), showing high potential for a fullscale application. Thus, our results highlight the need for a multiparameter assessment of the effects of chemical treatments on the acute toxicity of soils, as one-sided analyses may yield biased results. Evaluating the toxicity of HCHs contaminated and treated soils (and associated extracts) considering other aquatic organisms, such as Daphnia magna, and the chronic toxicity of these soils is highly recommended for future works. In the same line, soil enzyme activity assays could reveal valuable information on the biodegradability of the soils.
