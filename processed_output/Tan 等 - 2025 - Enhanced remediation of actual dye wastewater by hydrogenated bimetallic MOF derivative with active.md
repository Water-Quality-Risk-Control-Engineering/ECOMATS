# Enhanced remediation of actual dye wastewater by hydrogenated bimetallic MOF derivative with active peracetic acid

Shengmei Tan1,4, Liuwei Zhang2,4, Zhiliang Cheng1 , Yuxin Peng1 , Jinshan Tang1 , Hongrui Zhu3 & Facheng Qiu1

This study introduces a hydrogenated FeCo-MOF derivative catalyst $\scriptstyle ( { \mathsf { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } } } )$ to activate peracetic acid (PAA) for efficient degradation dye wastewater. Rhodamine B (RhB) as a model pollutant, the HC-FeCo@C350/PAA achieved a $9 9 . 3 3 \%$ removal efficiency within $2 0 \mathsf { m i n }$ , with a rate constant 18 times higher than the non-hydrogenated system. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and the toxicity evaluation software (T.E.S.T.) confirmed reduced intermediate toxicity. Quenching experiments, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) tests, and density functional theory (DFT) calculation show that $H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 }$ can effectively activate PAA to produce $^ 1 { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ , $\mathsf { C H } _ { 3 } \mathsf { C } ( \mathsf { O } ) \mathsf { O } ^ { \bullet }$ , and $\mathsf { C H } _ { 3 } \mathsf { C } ( \mathsf { O } ) \mathsf { O } \mathsf { O } \bullet$ . The magnetically recoverable catalyst maintained over $80 \%$ efficiency after 4 cycles. In actual dye wastewater treatment, the RE of chemical oxygen demand (COD), ammonia nitrogen $( N H _ { 3 } - N )$ , total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP) could reach approximately $5 3 . 5 7 \%$ , $5 3 . 2 3 \%$ , $2 9 . 2 5 \%$ , $1 9 . 2 \%$ , respectively, with reduced toxicity validated by mungbean germination experiment. This technology offers a promising approach for refractory dye wastewater treatment and reuse.

In the past few years, dye wastewater has been extensively utilized across various sectors, including the textile, paper, leather, and hair dye industries, as well as in food, agriculture, and other research fields1 , and are known for their high color intensity, pollution, toxicity, and poor biodegradability2 . Rhodamine B (RhB) is a significant water-soluble dye that poses substantial risks to aquatic environments3 . Its direct release can lead to respiratory, tissue, and reproductive damage, and may even induce carcinogenic effects in aquatic organisms4 . Furthermore, RhB adversely affects human health and the broader ecosystem. Due to its non-biodegradability and toxicity5 , RhB is a serious threat to humans, particularly as it accumulates through the food chain and contaminates domestic water supply systems. Hence, it is imperative to investigate the treatment methodologies for dye wastewater. Current approaches to treating dye wastewater, including photocatalysis, adsorption, and membrane treatment6 . However, these methods are limited by factors such as slow processing speeds, high costs, and site limitations3 . Consequently, there is an urgent need for more effective and economical solutions to mitigate the environmental pollution caused by these dyes7 .

Advanced Oxidation Processes (AOPs) have broad application prospects in treating dye wastewater8 . Free radicals with strong oxidizing properties, such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and sulfate radicals $( \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { - } \bullet )$ , as well as other potent oxidizing radicals, can be generated in situ9 . Additionally, non-radicals, including singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } )$ and high-valent iron ions $( \mathrm { F e ( I V ) , C o ( I V ) ) ^ { 1 0 } }$ , can also be produced. Advanced oxidation technology of peracetic acid (PAA-AOPs) have garnered increasing interest because of their lower O-O the bond dissociation energy of $5 9 \mathrm { k J } \cdot \mathrm { m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ , compared to hydrogen peroxide, which has a higher dissociation energy. PAA-AOPs are capable of generating highly R-O• species $\mathrm { { C H _ { 3 } C ( O ) O \bullet } }$ and $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C } ( \mathrm { O } ) \mathrm { O O } { \bullet } ) ^ { 1 1 , 1 2 }$ . Peracetic acid (PAA), a type of organic peroxy acid, serves a common role as both a disinfectant and a fungicide, even though it is recognized for its potent and irritating smell13. The degradation products of PAA have attracted considerable attention as an alternative to chlorine disinfection because of their lower toxicity and reduced formation of disinfection by-products14, as well as their extensive microbiological efficacy and cost-effectiveness. PAA can be effectively activated through various methods, including ultrasound, ultraviolet (UV) light, and transition metals $( \mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + } , \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } , \mathrm { M n } ^ { 2 + } )$ , leading to the generation of radicals and non-radicals that are highly effective for the degradation and mineralization of target pollutants. In the transition metal-activated PAA reaction15, R-O• species play a significant role. Therefore, the utilization of peracetic acid in advanced oxidation processes represents a significant and effective approach to the degradation of dye wastewater.

At present, numerous scientists have identified a cooperative bimetallic system in composite catalysts featuring metal cations with adjustable valence, including $\mathrm { F e / C u ^ { 1 6 } }$ , $\mathrm { F e / M n ^ { 1 7 } }$ , $\mathrm { F e / B i ^ { 1 8 } }$ , $\mathrm { F e / M o ^ { 1 9 } }$ , etc. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), which consist of metal ions and organic ligands, offer numerous benefits such as the customizable structures, the high porosity, and the large specific surface area20. Bimetallic MOFs exhibit a stronger synergistic effect and enhanced structural stability compared to their monometallic counterparts. Generally, based on metal composition, MOFs can be mainly divided into monometallic MOFs and polymetallic MOFs. Compared with mono-metallic MOFs, the morphology and resulting magnetic properties of bimetallic MOFs can be customized more flexibly and efficiently by changing the proportion of metal components. Unlike mono-metallic MOFs, bimetallic MOFs contain two different metal ions in their structure. This results in a greater abundance of catalytically active sites and adsorption sites21. In addition, MOF-derived bimetallic catalysts typically exhibit exceptional thermal and chemical stability. The two metal components are highly dispersed and uniformly mixed within the framework, effectively mitigating the issue of metal agglomeration commonly observed in traditional synthesis methods. This uniform dispersion not only enhances the accessibility of active sites but also significantly improves the catalytic activity and long-term stability of the material22. In summary,

MOF-derived bimetallic catalysts leverage the structural advantages of MOF precursors, such as high surface area and tunable porosity, while harnessing the synergistic effects of bimetallic systems. These unique characteristics make them highly promising for a wide range of catalytic applications. Consequently, utilizing bimetallic MOFs as precursors for catalyst preparation demonstrates significant benefits in the treatment of dye effluents.

In this work, hydrogenated FeCo-MOF derivative catalyst HC$\mathrm { F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ is proposed for the efficient degradation of actual dye wastewater. The main active substances and intermediate products in the system were studied, and the toxicity of the degradation process was analyzed. The results show that $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ catalyst provides an environmentally friendly and effective method for the catalytic decomposition of organic pollutants. This research is helpful to solve the problem of dye wastewater pollution, reduce its harm to the environment and biology, and has potential application value in textile, paper, leather, and other industries that produce dye wastewater, and is one of the effective ways to solve the actual dye wastewater pollution problem.

# Control experiments

The experiments to be controlled were summarized in Fig. 4a, b. The HC$\mathrm { F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ catalyst demonstrated minimal RhB adsorption in the absence of PAA. Furthermore, RhB degradation reached only $4 . 3 4 \%$ within $4 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ when PAA was present, which is primarily due to the insufficient production of reactive species (ROS and R-O• species) by PAA, as well as the absence of additional substances to enhance its activity. In contrast, the degradation efficiency was only $2 . 1 2 \%$ in $4 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ when $_ { \mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 } }$ was added to the RhB solution, indicating that this is a non-homogeneous system.

The association of PAA with $H C \mathrm { - F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ notably enhanced the constant rate, achieving a constant activity of $0 . 5 3 4 8 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , as illustrated in Fig. 4b. This observation underscores the congenerous effects of Fe and $\mathrm { C o }$ in activating PAA. The effective activation of PAA by the H $[ \mathrm { C - F e C o } @ \mathrm { C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ catalyst resulted in the generation of increased amounts of ROS and R-O• species, leading to a remarkable degradation rate of $9 9 . 3 3 \%$ for RhB. These findings suggest that the $\mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ system represents a promising strategy for the treatment of RhB-contaminated wastewater.

The performance of the catalysts was characterized using two complementary techniques: cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The electrochemical test conditions are in Supplementary Note 2. The Supplementary Fig. 5 presents the EIS results of all materials. It is evident that $3 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } { - } 2$ h $( \mathrm { F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } } )$ and $2 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ -1h-1.5 Mpa $\scriptstyle \left( \mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } } \right)$ ) exhibit the lowest charge transfer resistance $( \operatorname { R c t } )$ values, suggesting their superior charge transfer capability. As illustrated in the figure, HC$\mathrm { F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } }$ demonstrated a higher current density and a more rapid redox process, likely due to the electron migration occurring during the redox reactions between $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ , as well as $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 3 + }$ within the material. # Degradation performance of ${ \mathsf { H } } { \mathsf { C } } { - } { \mathsf { F e C o @ C } } _ { 3 5 0 } / { \mathsf { P A A } }$ system

The degradation of RhB via activation of PAA was applied to evaluate the catalytic activity of $H C \mathrm { - F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ .

The relationship between the concentration of the $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ catalyst and its effectiveness in activating PAA was illustrated in Fig. 5a and the first order kinetic reaction constants of catalyst concentrations in Supplementary Fig. 6a. The amount of ROS and R-O• species produced by activated PAA is influenced by the concentration of the catalyst, which in turn affects degradation efficiency. As the concentration of HC $- \mathrm { F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ was added from 0.05 to $0 . 2 \mathrm { g / L }$ , the efficiency of PAA activation improved progressively. This enhancement is due to the increased interactions between the catalyst and PAA, leading to a greater production of R-O• species and ROS. However, when the concentration of HC $- \mathrm { F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ was increased to $0 . 3 { \mathrm { g / L } } ,$ the degradation efficiency did not show an important change. This observation may be due to the overutilization of the HC$\mathrm { F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ catalyst, which could lead to accumulation and a subsequent decrease in catalytic activity45. In the absence of further PAA activation, the excess catalyst did not significantly enhance the yield of ROS and R-O• species. Consequently, increasing the catalyst concentration beyond this point would not only fail to substantially improve the degradation rate of RhB but would also result in catalyst wastage.

The impact of PAA concentration on the degradation rate was shown in Fig. 5b and the first order kinetic reaction constants of PAA in Supplementary Fig. 6b. The degradation rate of pollutants was importantly influenced by the addition of PAA. As the PAA dosage increased from $0 . 2 \mathrm { m M }$ to $1 . 6 \mathrm { m M }$ , the degradation rate improved from $8 1 . 8 4 \%$ to $9 9 . 8 7 \%$ , with the corresponding rate constant $( k )$ improving from $0 . 0 5 9 4 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ to $0 . 4 3 7 5 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Notably, the degradation rate plateaued at dosages exceeding $0 . 6 \mathrm { m M }$ . These results indicate that the degradation rate accelerated with increasing PAA concentration. After $0 . 4 \mathrm { m M }$ , the additional PAA did not completely saturate the active sites of HC$F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 }$ .

The concentration effect of RhB was shown in Fig. 5c and the first order kinetic reaction constants of initial concentrations of RhB in Supplementary Fig. 6c. An increase in RhB concentration resulted in a slower degradation rate. Elevated RhB concentrations further impede degradation efficiency by necessitating a greater consumption of free radicals.

# Identification of ROS and R-O• species

$\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ activates peracetic acid (PAA) through the generation of iron and cobalt on its surface, which subsequently produces multiple R-O• species and ROS. # Possible RhB degradation pathways and toxicity assessment

The second pathway pertains to the disruption of the conjugated structure of RhB. Specifically, the upper and lower sections of the RhB conjugated structure are linked by a single bond, which is relatively susceptible to cleavage. The peak observed at an M/Z value of 339 corroborates the disruption of the conjugated structure of the RhB molecule. Ultimately, these intermediates undergo gradual mineralization to produce $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ . As illustrated in Fig. 10d, g, the d-band centers for $\mathrm { F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ -Fe and $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ -Fe are -1.35 and - $\cdot 1 . 2 9 \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, whereas the d-band centers for $\mathrm { F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ -Co and ${ \mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C } } _ { 3 5 0 ^ { - } } { \mathrm { C } } ($ o are -1.77 and $- 0 . 6 4 \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively. Earlier research suggests that a decrease in the d-band center improves the electron occupancy of the antibonding state, which in turn reduces the electronic interaction occurring between the reaction intermediates and the surface of the catalyst64. Notably, $\mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } - C o / F e }$ is positioned closer to the Fermi energy level $\mathrm { ( E _ { f } ) }$ compared to $\mathrm { F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } - C o / F e } .$ . These findings suggest that the $\mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ system is more advantageous for treating wastewater generated from dyeing processes.

# Actual dye wastewater treatment

The actual textile dye wastewater assessed in this research was sourced from a garment manufacturing facility situated in Nansiba Industrial Park, Mianyang City, Sichuan Province.

As shown in Supplementary Table 4, it is the characterization of real textile dye wastewater treated by $H C \mathrm { - F e C o } @ C _ { 3 5 0 }$ before and after degradation. The total phosphorus (TP), total nitrogen (TN), chemical oxygen demand (COD), and ammonia nitrogen $\mathrm { ( N H } _ { 3 } – \mathrm { N } )$ levels in the actual dye wastewater were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 11a, the RE of COD, $\mathrm { N H } _ { 3 }$ -N, TN, TP could reach approximately $5 3 . 5 7 \%$ , $5 3 . 2 3 \%$ , $2 9 . 2 5 \%$ , $1 9 . 2 \%$ , respectively after $2 0 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ treatment by $\mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ . The COD of dye wastewater treated by catalyst was removed. There was little change in total nitrogen, and both total phosphorus and ammonia nitrogen were released to some extent. To evaluate the possible practical use of the HC$\mathrm { F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ system, a study was conducted to determine how effectively it treats actual wastewater generated from dye manufacturing in Fig. 11b, c. Due to the difficult degradation and complexity of actual wastewater, the adsorption step was eliminated and the reaction time is extended to 200 minutes. Moreover, the varieties of organic substances found in the wastewater itself, along with the routes through which these organic compounds change during breakdown, were examined utilizing Excitation Emission Matrix (EEM) spectroscopy. The EEM is segmented into five distinct regions, each representing a different type of organic matter: Regions I and II are associated with aromatic proteins, Region III is connected to fulvic acids, Region IV includes dissolved microbial metabolites, and Region V consists of humic acids65. As shown in Fig. 11b, c, after the breakdown of catalyst-activated PAA, the materials in their corresponding areas displayed alterations, particularly. The substances in Region IV were reduced to varying degrees, while those in Region V increased. Throughout the process of wastewater degradation, substances resembling proteins, compounds similar to soluble microbial products (SMP), humic materials, and xanthophylls are emitted into the supernatant. These compounds are then effectively decomposed through oxidation reactions, contributing to the overall degradation of the wastewater. The results indicate that the hydrogenated catalyst exhibits a more pronounced effect compared to the unmodified catalyst.

In order to further confirm the toxicity and fertility effectiveness of degraded wastewater, mung bean planting experiments were carried out. As illustrated in Fig. 11d–g, the biomass and plant height in the degraded wastewater system reached their peak after a growth period of three weeks. This can be attributed to the fact that the degraded wastewater not only effectively reduced toxicity but also facilitated the release of nitrogen and phosphorus, which are essential nutrients for plant growth. As illustrated in Fig. 11h, i, in the degraded wastewater system, the average plant height was approximately $2 0 . 2 \mathrm { c m }$ , with an average final biomass of $4 . 0 9 { \mathrm { g } }$ per sample plant. In contrast, in the tap water system, the average plant height was about $1 4 . 3 \mathrm { c m } ,$ and the average final biomass was approximately $3 . 1 0 { \mathrm { g } }$ per sample plant. Additionally, the plants in the treated wastewater system exhibited slightly superior growth compared to those in the tap water system, which aligns with the results of toxicity analysis and the changes in total phosphorus and ammonia nitrogen levels before and after wastewater treatment. It shows that the toxicity of wastewater has been greatly eliminated, which the technology is suitable for pre-treatment of industrial wastewater. The treated wastewater can promote the growth of mung beans, not only due to the reduction of toxicity during degradation, but also due to the release of nutrients during degradation. LC-MS and T.E.S.T analysis showed that the toxicity of intermediates produced during RhB decomposition was much less than its own toxicity. The removal efficiency $( R E )$ of COD, $\mathrm { N H } _ { 3 }$ -N, TN, and TP in the treated dye wastewater demonstrates that the treated effluent retains a variety of essential nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus compounds, which are beneficial for promoting plant growth. The treated wastewater contains a variety of nutrients, which is conducive to the growth of plants. The germination test of mung bean showed that the growth of mung bean irrigated with treated wastewater was better than that with tap water. The results showed that the treated dye wastewater not only significantly reduced toxicity, but also promoted the release of nitrogen and phosphorus.

In summary, this study provides a new idea and method for the treatment and recycling of refractory dyestuff wastewater.

# Discussion

In this study, a novel hydrogenated FeCo-MOF derivative catalyst $\mathrm { ' H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 } )$ was prepared for the first time and applied to activate PAA to remedy actual dye wastewater. The optimum hydrogenation conditions for $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ were $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { M p }$ for $^ { \textrm { 1 h } }$ , which increased the oxygen vacancy and the contents of Fe(II) and $\operatorname { C o } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ benifts for PAA activating. The results of EIS and CV characterization also showed that the $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ catalyst has higher current density and stronger oxidation capacity. Compared with the $\mathrm { F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ /PAA, the degradation rate constant $( k )$ of RhB for HC$\mathrm { F e C o @ C } _ { 3 5 0 }$ /PAA was 18 times higher. Under optimal conditions of $0 . 4 \mathrm { m M }$ PAA and a solution $\mathrm { p H }$ of 9, the $R E$ of RhB with $3 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ reached $9 9 . 3 3 \%$ within $2 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ with an extremely low catalyst dosage of $\scriptstyle 0 . 1 \ g / \mathrm { L }$ . In addition, $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ exhibits outstanding magnetic recycling and reuseability, the $R E$ of RhB remains over $8 0 \%$ after repeated use four times. The results of quenching experiments and EPR test showed that ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and R-O• of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C } ( \mathrm { O } ) \mathrm { O } \cdot$ , and $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C } ( \mathrm { O } ) \mathrm { O O } \cdot$ were the main active species in the $\mathrm { \Delta \mathrm { - } F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ system. The DFT calculation also shows that the $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ catalyst favors activating PAA, and the PDOS is closer to the Fermi level, which favors PAA adsorption on the catalyst. The LC-MS analysis and T.E.S.T. showed that the toxicity of the intermediates produced during the decomposition of RhB was much less than that of itself. As for the treatment of actual dye wastewater, the $R E$ of COD, $\mathrm { N H } _ { 3 ^ { - } } \mathrm { N }$ , TN, TP could reach approximately $5 3 . 5 7 \%$ , $5 3 . 2 3 \%$ , $2 9 . 2 5 \%$ , $1 9 . 2 \%$ , respectively after $2 0 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ treatment by $\mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ . Finally, the mungbean germination experiment revealed that not only the toxicity of the treated dye wastewater was dramatically decreased, but also promoted the release of nitrogen and phosphorus resulting in a better growth of the beans than that of tap water, which may the technology is suitable for pre-treatment of industrial wastewater. This research suggests an alternative solution for actual dye wastewater not only to be treated but also be reuesd due to its toxicity was sharply declined with $\mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ system. Currently, the research mainly focuses on the laboratory scale and has not been applied to the process on a large scale. For the complex actual wastewater, it may contain more interfering substances, which may affect the activity and selectivity of the catalyst, and it is necessary to further improve the anti-interference ability of the system. Therefore, in the future, pilot-scale studies can be conducted to optimize the reactor design and operating parameters, as well as to verify the feasibility of the $\mathrm { H C - F e C o @ C _ { 3 5 0 } / P A A }$ system for large-scale applications. Through the introduction of novel carrier materials or surface modification techniques, the long-term stability and anti-poisoning performance of $\mathrm { H C - F e C o } @ { \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 5 0 }$ can be further enhanced.
