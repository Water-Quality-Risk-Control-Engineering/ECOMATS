# . Introduction

With the rapid development of the industry, the persistent organic contaminants remaining in the groundwater and soil pose a huge threat to the ecosystem and human health. For a long time in the past, researchers have made great efforts to remove persistent organic contaminants through various methods, such as photodegradation, biodegradation, and electrochemical degradation [13]. In recent years, advanced oxidation processes based on sulfate radicals have been considered as an effective method to degrade persistent organic contaminants and have received widespread attention. The high oxidationreduction potential of sulfate radicals $( \mathrm { E } _ { 0 } = 2 . 5  – 3 . 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ ) makes it more capable of oxidizing organic contaminants [4]. Sulfate radicals can be obtained by cracking the unstable peroxide bond in persulfate by accepting electrons [5].

Nano-zero-valent iron (nZVI) has been used to activate persulfate to degrade persistent organic contaminants due to its large specific surface area, low reduction potential and environmental friendliness [68]. However, the high reaction rate and surface energy of the traditional nZVI which make it easy to be rapidly oxidized and agglomerated during storage and use process. At present, various materials can be used as stabilizer to improve the dispersibility and stability of nZVI, such as reduced graphene oxide, carbon materials, carboxymethyl cellulose, water-in-oil, etc [914]. According to previous studies, transition metal particles encapsulated in graphene nano-shells are used as catalysts for many important reactions, such as the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis reaction [15], electrocatalytic oxygen evolution and oxygen reduction reaction [16], hydrogen evolution [17] etc. The graphene layer encapsulation can not only effectively inhibit the rapid passivation and agglomeration of nZVI, but also act as an electronic conductor to promote electron transfer between the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ core and the external reactants, thereby further enhancing the catalytic activity of nZVI [18,19]. However, the performance and mechanism of oxidative degradation of persistent organic contaminants by using nZVI particles encapsulated by graphene nano-shells as heterogeneous catalysts are still unclear. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a highly dispersed and stable graphene nano-shells encapsulated nZVI particles and explore its performance as a persulfate activator for the degradation of organic contaminants.

So far, various synthesis methods have been used to develop catalysts with a coreshell graphite structure, such as chemical vapor deposition, template method, solgel method, and arc discharge [2022]. However, these synthesis methods usually use fossil fuels, such as acetylene and pyridine as carbon sources. These raw materials are not only highly toxic but also expensive. Therefore, it is very necessary to find renewable and inexpensive carbon sources. Forestry waste-sawdust, as a cheap, rich carbon and high-lignin-content bio-renewable resource, can be used as the preferred carbon-based material for metal catalyst carriers. Compared with other polymer coatings, it has higher chemical and thermal stability and biocompatibility. Meanwhile, the presence of a variety of oxygen-containing functional groups promotes the adsorption and dispersion of the metal catalyst. Previous studies have found that the uniform dispersion of iron particles on the carbon matrix play a vital role in the catalytic graphitization of carbon sources as a catalyst [23]. However, biomass and carbon-based materials have strong hydrophobicity and are difficult to exist in the form of molecules in aqueous solutions [24,25], which affects the interaction between them and iron ions and reduces the degree of contact between iron particles and carbon sources. Tetrahydrofuran (THF), as a unique, relatively non-toxic, green organic co-solvent, can be miscible with water at room temperature and form an azeotrope, promote the dispersibility of carbon-based materials in the solution [26], and increase the degree of contact between iron on and crbon-bamaterials. Forinstan, Deguchi  al. [26] pepared a stably dispersed fullerene colloidal dispersion in an aqueous phase by using a THF solvent without using any stabilizer. Besides, the THF co-solvent has exceptional solubility for many small organic molecules and macromolecules. Due to the strong interaction between the intermolecular segments of lignin and the THF solvent molecules [27], it is beneficial to promote the deconstruction of biomass and the dissolution of lignin [25], by improving the dispersibility of iron particles on lignin to further enhance the degree of catalytic graphitization of lignin though the iron-based catalyst, thereby promoting the formation of graphene shell layer [15,23]. Therefore, improving the dispersibility of carbon-based materials and promoting the interaction between iron particles and carbon-based materials are very important for enhancing the catalytic performance of nano $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ nanoparticles encapsulated by graphene nano-shells $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { n } } Z \boldsymbol { \mathrm { V I } } @ \mathbf { g } \mathbf { B } \mathbf { C } )$ catalysts. However, as far as we know, the stabilized $\scriptstyle \mathrm { n Z V I @ 8 } \mathrm { B C }$ catalyst with a coreshell structure prepared by tetrahydrofuran-assisted thermal reduction method using biomass-sawdust as a carbon source has not been reported yet.

Herein, in this study, a one-step carbothermal reduction method aided by Tetrahydrofuran was used to synthesize $\scriptstyle \mathrm { n Z V I } @ g \mathrm { B C }$ and used as a persulfate activator to degrade the organic contaminant 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP). The characterization of the material confirmed that the stabilized $\scriptstyle \mathrm { n Z V I } @ \mathrm { g B C }$ were uniformly dispersed on / in the carbon substrate derived from sawdust. Graphene nano-shells can effectively prevent the rapid passivation and agglomeration of the nZVI core, and due to the electronic interaction between the core iron nanoparticles and the graphene carbon shell, the catalytic activity and stability of $\scriptstyle \mathrm { n Z V I } @ g \mathrm { B C }$ are further improved. In addition, the long-term stability and possible reaction mechanism of synthetic materials with a coreshell structure have also been investigated. This simple, economical and green $\scriptstyle \mathrm { n Z V I } @ g \mathrm { B C }$ may be becoming a promising iron-carbon catalyst for large-scale applications in the field of environmental remediation.

# 2.2. Fabrication of graphene nanoshell-encapsulated nZVI composite

Forestry waste biomasssawdust was selected as carbon source. Ferric chloride hexahydrate $\mathrm { ( F e C l _ { 3 } { \cdot } 6 H _ { 2 } O ) }$ was used as the iron precursor. The formed black solid powder was milled in a quartz mortar, and it was annealed in a tube furnace at 300, 500, and $7 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with different heating rates and kept for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ under $\Nu _ { 2 }$ atmosphere. # 2.4. Catalytic degradation experiments

Batch degradation experiments were conducted in $5 0 \mathrm { m L }$ transparent glass bottle with Teflon mininerts, which kept sharking for $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ at a speed of $1 5 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ on reciprocating shaker. The reaction temperature was maintained at $2 6 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ A brief description of the typical procedure was as follows: firstly, $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g }$ composites was added into $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of $2 0 \ \mathrm { m g T L } ^ { - 1 }$ 2,4-DCP aqueous solution; after complete mixing, $2 3 . 8 1 ~ \mathrm { m g }$ of sodium persulfate was quickly added into the solution to initiate the reaction. At given time intervals, $0 . 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of the reaction solution was filtered through a $0 . 2 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ PTFE filter membrane and then added to the chromatographic sample vial. By injecting immediately into $0 . 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of MeOH, the reaction was then quenched. For the desorption experiment, the composite was used alone to remove 2,4-DCP without adding PS, and other reaction conditions remain unchanged. After $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of reaction, the material was separated from the reaction solution by vacuum filtration, and then added to the same volume of MeOH solution and shaken for different times. In a given time interval, $^ { 1 \ m L }$ of filtrate was taken to determine the 2,4-DCP concentration. As for reusability experiment, the material was separated magnetically and washed several times with ultra-pure water or MeOH, dried at $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and used for the next cycle. Scavenging experiment was carried out by adding different doses of quenchersMeOH, tert-butylalcohol (TBA), P-benzoquinone (BQ) and L-Histidine.

# 3.Results and discussion

# 3.1. Characterization of composite

SEM images (Fig. 1a-c) displayed the surface morphology of the composites prepared at different carbonization temperatures. When the carbonization temperature was $3 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ ,the degree of thermal decomposition of biomass was relatively low, and the carbon surface was relatively flat and uniform. When the temperature rose to $5 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the irregular defects on the surface of Fe@BM-500 increase significantly, and the appearance of pore-like structures can be clearly observed. The rod-like substance attached to the surface of $\mathrm { F e } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } { \cdot } 3 0 0$ and $\mathrm { F e @ B M } .$ . 500 are iron oxides. Interestingly, the three-dimensional reticular structure was observed in $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ , and the white particles attached to this structure are $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ nanoparticles. Electron mapping spectroscopy confirmed this result. As shown in Fig. 1d, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ nanoparticles in $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ are mainly distributed in the threedimensional reticular structure of the middle layer, but they were rarely distributed on the blocky carbon of the upper and lower layers. This may be due to the large amount of microporous structures and reticulated carbon skeletons that are more conducive to the adsorption and stable attachment of Fe species.

TEM micrographs shown that $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ particles with a diameter of $5 { \mathrm { - } } 7 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ are clearly observed, which are uniformly dispersed in the amorphous carbon matrix (Fig. 2a-b). In addition, no independent $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ particles were discovered outside the carbon matrix, indicating that the nZVI particles formed by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ reduction all anchored on the carbon substrate, which is conducive to the stability and dispersion of nanoparticles. Fig. 2c displayed that $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } { \cdot } 7 0 0$ particles have a coreshell structure composed of nZVI nanoparticle core encapsulated by graphene nano-shells formed through tightly compacted graphene carbon layers arranged with an orderly manner. The HRTEM image of an individual $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ particle reveals the core  shell structure (Fig. 2d). The lattice spacing of $\mathbf { d } = 0 . 2 0 \mathbf { n m }$ in the core corresponds to the (1 1 0) plane of $\alpha$ -Fe [31], and the lattice spacing of $\textrm { d } = 0 . 3 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ in the shell corresponds to the (00 2) plane of graphite [32]. The TEM image of the $\mathrm { F e } @ \mathbf { B M - } 5$ fabricated under the mass ratio of carbon source to iron source of 2:5 was shown in Fig. 2e-f. The results indicated that when the iron source is excessive, there is not enough carbon source to reduce the $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ to nZVI, which causes the remaining $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ to be highly agglomerated due to the strong magnetism and affects the activation performance of the catalyst and reduces the removal efficiency of organic pollutants. A lattice spacing of $\mathbf { d } = 0 . 2 5 \ : \mathrm { n m }$ corresponding to the (31 1) plane of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ [31] was observed in Fig. 2f. It is worth noting that the outer layer of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ particle is coated by amorphous carbon matrix without the formation of a graphene carbon layer, which further verifies the above conclusions and is consistent with the XRD results of Fig. 3.

Raman spectroscopy was used to characterize the change of carbon phase in the catalyst, and further explored the graphitization state of catalyst. In general, the relative strength between the D band and G band $\left( I _ { \mathrm { D } } / I _ { \mathrm { G } } \right)$ was used to reflect the degree of graphitization of the synthetic material. The $I _ { \mathrm { D } } / I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ ratios of Fe@BM-300, $\mathrm { F e } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } . 5 0 0$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ were 1.002, 0.994 and 0.967, which proved that the degree of graphitization is greatly enhanced as the pyrolysis temperature increases. Moreover, the lower $I _ { \mathrm { D } } / I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ ratio instructs a higher the order degree of graphite, which further illustrated that the graphene nanoshells in $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } \ – 7 0 0$ are composed of ordered multilayer graphitic carbon layers. # 3.2. Degradation of 2, 4-DCP by graphene nano-shells encapsulated nZVI/PS system

The XRD detection results showed that carbonization temperature and the mass ratio of the carbon source to iron source are the critical factors that affect the formation of graphene nano-shells encapsulated nZVI particles. Therefore, the experiments were carried out to explore the remove efficiency of 2,4-DCP by PS activation with composites from different carbonization temperatures and mass ratios of sawdust $\mathbf { \cdot F e C l _ { 3 } }$ (Fig. 4a-b). The results showed that $\mathrm { F e } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } { \cdot } 3 0 0$ and $\mathrm { F e } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } . 5 0 0$ have no obvious activation effect on PS to remove 2,4-DCP. Within $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of the beginning of the reaction, the removal rate of $^ { 2 , 4 }$ -DCP was only $1 0 { - } 1 5 \%$ However, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ displayed a significant activation effect on PS and the removal rate of 2,4-DCP as high as $9 1 \%$ within ${ 5 } \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . The larger $\begin{array} { r } { S _ { \mathrm { B E T } } , } \end{array}$ , total pore volume and higher iron content of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathrm { B M } \cdot$ . 700 may all beneficial to obtain more active sites with superior performance for the removal of 2,4-DCP [40]. This is consistent with the result of BET. In addition, as the proportion of carbon sources increased, the reduction of iron oxides was significantly improved. When the mass ratio of carbon source to iron source increased from 2:5 to 2:3, the removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP enhanced rapidly from $1 2 \%$ to $9 1 \%$ with the carbon source content increased after $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of reaction. When the mass ratio of carbon source to iron source was 2:3, the degradation effect was optimal, and 2,4-DCP was almost completely removed at the reaction proceeds to $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . However, when the content of carbon source was further increased, the removal rate of pollutants was greatly reduced. This may be due to the low iron content and the active sites of Fe being covered by excess carbon which deposited on the surface of the catalyst, thereby inhibiting the activity of the catalyst and leading to significant decrease in removal efficiency [36].

In order to further investigate the role of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } { \cdot } 7 0 0$ in the 2,4- DCP degradation reaction, control experiments were conducted in $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } \ – 7 0 0 + \mathbf { P S }$ ,commercial $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } + \mathrm { P S }$ . $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ alone, PS alone, and BM-700 alone systems (Fig. 4c). When PS exists alone in the system without $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ , the removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP was only $6 . 9 \%$ after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of reaction, which due to the slow decomposition rate of PS at room temperature. Through the adsorption experiment of pure biochar BM-700 without iron impregnation as a control, the removal rate of 2,4-DCP was only $1 5 . 9 \%$ at the end of reaction. When PS was activated by commercial nZVI, the removal rate of 2,4-DCP attained $3 5 . 5 \%$ after 5 min. However, the removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP rapidly reached $9 1 . 4 \%$ after ${ \mathsf { 5 } } \operatorname* { m i n }$ of reaction, and complete removal was achieved after $2 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbb { B } \mathbf { M } \mathbf { - } 7 0 0 + \mathbf { P S }$ system. This indicated that $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ exhibits superior activation performance for PS. Interestingly, when $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ was used alone to remove 2,4-DCP, the removal rates after 5 min and $2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction achieved $5 1 . 4 \%$ and $7 2 . 3 \%$ , respectively. The desorption experiment in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ alone and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ $+ \mathrm { \bf ~ P S }$ systems were carried out in the same time interval as the degradation reaction (Fig. S4). The results showed that at the end of the reaction, the desorption concentrations of $2 , 4 { \cdot } \mathrm { D C P }$ in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ alone and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } \ – 7 0 0 + \mathbf { P S }$ systems basically reached equilibrium and were $7 . 0 7 ~ \mathrm { \ m g ^ { \bullet } L ^ { - 1 } }$ and $3 . 0 7 ~ \mathrm { \ m g ^ { \bullet } L ^ { - 1 } }$ ,respectively. Therefore, the contribution to the removal of 2,4-DCP by adsorption in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } \cdot$ . $7 0 0 ~ + ~ \mathrm { P S }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ alone systems was $1 6 . 9 \%$ and $3 8 . 8 \%$ , respectively. This result showed that the addition of PS significantly enhances the proportion of oxidative degradation in the removal of 2,4- DCP. Moreover, When $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ was used alone to remove 2,4-DCP, its adsorption effect was significantly higher than the adsorption efficiency of 2,4-DCP in the BM-700 system. This indicated that the supporting of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ nanoparticles enhances the adsorption of the composite by increasing the specific surface area and providing more adsorption sites for contaminants [41]. We also compared the reported heterogeneous iron-based materials for PS activation to remove organic contaminants (Table S2). It was found that $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ showed obvious high oxidation efficiency in organic contaminants removal in comparison with the other iron-based materials which indicated that persulfate groups were efficiently activated by iron transformations of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathrm { B M } \cdot$ . 700 during the AOPs.

The ideal state for the removal of refractory organic contaminants is to be completely mineralized into $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ rather than just degrade the target pollutants into other low-toxic by-products. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the TOC content in different degradation systems to further clarify the mineralization degree of 2,4-DCP at the end of reaction, and the results were shown in Fig. 4d. When $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ was added to UW alone, the TOC concentration was only $1 . 3 6 ~ \mathrm { m g ^ { \bullet } L ^ { - 1 } }$ . However, the original TOC concentration of the 2,4-DCP solution was $1 3 . 6 9 \mathrm { m g } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ . If only PS was added, the removal rate of TOC was only $2 . 4 \%$ , which was consistent with the removal result of 2,4-DCP in the degradation experiment of PS alone system. Since the system with PS activated by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ , the remaining TOC concentration in the reaction solution was rapidly reduced to $2 . 2 4 ~ \mathrm { m g ^ { \bullet } L ^ { - 1 } }$ , and the removal efficiency increased to $8 3 . 7 \%$ . The combined effect of adsorption and oxidation lead to the removal of TOC. Due to the adsorption ratio in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } \mathbf { - } 7 0 0 + \mathbf { P S }$ system was $1 6 . 9 \%$ , the oxidation caused by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathrm { B M } \cdot$ 700 activated PS contributed $6 6 . 8 \%$ to the removal of 2,4-DCP. It is worth noted that the removal rate of TOC attained $6 8 . 1 2 \%$ in the system with $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ alone. This may be due to the reaction of nZVI particles with dissolved oxygen in solution leading to the formation of reactive intermediates $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ . The further reaction of generated $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ can form $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ ,thus promoting the highly efficient removal of 2,4-DCP [42]. In addition, while $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ was used alone to remove 2,4-DCP, the efficiency of adsorption and oxidation were $3 8 . 8 \%$ and $2 9 . 3 2 \%$ , respectively. This is a clearly indication that in addition to the adsorption of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ on 2,4-DCP removal, part of the oxidative degradation pathway in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ alone system also exists.

# 3.3. Effect of $F e ^ { 0 } @ B M  – 7 0 0$ dosage, PS concentration and initial solution $p H$ on 2,4-DCP degradation

The removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP with different dosages of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ from 0 to $0 . 4 ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ was studied, and the results were shown in Fig. 5a. The dosage of PS is an important factor affecting the catalytic performance of the catalyst to remove organic pollutants. The effect of PS concentration on 2,4-DCP removal was presented in Fig. 5b. With the dosage of PS increased from 0 to $3 ~ \mathrm { m m o l } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , the removal rate of 2,4- DCP rapidly increased from $5 1 . 4 \%$ to $9 2 . 4 \%$ within $5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of the reaction. When the dosage of PS was $2 \mathrm { m m o l } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ and $3 \mathrm { m m o l } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , the removal of 2,4-DCP was almost equivalent, which substantially attained the reaction equilibrium within $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . It is indication that the reaction of degrading 2,4-DCP by PS decomposition to generate free radicals was basically completed within $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . However, when the dosage of PS added was increased to $5 \mathrm { m m o l } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , the removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP decreased. This may be due to the self-scavenging effect of free radicals inhibiting the degradation of 2,4-DCP with excessive PS concentration [43]. In general, the results of removing 2,4-DCP with different composite dosages indicated that $0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ was the optimal dosage of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ .Therefore, as the PS concentration increased, PS would decompose to produce more active oxygen for effectively promoting the degradation efficiency of 2,4-DCP with the dosage of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ maintained in $0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ .

Previous studies reported that PS was stable at different pH values in the solution, but the difference in $\mathsf { p H }$ of wastewater containing organic pollutants had controlling effects on the generation of active species, Fe species, the form of pollutants, and catalytic activity, thereby affecting the removal efficiency of pollutants [34,44]. The effect of different initial pH (3, 5, 7, 9 and 11) of the solution on the removal of 2,4-DCP was shown in Fig. 5c. However, the removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP after $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of reaction at neutral conditions was obviously lower than that at acidic conditions. Moreover, the removal efficiency of 2,4-DCP gradually decreased with the pH increased, especially when $\mathrm { p H } = 1 1$ , the removal efficiency at the end of the reaction was only $4 2 \%$ This may be due to the influence of pH on the surface electric energy of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } { \cdot } 7 0 0$ and the formation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ . Therefore, the charge distribution on the surface of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } _ { - } 7 0 0$ in different pH systems performed by the Zeta potential was displayed in Fig. 5d. When $\mathsf { p H } = 6 . 5 3$ without adjusted, the Zeta potential of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ was $9 . 9 4 ~ \mathrm { m V }$ .When the initial $\mathsf { p H } \le 9$ of the system, the Zeta potentials are all positive. It is indication that a large number of positive charges accumulated on the surface of the composite promote the production of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and PS activation, thereby efficiently degrading 2,4-DCP. However, when the pH increased to 11, the Zeta potential of the material was $- 3 4 . 5 \mathrm { m V }$ The existence of a large number of negative charges lead to passivation of the surface in the material and repulsively react with the persulfate anion, which further inhibits the activation of PS by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ [44]. In general, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ was the main active species under acidic conditions, and •OH was the main free radical under alkaline conditions. However, the conversion between $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and •OH can produce various active oxygen species, which have different redox potentials, resulting in different removal efficiencies of organic pollutants [45].

# 3.4. Recyclability and air stability of $F e ^ { 0 } @ B M - 7 0 0$

The recyclability and air stability of catalyst are considerable factors that need to be evaluated. Due to that the synthetic material has the function of adsorption and the reaction system is acidic, the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } \cdot$ . 700 after one run was repeatedly washed with MeOH solution at least three times for the next operation. The removal effect of 2,4-DCP by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ activated PS after three runs was shown in Fig. S5a. The results exhibited that the removal efficiency decreased clearly in each recycle, especially in the second run. In the second and third cycle experiments, the 2,4-DCP removal efficiency dropped from $9 9 . 2 6 \%$ to $6 7 . 2 \%$ and $5 9 . 8 \%$ , respectively. Fig S6a-c showed the morphology changes of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ after three cycles of reaction. SEM and TEM showed that the nano-Fe(0) particles were still uniformly distributed in the carbon frameworks and the morphology did not change significantly (Fig S6a-b). However, it should be noted that HR-TEM found that part of the graphene layer encapsulated the nano-Fe(0) particles began to deconstruct and gradually became disordered (Fig S6c). This may be due to the gradual deconstruction of the graphene carbon layer as the electron transfer during multiple runs, thereby forming a disordered graphite carbon structure. Raman spectroscopy further confirmed the above results. It can be seen from Fig S6d that after 3rd run, the $I _ { \mathrm { D } } / I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ value of the material increased from 0.967 to 0.991. This showed that after the reaction, the ordered structure of graphene in the material is destroyed and the degree of disorder increases [19]. This is consistent with the HR-TEM results. Three major degradation intermediates were found in the system of PS activation by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B M - } 7 0 0$ to remove 2,4-DCP, which were 1,2- benzenediol, 1,4-benzenediol, phenol (Fig. S12). The chlorine at 2 and 4 points in 2,4-DCP was attack by $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ ,•OH, ${ \mathrm { O } } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ to achieve chlorine substitution and hydroxylation. Then the phenolic compounds formed by the dechlorination reaction were further opened ring under the action of free radicals to form small molecular acids, such as oxalic acid, acetic acid and formic acid which were finally degraded into $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ to realize the mineralization of contaminant.

# 4. Conclusion

The $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ nanoparticles encapsulated by graphene nano-shells were synthesized by one-step carbothermal reduction of biomass-sawdust impregnated with ferric chloride hexahydrate aided by THF, which have a core—shell structure. The formation of graphene nano-shells effectively enhanced the oxidation resistance of the nZVI core, and the use of sawdust-derived carbon as the carbon substrate could significantly inhibit the agglomeration of nZVI and improved the dispersion and stability of nZVI particles. In addition, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ nanoparticles encapsulated by graphene nano-shells have the superior catalytic performance of efficiently activating PS to degrade 2,4-DCP and even complete mineralization. As a conductor that promoted electron transfer, graphene nano-shells efficiently mediate the cycle between $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ to further facilitate the activation of PS. There are four active species $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ ,OH ${ \mathrm { O } } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ generated in the process of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } @ \mathbf { B } \mathbf { M } _ { - } 7 0 0$ activated persulfate to remove 2,4-DCP are involved in the oxidative degradation of 2,4-DCP through free radical pathways. In particular, $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - }$ as the active free radical that plays a primary role for 2,4-DCP degradation. Meanwhile, possible degradation pathways were proposed based on the identification of reaction intermediates. Interestingly, graphene nanoshells-encapsulated $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ nanoparticles could utilized dissolved oxygen to generate hydroxyl radicals to achieve degradation of organic contaminant in the absence of PS. This research provided a simple, low-cost and green preparation method for stabilizing nZVI. At the same time, it also provided a promising way for synthesis of carbon-supported iron-based nanomaterials to realize the mineralization of refractory organic pollutants in advanced oxidation process based on persulfate activation.
