# Lattice Confinement-Steered Adaptive Crystal-Field and Spin-State Transition of Co Single-Atom Sites for Robust Water Cleanup

a-enE.Yang Min-Pin Zhu,Chea Sun,Fu-iHuag Xin-an Zou, Yu-Ming Zheng,\* and Shaobin Wang\*

Single-atom catalysts (SACs) take a pivotal position in heterogeneous water purification, yet their deployment is restricted by poor yields, limited activity-stability and high costs. Here, A simple and economical "frustrated Lewis pairs"-assisted lattice confinement strategy is reported to construct tetrahedrally and octahedrally coordinated single-atom Co sites in millimetric $\gamma \mathsf { - A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ (SCoA) at a kilogram level, achieving site-specific spin state modulation of the lattice-confined Co. The flexible lattice confinement leverages the tetrahedral and octahedral site interactions to form high-spin-dominated Co(II) centres. Synergistic adsorption of Co-Al sites toward peroxymonosulfate induces an adaptive shift of high-spin Co(ll) from a tetrahedral to octahedral site, which provides an efficient spin channel for both proton/electron transport and conversion of high-spin ${ \sf C o } ( | { \sf I } | ) / { \sf C o }$ (III) into low-spin Co(IlI) in the octahedral site, lowering the energy barrier of synchronously producing $\$ 0_ { 4} ^ { \bullet- }$ and $\mathbf { \bar { \rho } } _ { \mathbf { O _ { 2 } } }$ . The unique mechanism sustains a high-efficient removal of multiple pharmaceuticals/antibiotics and antibiotic resistance genes. Notably, the estimated cost of SCoA at ${ \approx } 5 5 3 / 1 6 8$ is 23002600 times cheaper than that of commercial $3 d$ -transition metal SACs. Thus, the proposed framework of lattice confinement-augmented adaptive single-atom catalysis establishes a low-cost and versatile platform to address the issue of recalcitrant emerging contaminants, advancing clean water and water resilience against the global freshwater crisis.

# 1. Introduction

Global water ecosystems are under an increasingly rising threat caused by ubiquitous emerging contaminants (ECs, like pharmaceuticals and antibiotics) and biological antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in municipal and industrial wastewater effluents.[1-5] This necessitates an urgent development of high-efficient water purification technology to ensure the safety of aquatic ecosystems, advancing water resilience toward realizing the Sustainable Development Goal 6: Clean water and sanitation for all by 2030. Of the technologies presently viable for deep water purification, peroxymonosulfate (PMS)-oriented heterogeneous oxidation is most appealing, as it offers an advantage of flexibly producing robust radical and nonradical species to completely eliminate recalcitrant ECs and ARGs/ARGs-bearing pathogens under a wider pH operation window (210 vs 24 for classical Fenton oxidation).[68]

Over the past five years, fully dispersed transition metal single-atom catalysts (SACs) have been actively pursued to enhance PMS activation and selective catalytic oxidation processes to purify the challenging wastewaters.[9-11] Despite the thrilling advances of SACs on the kinetics and mechanisms, three fundamental, yet ticklish, issues still constrain their deployment: i) Most of current SACs fail to be cost-effective and commercially available for large-scale applications, due to their sophisticated and time-consuming synthesis (Table S1, Supporting Information) with a limited yield (typically at a milligram/gram level);([12] ii) Simply suspending particulate SACs in water for water purification, however, poses substantial barriers to their implementation, due to energy-intensive powder washing and recovery steps against catalyst loss and metal leaching. ii) Although the radical-nonradical synergy has shown improved oxidation performance of removing recalcitrant pollutants,[1315] SACs-directed synchronous radical-nonradical production highly relies on coupled heterometal engineering and nitrogen coordinatio moulation,(9,6, whi ichallegeby balan th stability/activity and cost.[18]

Toward above technical bottlenecks, anchoring atomic metals on membranes and millimetric spheres/aerogels provides a compelling opportunity to the concurrent large-scale production, application and recycling of SACs,[1921] and simultaneously shows the potential of manipulating a confinement effect in reinforcing the (non)radical exposure in their confining geometries.[10,2] For example, Dai et al.[23] reported gram-grade and sphere-shaped Co—Cu dual single-atom catalysts with a diameter of $1 0 { - } 1 2 \mathrm { m m }$ . O-bridged Co—Cu dual-atom sites in the catalysts improved PMS utilization and decontamination kinetics via reinforced interfacial proton-coupled electron transfer. However, SACs possess a wide variety of metal coordination numbers/configurations, orbital hybridization and spin states,[18] and no uniform conclusion/rule is available on the atomic-level precise regulation of metal sites for their mass production (especially up to a kilogram level).[6,24]

In heterogeneous catalysis, local structures of active metal sites usually evolve dynamically due to the local orbital hybridization between the host catalyst and the guest reactants.[2527] For instance, reversible stretching vibrations of O—Sr—O and Co/Ti—O bonds in a $\mathrm { C o / L a } { \cdot } \mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ catalyst are conducive to increasing the electron transfer number at the metal-PMS hybrid interface,[28] thereby realizing increased activity and stability of the catalyst in generating $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ and ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ for removal of ECs. The adaptive spin state tuning of confined CoMn dual single atoms is also crucial to the selective decomposition/transformation of PMS to enhance the singlet oxygen yield.[6] Despite their merits of disclosing the origin of catalysis behind the complex reaction proceses for deoveuntalyts,(,9 they evolutions of SACs are yet to be fully recogized in Fenton-like activation.

Here, we report an economical and scalable "frustrated Lewis pairs"-oriented lattice confinement method to rationally design tetrahedrally and octahedrally coordinated Co single-atom sites in millimetric $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ (SCoA) at a kilogram level, achieving a site-specific spin state modulation of lattice-confined Co atoms for synergistic radical-nonradical water purification. For the first time, we revealed that the synergistic adsorption of $\mathrm { C o - A l }$ sites (featuring $_ { \mathrm { C o - O - A l } }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { A l } }$ bonds) toward PMS induced an adaptive shift of local high-spin ${ \mathrm { C o } } ( { \mathrm { I I } } )$ from the tetrahedral to octahedral field. This shift provided an energy-efficient spin channel for subsequent electron/proton transport and conversion of high-spin $\mathrm { C o ( I I ) / C o ( I I I ) }$ to low-spin ${ \mathrm { C o } } ( { \mathrm { I I I } } )$ in the octahedral field, thus lowering the energy barrier of activating PMS to produce $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ and ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ concurrently. The adaptive single-atom site catalysis behavior of SCoA enabled its dynamically stable and durable performance for a high-efficient removal of typical pharmaceuticals, coexisting antibiotics and multiple ARGs. Moreover, the cost-effective millimetric SCoA catalysts $( \approx \$ 53/4,2- 3$ orders of magnitude cheaper than commercial SACs and nanocatalysts) are conducive to designing an integrated, monolithic catalysis device to deploy the large-scale application of the SACs for decentralized water cleanup. Overall, this study provides a versatile platform for a facile and low-cost design of high-efficient SACs with dynamic active-sites. With a great promise in eliminating a range of chemical and biological ECs from water ecosystems, the lattice confinement-triggered adaptive single-atom catalysis pave a smooth way for advancement of the global water resilience and sustainability.

# 2.1. Design of High-Efficient, Stable and Recyclable SCoA Catalysts

Text S1, Supporting Information for details). The selection of an easily accessible and cost-effective $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ support lies in its flexible alumina coordination numbers (four, five, six), abundant Lewis acids and a diversity of distortions,[30,31] which provide a dynamic lattice confinement to synchronously tune the $3 d$ orbital electron configurations and mass transfer of atomic Co sites. Considering the strong interaction between a basic monomethylamine and the surface acid sites of $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ ,[31] 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM, a basic molecule) was selected to bond with the acid sites of $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ , forming inorganic-organic hybrid "frustrated Lewis pairs";[32] meanwhile, 2-MIM serves as a chelating agent to coordinate cobalt cations, in situ generating a metal-organic complex on the $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ surface via a solvothermal reaction. The bonded Co cations are spontaneously embedded into distorted tetrahedral and octahedral geometries of $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ during pyrolysis of the metal-organic complex, thus producing atomic Co sites on the surface interface of host support with tailored metal coordination configurations and electron spin states.

The XRD patterns of SCoA show that, besides the diffraction peaks of $\mathsf { \Pi } ^ { \prime } { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ (JCPDS#: 100425), no other peak of Co-based oxides was observed (Figure S2, Supporting Information). This is due to two facts: i) low loading of non-crystalline cobalt atoms (below $0 . 1 6 ~ \mathrm { w t . } \%$ , as determined by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICPOES, Table S2, Supporting Information), and ii) a perfect lattice substitution of alumina atoms in $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ by cobalt cations. The slight shift in XRD peaks could be ascribed to the structural strain effects induced by the change of support diameter.[37] The highresolution Co $2 p$ XPS fitting indicates that most of cobalt cations are present in their divalent states (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, it is found that increasing the support diameters will slightly increase the cobalt valent states for SCoA (Table S2, Supporting Information).

A typical HAADF-STEM image (Figure 1F) clearly shows atomically dispersed bright dots on the support matrix (labeled with red circles). Based on quantitative measurement, the radial size of metal dots was 1.09 Å on average (Inset of Figure 1F), which is close to the theoretical radius of Co single atoms. This signifies the formation of atomically lattice-confined and dispersed Co throughout the $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ support. The energy dispersive X-ray mappings of HAADF-STEM (Figure 1G,H) suggest a homogeneous distribution of confined Co atoms. The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES, Figure 1I) shows that the energy adsorption threshold of SCoA1 lies intermediately between those of $\mathrm { C o O }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ but it is close to that of CoO. This suggests that the atomically dispersed Co sites uphold a positive valence-state $( \mathsf { C o } ^ { \delta + } { \approx } 2 )$ . The valence state of cobalt in SCoA1 was determined to be\~2 by a reported linear interpolation method with the standards of Co foil, CoO, ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ at a normalized absorption of $\approx 0 . 7 – 0 . 8$ on 3d-KXANES spectra.[38,39] These findings coincide with the observation of Co 2p XPS spectra (Figure S3A and Table S2, Supporting Information). The Fourier transformed $k ^ { 3 }$ -weighted extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) curve of SCoA1 (Figure 1J) shows one prominent peak of ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { O } }$ bond $( \approx 1 . 8 6 \mathrm { \AA } )$ coupling with two shoulder peaks for ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { A l } }$ and $_ { \mathrm { C o - O - A l } }$ bonds. No appreciable ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { C o } }$ coordination excludes the presence of cobalt clusters in SCoA1, further confirming the formation of atomic Co species via lattice confinement and substitution reactions.

The atomic configurations of SCoA1 were revealed by wavelet transform EXAFS (WT-EXAFS, Figure S4, Supporting Information). In line with the FT-EXAFS analysis, the WT-EXAFS contoue plots show one intensity maximum at $5 . 2 \mathrm { ~ \AA ^ { - 1 } }$ (Inset of Figure 1K), which is referred to the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { O } }$ bond. The signals are distinctly different from those for Co foil, CoO and ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ which have higher intensity maxima at 8.0 and $6 . 8 \mathring \mathrm { A } ^ { - 1 }$ , assigning to Co—Co and Co—O—Co coordination, respectively. The EXAFS curve-fitting analysis was carried out to further quantitatively determine the structural parameters of Co single-atom sites (Figure 1K; Figure S5, Supporting Information), and the corresponding parameters are shown in Table S3, (Supporting Information). Specifically, the least-square fitting for the first coordination shell probes that the Co atom was coordinated by 1.9 oxygen atoms on average, suggesting the presence of a ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 2 }$ configuration. Meanwhile, the Co atom was found to coordinate with 2.6 surrounding aluminum atoms on average, resulting in a ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { A l } } _ { 3 }$ configuration in SCoA1. Additionally, the Co atom can be coordinated by the O—Al ligand of support (0.3 on average).

Altogether, the above findings indicate that: i) The frustrated Lewis pairs-based "anchoring-chelating-conversion" proces can confine Co atoms in the alumina tetrahedral and octahedral lattice structures via the strong metal-support interactions; ii) The coupled $d \cdot p$ and $s { - } p$ orbital hybridization interactions are the key to form the lattice confined-Co single-atom sites on the millimetric support; iii) The unsaturated coordination of $\mathrm { C o - A l }$ and Co—O—Al bonds at the atomic interface could induce the formation of dangling bonds for the exposed alumina sites,[40] which in turn would shape the catalytic behavior of Co sites (shown in Section 2.3).

In all, these findings suggest that the spin-state of Co single-atom sites coupling with their coordination environments probably dominated the catalytic behavior of SCoA monoliths in PMS activation.

Figure 3B shows that SCoA1 monoliths exhibited high catalytic activity and reusability over their consecutive recycles. Specially, in the reaction time of $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , more than $9 8 \%$ of CBZ can be removed in ten-cycle runs without regeneration treatment (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Expectedly, the aqueous concentrations of cobalt ions leached from SCoA1 in each cycle $( < 5 8 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { g } ~ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , Figure 3B; Table S4, Supporting Information) are much lower than the suggested Co discharge standard of Chinese groundwater $( 1 0 0 0 \mu \ g \ L ^ { - 1 }$ , GB 3838-2002), implying their excellent stability in PMS activation. On the basis of comparative studies (Figure S9, Supporting Information), it is found that the adsorption of SCoA1 and $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ or oxidation of PMS has a negligible role in the removal of CBZ $( < 4 \% )$ , and that the catalytic activity and stability of SCoA1 monoliths are superior to their supports and precursors. We attribute the excellent activity, reusability and stability of SCoA1 monoliths to their tailored spin state and stable metal coordination configuration.

# 2.4. Adaptive Cobalt Crystal-Field and Spin-State Transition in PMS Activation

As shown in Figure 3C-E, there exist significant changes in UV vis, Co L-edge and O K-edge spectra of SCoA1 after activating PMS. An increased intensity of singlet peak at $2 2 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and decreased triplet peaks $( 5 4 0 - 6 3 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ suggest a transition trend of coordinated Co atom sites from tetrahedral to octahedral field.

Compared to fresh SCoA1, the curve of Co $L _ { 3 }$ -edge of used SCoA1 was evolved into doublet peaks with an equal height (i.e., 777.83 and $7 7 8 . 8 1 \mathrm { e V } )$ . This finding also implies a change of the coordination of Co atom sites from tetrahedral to octahedral field.[48]

The fitting results of Co $L _ { 3 }$ -edge clearly verify the above findings. As depicted in Figures 2D and 3E, during the PMS activation, the ratio of HS $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + } \mathrm { _ { T d } ^ { \phantom { + } } }$ in SCoA1 catalysts had reduced from $5 9 . 3 \%$ to $4 4 . 0 \%$ , while that for all the octahedral Co sites had increased by $3 7 . 6 \%$ (from $4 0 . 7 \%$ to $5 6 . 0 \%$ . Interestingly, a clear reduction in the intensity of peak at $5 4 1 . 2 \mathrm { e V }$ could been detected after PMS activation (Figure 3F), strongly indicating a change in the interactions between $\mathrm { O - A l }$ and $\mathrm { O - } \mathrm { C o }$ bonds during the crystal field shift of Co atom sites in SCoA1. We think this is due to the changes of amorphous surface and structural strain during the catalytic activation (evidenced by the shift in XRD peak/intensity in Figure S11, Supporting Information).[37,43] This characteristic could reinforce the stability of dynamic Al—O—Co/Co—O bonds, thus sustaining high-efficient catalytic activity.[28]

Based on the above atom structure model of SCoA1 (Figure 2G), we further carried out DFT calculations to elucidate whether the unique crystal field transition of SCoA1 toward PMS activation is thermodynamically feasible. Impressively, it is found that, upon attachment of the terminal oxygen of PMS molecules to Co single-atom sites, tetrahedrally coordinated Co sites with local high-spin states tended to convert into octahedrally coordinated ones under the synergism of adjacent Al and Co atoms in SCoA1 (Figure 3G). The theoretical finding on the self-adaptive crystal field shift is in good accordance with the results of Co $L _ { 3 }$ . edge and O K-edge spectra, which inspires us to further reveal the decomposition behavior of PMS and the generation process of ROS.

# 2.5. Mechanisms of Adaptive Crystal-Field and Spin-State Transition for ROS Formation

Integrated theoretical and experimental studies have been performed to comprehensively elucidate the interfacial processes of PMS activation and ROS formation via the adaptive crystalfield and spin-state transition of Co single-atom sites. Preliminary DFT calculations indicate that deprotonation of the terminal $- \mathrm { O H }$ groups of PMS $\mathrm { ( H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - } \mathrm { ) }$ molecules is thermodynamically favored upon their adsorption on the positively charged atomic Co sites, resulting in formation of deprotonated $\mathrm { S O } _ { 5 } ^ { 2 - }$ at the active sites (Figure 4A-C). This agrees well with the previously documented coupled proton-electron process for PMS activation.[23,55]

Given the unsaturated ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { A l } }$ and Co—O-Al bonds in SCoA (Section 2.1), we propose that the dandling bonds on exposed alumina surface could participate in the $\mathrm { O - } \mathrm { O }$ bond decomposition due to their reduction potential in PMS activation. Therefore, in DFT calculations, we modelled three different combinations of Co—Co, Co—Al and Al—Al pathways and investigated their potential role in ROS formation during Co singleatom site catalysis. The adsorption configurations and evolutions of deprotonated $\mathrm { S O } _ { 5 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ at the three scenerios are shown in Figure 4A-C. Considering the strong oxidation potential of the $\mathrm { O - } \mathrm { O }$ bonds in the deprotonated $\mathrm { S O } _ { 5 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ ions, we compared their adsorption states under the Co—Co, Co—Al, and Al—Al pathways. It is found that, when the terminal $\mathrm { O - } \mathrm { O }$ bonds interact with $\mathrm { C o - A l }$ simultaneously, it can induce a more stable adsorption state compared to ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { C o } }$ site (Figure 4D), which indicates that the unsaturated Al atoms may have a stronger adsorption capacity than the four-coordinated Co atoms. This was further confirmed by the case of pure Al—Al combination, which presented the highest adsorption energy of deprotonated $\mathrm { S O } _ { 5 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ ions (Figure 4D).

Subsequently, we analyzed the dissociation steps of intermediates for ROS formation. It is found that the overly strong adsorption (induced by the Al—Al combination) is not conducive to the release of reaction products, making the overall catalytic activity of the Al—Al site be significantly lower than that of the Co—Al site (Figure 4D). From this, we have the following conclusion: unsaturated Al can enhance the initial adsorption of reactants and dissociation of intermediates during PMS activation, while four-coordinated Co single-atom site can avoid excessive adsorption and achieve the optimal performance through the Co—Al synergy. Under such a synergistic effect, tetrahedral Co atoms with a localized high-spin state tend to interact with the terminal oxygen of PMS molecules via the $3 d - 2 p$ orbital coupling, which can spontaneously induce the coordination reconstruction to produce thermodynamically stable octahedral Co sites (Figure 4E). All these reactions synchronously promote radical and nonradical formation (Figure 4F). In addition, as shown in Figure 4G, we also probed four adsorption forms of surface-bonded atom oxygen $( ^ { \ast } \mathrm { O } )$ . It is found that, compared to $\mathrm { C o } _ { 4 \mathrm { c } } { - } \mathrm { O - } \mathrm { C o } _ { 4 \mathrm { c } }$ and $\mathrm { C o } _ { 4 \mathrm { c } } { - } \mathrm { O - } \mathrm { C o } _ { 3 \mathrm { c } }$ interfaces, the atomic interfaces of $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 \mathrm { c } } { - } \mathrm { O }$ and $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 \mathrm { c } } { - } \mathrm { O - } \mathrm { C o } _ { 3 \mathrm { c } }$ fall into a V-shaped region, which can cause extremely strong $^ { * } \mathrm { O }$ adsorption and thus prevent the release of singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 } )$ .

The above theoretical calculations suggest that dynamic Co single-atom sites of SCoA1 tend to activate PMS to produce $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ and ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ concurrently under the ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } { \mathrm { A l } }$ synergy. To further confirm the types of ROS and their contribution to pollutant removal, we used 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidinol (TEMP) and 5,5-dimethyl-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as the potential trapping reagents for electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) characterization. Accordingly, using different quenchers (Table S5, Supporting Information) and 9,10-diphenylanthracene (DPA), we performed integrated scavenging/probe tests.

As shown in Figure 4K, with TEMP as the spin-trapping reagent, a distinct signal of TMEP. $^ 1 { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ adduct with a 1:1:1 intensity was detected in the SCoA1/PMS system. When DMPO was used as the spin-trapping reagent for radical identification, a sextat signal of DMPO. $\cdot \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ adduct was recorded (Figure 4L). On the contrary, the detected signals of DMPO-OH and $\mathrm { D M P O { \cdot } S O _ { 4 } { \cdot } ^ { \bullet - } }$ adducts are very weak (Figure 4M). In most cases, if hydroxyl radicals generated in an aqueous environment, a quartet signal of DMPO-\*OH adduct with a 1:2:2:1 intensity can be easily traced in the EPR spectra.[56] Thus, the weak DMPO. ${ \cdot } \mathrm { O H }$ adduct can exclude the dominance of hydroxyl radicals in the SCoA1/PMS system. However, due to the very weak EPR signal of DMPO$\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ adduct and its potential misjudgment,[56] the formation of $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ and its dominance in the pollutant removal need a further verification.

As the scavenging experiments suggested (Figure 4N,O), addition of tert-butyl alcohol (TBA, a typical scavenger of •OH) exerted a slightly inhibitory effect on removal of CBZ and phenol. # 3. Conclusion

A simple and cost-effective frustrated Lewis pairs-assisted lattice confinement strategy was sucessfully developed for confining Co atoms in the tetrahedral and octahedral coordination configurations of millimetric $\gamma { \cdot } \mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ (SCoA), achieving highly recycable and stable single-atom site monolithic catalysts at the kg-level for sustained water cleanup. The adaptive cystal-field and spin-state transition of local high-spin Co enables the optimization of reactant adsorption configurations at the atomic interface, mediating a thermodynamically favorable route for the $0 { \mathrm { - H / O \mathrm { - } O } }$ cleavage and synchronous production of $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ and ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ in PMS activation. The dynamic single-atom catalysis endows the optimized SCoA robust acitivity and stability in 10 consecutive Fenton-like reactions $( > 9 8 \%$ CBZ removal within $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ for each cycle). Impressively, the optimized SCoA show great potential in removing 9 coexisting trace antibiotics and 12 coexisting ARGs $( > 9 0 \%$ for each), and their synthesis cost is ${ \approx } 3$ orders of magnitude lower than commercial $3 d$ -metal SACs, and even 2 orders of magnitude lower than nanocatalysts. Although our work focuses on the Fenton-like oxidation of typical chemical and biological emerging contaminants, the lattice confinement-reinforced dynamically stable single-atom catalyst platform can also be applicable to heterogeneous ozonation in addressing more recalcitrant EfOMs, thereby ensuring safe clean water production, facilitating sustainable water reclamation and advancing water resilience against the global water crisis.

Mechanism Investigation: Integrated characterization techiques and analysis methods were used for reaction mechanism studies: 1) Different quenchers (TBA, MeOH, DMSO, HD and BQ) were added to the target solutions containing CBZ or phenol following the procedures of the above experiments. 2) With DMPO and TEMP as the spin trapping reagents, an EPR spectrometer (Bruker ELEXSYS E500) was used to record the signals of different ROS generated in the bulk solutions. 3) The model structure analysis and theoretical calculations were performed based on the density functional theory using the CASTEP (Cambridge sequential total energy package). More information is available in Text S3 (Supporting Information).
