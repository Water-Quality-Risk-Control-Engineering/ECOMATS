# . Introduction

Industrial wastewater containing organic contaminants poses a significant threat to human health and environment [1]. These hazardous organic compounds are chemically stable and non-biodegradable [2], making their removal from aquatic environments challenging using nondestructive techniques, such as flocculation [3], adsorption [4], coagulation and/or filtration [5,6]. Chemical and biological processes like chemical oxidation, plasma, disinfection/ozonation and chlorination, although effective for water treatment, can generate toxic by-products [7,8]. Therefore, there is a need for innovative treatment methods capable of completely removing persistent contaminants.

Heterogeneous photocatalysis, an efficient and eco-friendly advanced oxidation process (AOP), offers distinct advantages over other treatment techniques. It can effectively degrade target molecules, leading to high mineralization [911]. This process can efficiently mineralize numerous drugs, transforming them into comparatively nontoxic final products such as water $\mathrm { ( H _ { 2 } O ) }$ and carbon dioxide $\left( \mathrm { C O _ { 2 } } \right)$ [12]. The $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ is a widely used photocatalytic semiconductor, known for its high surface area, affordability, low toxicity, photochemical stability, and commercial availability [12].

However, the reactor used in the photocatalytic process typically relies on external light, which can be extremely energy-intensive (20150 W) for a relatively low durability, usually around $1 0 0 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ [13]. Consequently, optimizing the contact between the catalyst, the light, and the contaminant has become a primary challenge to overcome poor light penetration. To address this issue, researchers have proposed to use of immobilized photocatalysts and $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ coated optical-fibers as a promising technique for the environmental depollution [14].

Recently, luminous textiles have been explored for photocatalytic water treatment, wherein optical-fibers are connected to an ultraviolet light-emitting-diode (UV-LED) source. Luminous photocatalytic textiles offer several advantages; (i) They enhance the interactions between the solution and the support, (ii) They consume less energy through optimized LED usage, and (i) They optimize the contact between the photocatalyst support and the transmitted light [14]. The performance of luminous textiles has been evaluated for the degradation of various air pollutants, including formaldehyde [13], toluene and acetic acid [15], Butane-2,3-dione, and Heptan-2-one [16], as well as chloroform and glutaraldehyde [17]. However, only a limited number of research works have reported the use of luminous textiles for removing antibiotics from wastewater [18].

In the present study, the antibiotic Ciprofloxacin (CIP) was chosen as the model pollutant molecule. This synthetic antibiotic is frequently detected in water sources at concentrations ranging from $1 \ \mu { \bf g } / \mathrm { L }$ to 31 mg/L [19]. The presence of CIP in the aquatic environment contributes to the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria [20], posing a threat to both human health and the environment. Various advanced oxidation processes have been employed to remove CIP antibiotic, including the AOP method with Ferrate (VI) from Extreme Base Electrosynthesis [21], Co-Mn oxide supported by carbon fiber frameworks [22] electrocoagulation using iron electrodes, and electro-peroxone application [23,24]. The primary objective of this paper is to investigate the CIP degradation efficiency of the compact photocatalytic batch reactor. To the best of our knowledge, the photodegradation of CIP using luminous photocatalytic textiles has not been previously reported. A comparative study between three different configurations of luminous textile reactors is conducted. The influence of CIP initial concentration and UV light intensity on removal and mineralization efficiency is examined using the most efficient design. Furthermore, an intriguing challenge is addressed in this study: the impact of the addition of oxidants, specifically hydrogen peroxide and persulfate, on CIP mineralization is investigated.

This study represents the first investigation in which hydrogen peroxide and persulfate oxidants are added to a compact reactor with a luminous textile catalyst to enhance CIP degradation. The types of radicals and their role in CIP mineralization are also explored using external radical scavengers. Finally, the photocatalytic performance of luminous textiles in various water matrices is assessed to understand the effects of other ions present in the water. To our knowledge, no previous work has focused on the treatment of various water matrices using a compact reactor with a luminous textile catalyst for CIP removal.

The influence of the initial CIP concentration on the photocatalytic degradation efficiency, for DF luminous-textile reactor, was investigated for five CIP concentrations in the range of 2 to $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ . As reported in Fig. 5(a), the degradation efficiency decreased from $9 9 . 7 ~ \%$ to $2 8 . 4 ~ \%$ and the mineralization yield decreased from $4 4 . 7 \%$ to $1 4 . 7 \%$ after 360 min, when CIP concentration was increased from 2 to $1 0 \mathrm { \ m g / L }$ respectively.

The photocatalytic activity diminishes as the antibiotic concentration rises due to the increased quantity of adsorbed contaminants on the photocatalyst surface. The adsorbed CIP molecules can limit the number of photons reaching the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ surface. Consequently, the production of reactive oxygen species on the surface also decreases [30]. Additionally, the increase in the initial CIP concentration leads to a reduction in available active sites. Therefore, in a photocatalytic process consisting of successive reactions, the competition for active sites on the catalyst becomes more pronounced.

As depicted in Fig. 5(b), the mineralization efficiency decreased from $4 0 \%$ to $1 5 \%$ as the CIP inlet concentration increased from 2 to $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ . This observation underscores the importance of the number of available active sites on the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ surface and the quantity of UV photons reaching the catalyst surface in controlling mineralization. It can be inferred that the optimization of active sites on the surface, which includes factors such as the weight of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and the total exposed area, should be based on the pollutant concentration to achieve complete degradation and mineralization.

# 3.4. Effect of UV light intensity on CIP removal

Given that the production of electron-hole pair by UV-light, the UV light intensity is a crucial parameter in antibiotic degradation. To assess the impact of light intensity on the CIP photodegradation process, various experiments were conducted at different light intensities using the DF configuration. The calculated intensities were 0.083, 0.150 and $0 . 2 5 5 \mathrm { \ m W / c m ^ { 2 } }$ using three different LED sources. Fig. 7 illustrates the effect of UV light intensity on CIP degradation and mineralization efficiencies.

The results demonstrated that the CIP removal improved as the irradiation intensity increased, which can be attributed to the higher production of hydroxyl radicals on the catalyst surface. Indeed, under high UV-light intensity, electron-hole pair formation predominates, and the recombination of electron-hole pairs becomes negligible. In contrast, at low UV light intensity, a competition between the separation and recombination of electron-hole pairs occurs, reducing the formation of free radicals and subsequently decreasing antibiotic photodegradation efficiency [19]. The results obtained indicate that the DF luminous textile exhibited highly satisfactory photodegradation rates, even at the lowest light intensity tested. As depicted in Fig. 7(b), the increase in UV light intensity from 0.083 to 0.255 (3 fold) $\mathrm { \ m W / c m ^ { 2 } }$ has raised the mineralization efficiency from 27 to $4 2 \ \%$ (1.6 fold). Overall, it is evident that an increase in UV light intensity has led to an improvement in mineralization. . However, the impact on mineralization is relatively minor. For instance, the ratio between conversion and mineralization for 0.15 and $0 . 2 5 5 \mathrm { \ m W / c m 2 }$ does not exhibit significant changes; specifically, the conversion/mineralization ratio is 2.5 for $0 . 1 5 \mathrm { ~ m W } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ and 2.4 for $0 . 2 5 5 ~ \mathrm { m W / c m ^ { 2 } }$ .This finding suggests that the excess UV light intensity has had an insignificant effect on mineralization. Therefore, it can be proposed that CIP mineralization is primarily governed by the interaction between CIP, $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ , and UV photons, rather than being solely dependent on the quantity of available photons.

# 4. Conclusion

In this study, the degradation of CIP in a compact batch reactor using a photocatalytic luminous textile reactor was thoroughly examined. The "double-face" (DF) luminous textile reactor exhibited the highest degradation efficiency $( 9 9 . 7 ~ \% )$ and mineralization yield $( 4 0 . 3 ~ \% )$ compared to the "mono-face" (MF) and "double-face without $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ^ { \cdot \cdot }$ (DFwithout $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ )luminous textiles. This improved performance can be attributed to the uniform distribution of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ on the optical fibers' surfaces on both sides of the DF reactor, enhancing CIP removal and byproduct mineralization. Characterization of the optical fibers of the DF reactor before and after photocatalytic reactions using FTIR and SEM confirmed the uniform $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ distribution, further supporting its role in enhancing CIP degradation and mineralization. The study revealed that the efficiency of CIP degradation and mineralization decreased as the initial concentration of CIP increased or as the UV light intensity decreased. The most favorable photocatalytic activity was observed when the initial CIP concentration was at $2 \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ , and the UV light intensity was $0 . 1 5 \mathrm { \ m W / c m ^ { 2 } }$ .The decrease in photocatalytic activity at higher CIP concentrations can be attributed to the limited availability of active sites on the catalyst surface. When the concentration of CIP is high, these active sites can become saturated with CIP molecules, reducing the number of available sites for photocatalytic reactions. Under conditions of low UV light intensity, there is a competition between the separation and recombination of electron-hole pairs. This competition results in fewer free radicals being formed, leading to a decrease in the efficiency of CIP photodegradation. In essence, at lower UV light intensities, the rate of electron-hole recombination becomes more pronounced, impeding the formation of the reactive radicals necessary for effective photocatalysis.

The highest $\mathbf { k _ { a p p } }$ constant $( 0 . 0 1 1 4 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } } \cdot$ ) and mineralization level $( 5 4 . 7 ~ \% )$ were achieved with $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ The addition of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ generated $\cdot O H$ radicals, reduced the electron-hole recombination, and enhanced the photocatalytic efficiency. Furthermore, the introduction of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ molecules into the system increased the rate of ·OH radical recombination. Additionally, the highest $\bf k _ { a p p }$ constant $( 0 . 0 1 3 0 \mathrm { \ m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ and mineralization level $( 6 1 . 2 ~ \% )$ were attained with $2 0 2 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ of PS. The inclusion of PS as an oxidant to the solution resulted in faster degradation kinetics. However, an excess of PS molecules in the system led to the scavenging effect of reactive species. The addition of oxidants significantly improved the photocatalytic performance of the luminous textile. The study explored the influence of various scavengers on CIP degradation, confirming that $H \dot { O _ { 2 } }$ , $O _ { 2 } ^ { - }$ , and $\cdot { O H }$ radicals, played pivotal roles in the CIP degradation reaction. Furthermore, the photocatalytic performance of luminous textile was assessed in different water matrices, shedding light on the underlying mechanisms of CIP degradation involving various oxidative processes. The results revealed that CIP degraded more rapidly in ultra-pure water (UPW) compared to tap water (TW) and synthetic water (SW). The degradation efficiency decreased progressively from $9 9 . 7 \%$ in UPW to $6 3 . 6 \%$ in TW and 46.1 $\%$ in SW. This decrease in efficiency could be attributed to the detrimental effects of nitrate, chloride, sulfate, and carbonate ions present in TW and SW, which negatively impacted TiO2 photocatalysis, resulting in reduced CIP degradation efficiency and mineralization yields. In summary, this study showcased the remarkable photocatalytic prowess of the luminous textile for degrading the antibiotic CIP. This was achieved by enhancing the interaction between CIP, the catalyst surface, and UV light. Additionally, the use of LED technology significantly reduced energy consumption, making this approach highly promising for environmental applications.
