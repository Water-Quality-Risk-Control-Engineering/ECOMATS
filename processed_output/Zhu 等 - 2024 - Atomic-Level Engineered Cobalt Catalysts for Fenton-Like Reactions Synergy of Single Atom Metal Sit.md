# Atomic-Level Engineered Cobalt Catalysts for Fenton-Like Reactions: Synergy of Single Atom Metal Sites and Nonmetal-Bonded Functionalities

Zhong-Shuai Zhu, Yantao Wang, Xiaoguang Duan,\* Pengtang Wang, Shuang Zhong, Shiying Ren, Xing Xu,\* Baoyu Gao, Jitraporn (Pimm) Vongsvivut, and Shaobin Wang\*

Single atom catalysts (SACs) are atomic-level-engineered materials with high intrinsic activity. Catalytic centers of SACs are typically the transition metal (TM)nonmetal coordination sites, while the functions of coexisting non-TM-bonded functionalities are usually overlooked in catalysis. Herein, the scalable preparation of carbon-supported cobalt-anchored SACs (CoCN) with controlled $\subset o \mathrm { - N }$ sites and free functional N species is reported. The role of metal- and nonmetal-bonded functionalities in the SACs for peroxymonosulfate (PMS)-driven Fenton-like reactions is first systematically studied, revealing their contribution to performance improvement and pathway steering. Experiments and computations demonstrate that the $C o \mathrm { - } \mathsf { N } _ { 3 } \mathsf { C }$ coordination plays a vital role in the formation of a surface-confined PMS\* complex to trigger the electron transfer pathway and promote kinetics because of the optimized electronic state of Co centers, while the nonmetal-coordinated graphitic N sites act as preferable pollutant adsorption sites and additional PMS activation sites to accelerate electron transfer. Synergistically, CoCN exhibits ultrahigh activity in PMS activation for $\pmb { p }$ -hydroxybenzoic acid oxidation, achieving complete degradation within 10 min with an ultrahigh turnover frequency of $0 . 3 8 \mathrm { \ m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , surpassing most reported materials. These findings offer new insights into the versatile functions of N species in SACs and inspire rational design of high-performance catalysts in complicated heterogeneous systems.

# 1. Introduction

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have been widely recognized as a technology for converting refractory and toxic pollutants into harmless mineralized products.[] As one of the most representative AOPs, peroxymonosulfate (PMS)-induced Fenton-like reaction shows great potential and attracts widespread attention.[2] The efficiency of AOPs significantly relies on the catalysts, particularly their role in activating PMS to generate reactive oxygen species to attack organic pollutants.[3] Although abundant transition-metal (TM)-based catalysts have been developed for Fenton-like reactions, the practical applications have been limited by their slow degradation kinetics, metal leaching, and low TM utilization efficiency.[4] Therefore, developing a scalable synthesis protocol of SACs with high intrinsic activity and well-defined molecular structures to address these challenges is essential.

Single-atom catalysts (SACs) with atomically dispersed metal sites and low surface free energy can maximize the exposure of metal sites to ensure high stability, kinetics, and atomic efficiency in catalysis.[5] In particular, nitrogen (N)-coordinated-carbonbased SACs, favored for their simplicity in preparation and cost-effectiveness, have emerged as promising catalysts in PMS-induced Fenton-like reactions for pollutant degradation.[6] Commonly, the presence of N can help accommodate metal atoms via forming TM—N sites with strong metal-support interactions, thus attaining superior molecular stability. On the other hand, different types and numbers of N coordination can precisely regulate the electronic structure of SACs and substrate conductivity to achieve on-demand activity and selectivity.[7] Given this, substantial studies have highlighted the enhanced activity of N-coordinatedTM-based SACs in PMS activation and pollutant degradation.[8] For example, Zhang and co-workers demonstrated that tailored $\mathrm { C o N _ { 4 } }$ sites preferred to bond with PMS to produce singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 } )$ as reactive species for efficient pollutant degradation.[9] Guan and co-workers revealed that the $\mathrm { F e N } _ { 4 }$ sites can improve the pollutant degradation via forming high valence iron species $\mathrm { F e N _ { 4 } { = } O }$ intermediate).[10] While these studies enhance our understanding of the structural engineering of TM-based SACs in PMS-induced Fenton-like reactions, the resulting improvement in catalyst performance is still modest and falls short of meeting the requirements for practical applications. In addition, the identification and clarification of the catalytic sites responsible for PMS activation and pollutant adsorption, crucial for steering the reaction pathway, are still unclear and subject to debate. This lack of clarity poses a significant obstacle to mechanism elucidation and precise catalyst design.

Except for the TM—N coordination, other N specie including graphitic, pyridinic, and pyridinic N also coexist as individual sites in the N-coordinated-TM-based SACs.[11] These N sites also show functions in regulating charge distribution, fine-tuning conductivity, and creating more polarized point defects to improve the intrinsic activity of inert carbon materials, which has been reported in energy storage and green catalysis.[12] Nevertheless, due to challenges in controllable synthesis and the complex reaction mechanisms, the functions of these N sites in Ncoordinated-TM-based SACs for Fenton-like reactions are rarely elucidated.[13]

In this study, we designed a platform of carbon-supported cobalt-based SACs (denoted as CoCN) with both Co-bonded and free N sites via a simple adsorption—pyrolysis strategy method. Via designated materials engineering and in situ analyses, an electron transfer pathway (ETP) with boosted kinetics was triggered and evidenced on CoCN for PMS-induced catalytic oxidation. Accordingly, a significantly improved activity with an ultrahigh turnover frequency (TOF) of $0 . 3 8 \ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ was achieved on CoCN for $p$ -hydroxybenzoic acid (HBA) degradation. Based on theoretical calculation and established correlations between N categories and apparent kinetic parameters, the roles and synergy of $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ sites and free N sites in CoCN during catalysis were studied and first clarified. The $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ sites with tailored electronic state preferred to adsorb and activate PMS rapidly, while the free graphitic N remained PMS activation but more favorable for pollutant adsorption preferentially. This work intends to enlighten advanced catalyst design with customizable species and population of active sites for high performance in catalytic oxidation reactions.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Synthesis and Structural Analysis of CoCN

CoCN was fabricated by a controllable adsorption—annealing method (Figure 1a). For it, chitosan with abundant biofunctional groups was used to prepare a N-doped carbonaceous substrate.[14] ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ and ${ \mathrm { Z n } } ^ { 2 + }$ will coordinate with chitosan via oxygen-containing functional groups to form self-assembled and insoluble metalchitosan complexes. Dicyandiamide (DCD) as a N source was adopted to modify the carbon support with various $_ \mathrm { N }$ species and stabilize the metal ions within the matrix.[15] During annealing, DCD will transform into a layered graphitic carbon nitride at the temperature around $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to limit the polymerization of the complexes and ensure the uniform distribution of metal atoms.[16] With the annealing temperature increased, the generated graphitic carbon nitride will undergo disintegration to release a large amount of carbonized gas. Given this, it resulted in the formation of a defect-rich, nitrogen-doped carbon framework, thereby providing numerous sites for anchoring metal atoms.[17] Finally, following the evaporation of Zn at $1 0 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , Co sites were atomically dispersed. The obtained samples were denoted as $\mathrm { C o C N } { \cdot } x .$ where $_ x$ represented the mass ratio of DCD to precursor (0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 15).

X-ray diffraction patterns of CoCN and N-doped carbon (CN) were shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). No peak of Co nanoparticles was observed except for the sample without adding DCD (CoCN-0). Accordingly, the formation of atomically dispersed Co atoms was attributed to the N complexation, ensuring strong supportmetal interactions to prevent their aggregation into metal nanoparticles. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed the morphology of CoCN-15 and CN-15 as ultrathin graphitic nanosheets (Figure 1b and Figures S2 and S3 (Supporting Information)). High resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images showed the bright dots under high magnifications (Figure 1c), corresponding to atomically isolated Co single sites. The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping also verified the homogeneous distributions of Co, N, and C on CoCN-15 (Figure 1d).

Synchrotron-based X-ray absorption spectroscopy techniques were also performed to determine the atomic structure and local coordination environment of Co atoms in CoCN-15. The $k ^ { 3 }$ - weighted Fourier transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectra (FT-EXAFS, Figure S4 (Supporting Information)) of the Co K-edge of CoCN-15 exhibited only one prominent peak at ${ \approx } 1 . 3 8 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ , assigned to $\mathrm { C o - N }$ bonds in the first shell which excluded the presence of Co clusters and nanoparticles.[18] These results were further confirmed by the wavelet transform of EXAFS (Figure S4, Supporting Information). X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analysis revealed a pronounced pre-edge peak (1s-to-3d, 7710 eV) for CoCN-15 (Figure 1e), highlighting asymmetric Co coordination, distinct from $\mathrm { C o - N _ { 4 } }$ in CoPc, which showed the negligible peak due to $\mathrm { D } _ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ (square planar) symmetry. The absorption edge position of CoCN-15 was located between Co foil and CoPc, indicating that the valence state of Co atoms was lower than CoPc $( + 2 )$ . The coordination number of Co for $\mathrm { N } / \mathrm { C }$ was deduced to be 3.8 according to the EXAFS fitting curves (Figure 1f and Table S1 (Supporting Information)). To further validate the accurate coordination structure of CoCN15 obtained from EXAFS curve fitting, we conducted the density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The formation energy for $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ at $- 3 . 2 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ was lower than that for $\mathrm { C o - N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C } _ { 2 } { \cdot } 1$ $( - 1 . 8 2 \mathrm { e V } ) , \mathrm { C o - N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C } _ { 2 } { \cdot } 2 ( - 1 . 6 7 \mathrm { e V } )$ , and ${ \mathrm { C o } } { - } \mathrm { N C } _ { 3 }$ $( - 0 . 5 7 \mathrm { e V } )$ configurations (Figure S5, Supporting Information), indicating that the isolated Co atoms would adopt a favorable structure with three N atoms and one C atom, forming the $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ coordination.

# 2.2. HBA Degradation in Fenton-Like reaction

HBA was selected as a model pollutant to investigate the degradation performance, because it could be oxidized by both radical and nonradical species.[21] Figure 2a depicted that PMS alone cannot degrade HBA, and that the removal efficiency of HBA was gradually improved from CoCN-0 to CoCN-15. CoCN-15 exhibited the best performance for HBA degradation, where the rate constant $( k _ { \mathrm { o b s } } )$ value $( 0 . 3 2 3 \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } } )$ almost increased by 13 times compared to CoCN-3 $( 2 . 4 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ ,Figure S11, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the strong coordination at the Co single site led to minimal $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + }$ leaching, showing a minimal contribution to PMS activation (Figure S12, Supporting Information). The removal rates of CN were in the similar variation trend to CoCN (Figure S13, Supporting Information), and the dosage of DCD controlled the N species and population in the carbon substrate.[22] The $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ values of CoCN were significantly enhanced compared to CN (Figure S14, Supporting Information). Additionally, adjusting the initial $\mathrm { p H }$ from 3 to 9 had little influence on HBA degradation (Figure S15, Supporting Information), illustrating the broad $\mathsf { p H }$ compatibility of CoCN-15 for HBA degradation. Through normalizing $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ to a specific sur$k _ { \mathrm { S S A } }$ $\mathrm { g } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , referring to specific activity), we investigated the intrinsic activity of catalysts.[23] As shown in Figure 2b, the variation trend of $k _ { \mathrm { S S A } }$ in a function of DCD dosage was similar to $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ for both CoCN and CN. However, the $k _ { \mathrm { S S A } }$ values of CoCN were significantly improved compared to CN. Particularly, CoCN-15 also exhibited the highest $k _ { \mathrm { S S A } }$ $( 3 . 3 6 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \ \mathrm { g \ m ^ { - 2 } \ m i n ^ { - 1 } } )$ . All these indicated the high intrinsic activity of Co single atoms for PMS activation.

To further validate the ETP between the HBA and $\mathrm { C o - N - P M S ^ { \ast } }$ intermediates, we constructed a dualcompartment galvanic oxidation setup (Figure 2f). Differently, the obtained slopes of graphitic N in CoCN were all much lower than those of the active $\mathrm { C o - N }$ sites, demonstrating the primary function of $\mathrm { C o - N }$ in promoting the three critical factors in ETP. Therefore, all the above observations demonstrated that $\mathrm { C o - N }$ held superior activity to graphitic N in activating PMS, in light of both elevating the complex potential and accelerating ETP oxidation kinetics.

# 2.4. Theoretical Calculation

DFT calculations were further employed to elucidate the above findings. As shown in Figure 4a, both $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ and graphitic N structures exhibited metallic characteristics in terms of the bandgap. Differently, the $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ structures showed lower defect levels compared to graphitic N, thereby achieving better conductivity for electron transfer. Notably, the higher electronic state near the Fermi level in $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ structure was not solely attributed to the Co atom. Instead, the Co atom optimized the electronic structure of surrounding C and N atoms by filling the defect levels, thereby enhancing conductivity during ETP oxida$\mathrm { C o } 3 \mathrm { d }$ orbitals $( \mathrm { d } _ { x \gamma }$ and ${ \mathrm { d } } _ { \gamma z }$ and the $\textnormal { N } 2 \textnormal { p }$ orbital $( \mathsf { p } _ { z } )$ near the Fermi level that promoted the rearrangement of the electronic structure in $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ (Figures S43 and S44, Supporting Information). Moreover, the analysis from atomic distribution revealed that the 3d orbital of the Co atom and the 2p orbital of the N atom had an obvious overlap, which indicated that incorporating Co enhanced the covalent bonding between Co and N atoms through strong orbital hybridization (Figure S45, Supporting Information). This enhanced covalent bonding facilitated stronger chemical interactions and resulted in a more favorable environment for the adsorption and activation of PMS on catalyst surface.

The adsorption of PMS on graphitic $_ \mathrm { N }$ and $\mathrm { C o } { - } \mathrm { N } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ sites as well as their adsorption energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } \right)$ and Bader charges $( \boldsymbol { \mathcal { Q } } )$ were further analyzed (Figures S46S48 and Table S3, Supporting Information). PMS offered three types of O atoms for adsorption, a hydroxyl oxygen atom $( \mathrm { O } _ { \mathrm { t y p e I } } )$ , a peroxy oxygen atom $\scriptstyle ( \mathrm { O } _ { \mathrm { t y p e I I } } )$ , and a terminal oxygen atom $\scriptstyle \left( \mathrm { O } _ { \mathrm { t y p e I I I } } \right)$ (Figure S49, Supporting Information). A chemical bond between Co and $\mathrm { O } _ { \mathrm { t y p e I I I } }$ on PMS was formed in the $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ configuration (Figure 4b), leading to strong chemical adsorption and electron transfer for PMS. However, graphitic N tended to interact with $\mathrm { O _ { t y p e I I } }$ on PMS through van der Waals forces and cannot form stable chemical bonds (Figure 4b), suggesting that the adsorption capacity of graphitic N on PMS was relatively weak. This difference can be clearly reflected by $E _ { \mathrm { a d s } }$ ; $E _ { \mathrm { a d s } }$ values for $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ toward three type adsorption configurations of PMS were all more negative than those on graphitic N. This essentially suggested that electron transfer between the catalyst and PMS was enhanced, compared to graphitic N. Furthermore, the interaction between $\mathrm { C o ~ t } _ { 2 \mathrm { g } }$ $( \mathrm { d } _ { \gamma z } )$ and $\mathrm { e } _ { \mathrm { g } } \ ( \mathrm { d } _ { z } ^ { \ 2 } )$ orbitals with the O $\mathtt { p } _ { z }$ orbitals promoted the adsorption and charge transfer of PMS (Figure S51, Supporting Information). Therefore, both experiment and calculations showed that single-atom $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ coordination exhibited higher intrinsic activity for PMS activation and induced the nonradical ETP regime for subsequent micropollutant degradation.

# 2.5. Discussion

Based on the above analyses, understanding the intricate interplays between the diverse potential active sites of artificial SACs and the triggered nonradical ETP regimes are of paramount importance (Figure 4c). The adsorption of PMS molecules in the vicinity of $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ sites leads to the formation of surfacereactive complexes via chemical bonding, which significantly influences the redox potential of the $\mathrm { C o - N _ { 3 } C - P M S ^ { \ast } }$ complexes. When the potential of the complexes surpasses the oxidation potential of the pollutant, ETP will favorably occur between the complexes and surface-adsorbed pollutants over the conjugated carbon framework to realize pollutant oxidation.[19] Moreover, in single-atom Co catalysts, the graphitic N sites are favorable for both PMS (lower than $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { N } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C } )$ and pollutant adsorption through a "donor—acceptor complex," thus enriching the surface reactant population to accelerate the overall ETP kinetics. [9] The $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ coordination enhances the catalytic ETP process via 1) improving PMS adsorption quantity (stronger adsorption), generating $\mathrm { C o - N _ { 3 } C - P M S ^ { \ast } }$ complexes (elevated oxidative potential), and 3) accelerated electron-transfer rates (improved conductivity and increased potential gap) during HBA oxidation. Crucially, the presence of Co single atoms reduces defect levels and strengthens electronic states near the Fermi level, thereby enhancing conductivity to accelerate ETP. The optimized electronic properties of $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ effectively regulate the adsorption behavior of PMS (the rate-determining step of ETP) at active Co sites, consequently, promoting the thermodynamic feasibility of PMS activation to form active complexes and trigger ETP. The nonmetal-bonded species will create a favorable microenvironment to elevate surface potential via mediocre PMS activation, and more importantly, accumulate organic pollutants on the surface to boost oxidation kinetics.

Therefore, for intelligent design of materials that can effectively accommodate a multireactant system, one should consider a mix of factors regarding both thermodynamics and kinetics. For Fenton-like systems, optimizing the coordination environment of the active sites to fine-tune electronic properties, like the $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { - N } } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C }$ coordination, can spontaneously promote catalyst electron-transfer capacity and regulate the PMS adsorption behavior to attain a reduced energy barrier of PMS activation. Balanced attributes, such as adsorption strength and configuration, PMS coverage and overall potential, and pollutant features, collectively regulate the thermodynamic feasibility of ETP. Additionally, graphitic N sites offer dual-functional sites for synergistic PMS activation to mildly raise surface oxidation potential and more importantly promote adsorptive interactions with the reactants to intensify overall kinetics via enriching surface pollutant concentration. Therefore, nonbonded N plays an indispensable role in Fenton-like reactions, which has been overlooked in previous SAC studies due to the low intrinsic activity. Therefore, for complex catalytic systems that involve multiple reactants, both the intrinsic activity/selectivity of SAC sites as well as auxiliary sites to control local kinetics are of vital significance in determining the reaction mechanism and overall efficiency, which can be realized to incorporate synergistic active sites (such as graphitic N in this work).

# 3. Conclusion

In this work, we developed a series of carbon-based Co SACs with controlled N-loading, achieving $100 \%$ ETP for nonradical and selective oxidation. We for the first time explored the distinctive roles of metal-bonded and independent $_ \mathrm { N }$ species in SACbased Fenton-like reactions. We have established positive linearity based on quantitative structure—activity relationships and obtained the direct correlations of $Q _ { \mathrm { { P M S } } }$ , redox potential, and ultimately HBA oxidation kinetics. The $\mathrm { C o - N }$ coordination was most favorable for PMS adsorption and catalytic activation to trigger ETP, while nonmetal-bonded graphitic N helped adsorb target micropollutant and acted as a secondary site for PMS adsorption, synergistically promoting ETP for the overall degradation efficiency. This study provides valuable insights into the design and optimization of highly efficient SAC catalysts in addition to coordination engineering. By rationally tailoring the N species and populations within the SAC catalysts, one can precisely regulate the adsorptive and catalytic behaviors in complex reactions that involve multiple reactants (taking the PMS chemistry, for example), leading to on-demand oxidation capacity and kinetics. The SAC-empowered oxidation system offers innovative strategies for tackling microorganic pollution with enhanced efficiency and selectivity.
