Graphene has been applied as a catalyst in peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation for the removal of pharmaceuticals in water. Firstly, a kinetic adsorption study of PMS was developed, fitting the results to the Elovich’s equation. Moreover, the influence of the main variables in the adsorptive process such as $\mathsf { p H }$ , initial PMS concentration, and graphene dose were assessed. Secondly, the degradation of diclofenac as a target compound was studied comparing PMS-catalytic versus adsorption processes. PMS-catalytic process enhanced the removal of the micropollutant if compared to adsorption when using a low dose of graphene (less than ${ 5 0 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 } } ;$ or after surface saturation. Studies using radical scavengers suggested the lack of radicals in the process, suggesting the non-radical activation of PMS. Thirdly, the adsorption versus PMS-catalytic processes were also compared for the oxidation of a mixture of three antibiotics (norfloxacin, tetracycline and sulfamethoxazole) with different chemical structure. PMS-catalytic activation was more effective for the removal of those compounds that presented less affinity towards adsorption onto the graphene surface. Finally, characterization of the fresh and PMStreated material was performed. Graphene demonstrated to be stable after its use as catalysts in PMS activation, suffering only slight transformation of the surface oxidation groups.

# 1. Introduction

Since graphene monolayer was first reported in 2004 (Novoselov et al., 2004), diverse applications based on graphene-derived materials have been launched. Graphene is defined as a single sheet of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ hybridized carbon, defining a 2D carbonaceous crystalline allotrope with regular hexagonal pattern. This bonding carbon lattice allows graphene to present excellent characteristics such as high thermal conductivity, high mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, high electronic capacity and excellent optical properties, among others (Stankovich et al., 2006). Graphene’s properties enable it to be a promising material for many applications in the field of water treatment, such as its flexibility and hydrophobicity to build membranes of small pores for applications in filtration (Han et al., 2013; Hu and Mi, 2013) and desalination (Cohen-Tanugi and Grossman, 2012; Werber et al., 2016). Moreover, due to the mentioned properties, graphene is under research as nanoadsorbent (Chowdhury and Balasubramanian, 2014; Xu et al., 2012; Ersan et al., 2017) and nano-catalyst (Upadhyay et al., 2014; Cai et al., 2018; Meng et al., 2019).

Water treatment processes should address the new challenges and needs that current Society demands. For example, most wastewater treatment plants cannot completely remove trace organic chemicals, including Contaminants of Emerging Concern (CECs) (Michael et al., 2013). The current implemented wastewater treatment processes, usually, biological-based on activated sludge, do not degrade or only slightly oxidize these contaminants, in some cases to other recalcitrant intermediates, and thus a significant portion of these trace organic CECs are present in the effluent. Although the concentration of CECs in the wastewater effluents is typically very low, they are frequently referred as micropollutants since are typically reported at $\mu \ g \textrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ or ng $\mathrm { ~ L ~ } ^ { - 1 }$ level, the consequences of their exposure in the environment in the long term are still largely unknown and further research to get more insights is underway (Gavrilescu et al., 2015; Petrie et al., 2015). From all the feasible treatment solutions proposed by the research community, Advanced Oxidation Processes (AOPs) based on either hydroxyl radicals (Oller et al., 2011) or sulfate radicals (Guerra-Rodríguez et al., 2018) are a promising option because of their effectiveness in the oxidation of a wide range of recalcitrant organic substances (Miklos et al., 2018; Alharbi and Price, 2017). However, the main concern of the use of S containing oxidants is the release of sulfate anions after the treatment. This aspect could be considered acceptable depending on the oxidant dose added and the further application of the treated effluent.

Sulfate radical-based AOPs are processes that rely on the production of sulfate radical as the main oxidation species by the breakage of the peroxy bond of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) or peroxydisulfate (PDS). The generation of sulfate radicals may be triggered by thermal (Liang and Su, 2009), radiation (Wacławek et al., 2017), or catalytic activation (Ghanbari and Moradi, 2017a; Matzek and Carter, 2016), among other methods. Much research has been conducted regarding the catalytic activation of these species with metal-based substances, either as dissolved metals or as metals immobilized on supports. However, the main concerns regarding the use of metallic catalysts are related to toxicity, if they are released to the treated effluent, and the high cost for applications with metal catalysts. In this sense, graphene has emerged as a promising material applicable to catalytic AOPs (Liang and Su, 2009). The activation of PMS or PDS through the use of carbonaceous catalysts (i.e. activated carbon, graphite, carbon nanotubes, nanodiamonds or graphene) (Duan et al., 2016a; Zhao et al., 2017; Duan et al., 2016b; Wang et al., 2019; Duan et al., 2016c; Wang et al., 2016) is attracting significant research interest since activated carbon is already established and economically feasible in many water treatment plants. The addition of oxidants enables the removal of adsorbed species as the carbon material is used as a catalyst and not as an adsorbent that needs frequent regeneration. The carbocatalysis of PMS and PDS is still under research, and a non-radical pathway is thought to take place via the formation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ species (Duan et al., 2016a; Wang et al., 2018). It is well known that the self-decomposition of PMS at alkaline conditions $\mathrm { ( p H > \ p K _ { a } ) }$ slowly generates singlet oxygen (Zhou et al., 2015a)

$$
\mathrm { H S O _ { 5 } ^ { - } + S O _ { 5 } ^ { 2 - k = 0 . 2 M ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } } } \mathrm { H S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } + S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } + O _ { 1 2 } }
$$

Similarly, singlet oxygen release has been described during the autodecomposition of PDS (Cheng et al., 2017). Recently, it was reported that the reaction of PMS and benzoquinone involves the generation of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ through the formation of a dioxirane intermediate (Zhou et al., 2015a). Diverse studies have shown uncertainties and different conclusions about the formation of radical, non-radical pathway, or combination of both. However, it has been suggested that the non-radical pathway is the main contribution in carbocatalysis. The low proportion of oxygenated groups and carbon edges $( \mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 } .$ -conjugation with unpaired electrons) are usually the active sites to interact with the peroxide bond of PMS (Duan et al., 2018a).

Few works have focused on the interaction of peroxymonosulfate and graphene (Duan et al., 2018b), and those available do not report the kinetic study of PMS evolution during these processes. The chemisorption of PMS onto reduced graphene oxide has been recently studied from a thermodynamic point of view (Duan et al., 2016b). The research available related to PMS and graphene is focused on the evaluation of degradation of target compounds such as phenol (Duan et al., 2016a; Sun et al., 2012; Peng et al., 2013; Indrawirawan et al., 2015), dyes (Peng et al., 2013) or Bisphenol A (Olmez-Hanci et al., 2018).

This work presents the study of PMS interaction onto commercial graphene, focusing the interest on the kinetics of PMS adsorption, and how the main variables (pH, PMS dose and graphene dose) affect the process. The characterization of the material before and after the treatment was also performed. Diclofenac was chosen as the target CEC, and the efficiency of adsorption versus catalysis on the pollutant’s removal processes was studied. The role of the main process parameters (pH, graphene dose, diclofenac and PMS initial concentrations, reusability of the solid, etc.) was investigated with respect to the kinetics of the process. Scavenging probe studies revealed the importance of the non-radical pathway. PMS was found to be the most efficient oxidant compared to other oxidants such as hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ , peroxydisulfate, and peracetic acid. Finally, the process was evaluated for the degradation of different types of antibiotics.

The $\mathsf { p H }$ value at which the experimental curve undergoes $\mathsf { p H } _ { \mathrm { i n i t i a l } } = \mathsf { p H } _ { \mathrm { f i n a l } }$ was estimated as the $\mathrm { p H } _ { \mathrm { p z c } }$ .

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Kinetics modeling of PMS adsorption onto graphene. Influence of variables

Adsorption of peroxymonosulfate onto carbonaceous materials is thought to take place via chemisorption (Duan et al., 2016b). The presence of electrostatic interactions between PMS and π-π bonds of graphene plays an important role (Chen et al., 2018a). To study the kinetic of PMS adsorption onto graphene, a Design of Experiment (DOE) using Surface Response Methodology (RSM) was carried out selecting initial PMS concentration, graphene dose, and $\mathsf { p H }$ as the essential variables to study. Design Expert® software was used to build the Central Composite Design (CCD) with a bunch of 20 experiments (6 repetitions of the central point). Fig. 1A depicts the conditions of the set of tests launched, the range for each variable being the following: $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P M S } , 0 }$ from $5 { \cdot } 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ to $1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathbf { M }$ ; Cgraphene from 0.3 to $1 . 0 { \ g } { \ L } ^ { - 1 }$ ; and pH from 5 to 9. The PMS concentration decay in the presence of graphene was monitored and results showed a fast decrease which slowed down after the first minutes. Fig. 1B shows, as an example, the kinetic of PMS concentration evolution for the central point of the selected CCD.

At first sight of the profiles observed for the PMS evolution, since a stable plateau seems to be reached, no predominance of catalytic decomposition should be assumed. The main mechanism for PMS disappearance takes place by adsorption. Therefore, the experimental data were fitted to adsorption kinetic models. From all the empirical models available in the literature to describe the process of adsorption, the Elovich’s model was selected, as it better fitted than pseudo-first or pseudo-second order models. This model was first applied to fitting gas chemisorption processes (Aharoni and Tompkins, 1970). Nonetheless, it has also been widely used to describe the adsorption processes of aqueous organic pollutants (Thakur and Kandasubramanian, 2019). According to Elovich’s equation, the kinetics of PMS adsorption can be described as follows:

$$
\frac { \mathrm { d } { \sf q } _ { \mathrm { P M S } } } { \mathrm { d t } } = \mathrm { a e } ^ { - \alpha q }
$$

where $\mathbf { q } _ { \mathbf { P M S } }$ is the PMS adsorption capacity; a represents the initial adsorption rate, and $\alpha$ is the adsorption rate. To simplify Elovich equation, Chien-Clayton assumed that $( \mathsf { a d } ) > \mathsf { \Omega } > 1$ (Chien and Clayton, 1980). Taking the boundary conditions as $\mathbf { q } _ { \mathrm { t } } = 0$ for $\mathbf { t } = 0$ and ${ \sf q } _ { \mathrm { t } } = { \sf q } _ { \mathrm { t } }$ at ${ \bf t } = { \bf t } ,$ eq. (2) can be transformed into the following:

$$
{ \tt q } _ { \mathrm { P M S } } = \alpha \ln \left( { \tt a } \frac { 1 } { \alpha } \right) + \alpha \ln ( { \tt t } )
$$

As the PMS adsorption capacity is defined as:

$$
\mathsf { q } _ { \mathrm { P M S } } = \frac { \mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P M S } _ { 0 } } - \mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P M S } } } { \mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { g r a p h e n e } } }
$$

eq. (3) can be expressed as:

$$
\mathrm { C _ { P M S } } = \mathrm { C _ { P M S _ { 0 } } } - \mathrm { k } \left[ \mathrm { l n } \left( \mathrm { a } \frac { 1 } { \alpha } \right) + \mathrm { l n } ( \mathrm { t } ) \right]
$$

where $\mathbf { k } { = } \mathbf { { \vec { \alpha } } } \mathbf { \tilde { \mathbf { { C } } } } _ { \mathrm { g r a p h e n e } }$ . A representation of $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P M S } }$ versus $\ln ( \mathrm { t } )$ gives a straight line, from the slope of which it is possible to obtain k. Eq. (5) was used to model the experimental data. A good correlation was found, with $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } > 0 . 9 7$ in most cases. Fig. 1B depicts the evolution of normalized PMS concentration and the goodness of the proposed fitting for the results of the central point.

The constant k was selected as the response for the Surface Response Methodology in the CCD, assessing the influence of the three variables chosen in the process. The results attained for each experimental series is accomplished in Table S2. From the analysis of Expert-Design® software, the best output with a reasonable agreement between experimental $\mathbf { k }$ and model was a linear plus interaction regression:

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { k ^ { - 1 } = ( \alpha C _ { g r a p h e n e } ) ^ { - 1 } = b _ { 0 } + b _ { 1 } C _ { P M S } + b _ { 2 } C _ { g r a p h e n e } + b _ { 3 } p H } \\ & { ~ + ~ b _ { 4 } C _ { P M S } C _ { g r a p h e n e } + } \\ & { + ~ b _ { 5 } C _ { P M S } p H + b _ { 6 } C _ { g r a p h e n e } p H + b _ { 7 } C _ { P M S } ^ { 2 } + b _ { 8 } C _ { g r a p h e n e } ^ { 2 } + b _ { 9 } p H ^ { 2 } } \end{array}
$$

Table 1 summarizes the statistical characteristics of the adjustment, with a reasonable regression coefficient (adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 4 1 6 \mathrm { ) }$ . Coded values for $\mathbf { b _ { i } }$ constant can serve to know the relative importance of each variable and their interactions. Hence, a decreasing impact following the order PMS initial concentration $>$ graphene dose $> \mathrm { { \ p H } }$ was observed. From the ANOVA analysis, the $\mathbf { p }$ -values obtained reveal higher significance $( \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ of the first-order terms if compared to the second-order interactions. The adsorption capacity of graphene-based materials is highly related to the structure of the molecule, and therefore, the interactions that may be taking place onto the surface of the material. $$

$$

(10) was solved by non-linear regression fitting, providing a satisfactory description $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 8$ ; see Fig. 3B) with the following values for the constants: ${ \tt q } _ { \tt D C F , m a x } = 6 1 0 . 8 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { D C F } $ $( g$ graphene)−1; and ${ \bf k } _ { \mathrm { L } } = 0 . 2 7 9 \mathrm { L }$ $( \mathrm { m g \ D C F } ) ^ { - 1 }$ . The maximum capacity value obtained is in good agreement with the reported previously for graphene oxide $( 5 0 0 \mathrm { m g }$ DCF per g of graphene) (Nam et al., 2015b).

The direct reaction between organic substances and PMS has been reported to be thermodynamically feasible for pollutants of emerging concern (Ji et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2018b; Yang et al., 2018a; Solís et al., 2016). As shown in Fig. 4A, graphene dose was found to be one of the most important variables since the catalytic effect on DCF removal was quite influenced by the great adsorption capacity of graphene. A stable plateau of $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { D C F } }$ was observed during the adsorption process when graphene dose was within 10 and $5 0 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ , due to the saturation of the surface. The addition of PMS under these conditions improved the initial DCF degradation with a slower linear stage. Notably, $5 0 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ of graphene was capable of completely removing DCF from the aqueous media in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . The higher dose of graphene led to complete adsorption of DCF, and the addition of PMS slightly accelerated the process.

pH exerts an important role since $\mathrm { p K _ { a } }$ of DCF and PMS, in conjunction with the pH of the point of zero charge $\mathrm { ( p H _ { p z c } ) }$ of graphene, determine the electrical interactions between the adsorbate and solid surface. $\mathrm { \ p H _ { \mathrm { p z c } } }$ for fresh graphene was found to be 5.37 (see Fig. S2). Aqueous $\mathrm { \ p H < \ p H _ { \mathrm { p z c } } }$ means surface positively charged; therefore, high attraction potential for anions. DCF’s $\mathrm { p K _ { a } }$ is 4.15 while the second dissociation constant for PMS into ${ \mathsf { S } } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 5 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ has a $\mathrm { p K _ { a } } = 9 . 4$ (Ghanbari and Moradi, 2017b). Adsorption of both species is expected to play the main role at pH lower than $\mathrm { \ p H _ { \mathrm { p z c } } }$ . At pH below $\mathrm { p H } _ { \mathrm { p z c } } ,$ , higher adsorption affinity of DCF (neutral) is expected if compared to $\mathrm { \ p H _ { \mathrm { \ell } } > \ p H _ { \mathrm { p z c } } }$ . At basic $\mathrm { p H } _ { \mathrm { \ell } }$ , it is expected that the electrostatic repulsion between graphene surface and deprotonated DCF impedes the adsorption process. As can be observed from Fig. 4B, there is a negative influence of increasing pH from 3 to 9, during PMS catalytic process. A less positively, or negatively, charged graphene surface would disfavor adsorption of DCF. The enhancement of catalysis with respect to adsorption process is higher at acidic conditions. The raise of $\mathsf { p H }$ during adsorption of PMS, contrary to what expected according to the same reasoning carried out for DCF, exerted a positive effect. That indicates that another mechanism different from electrostatic repulsion might play an important role for PMS.

The influence of the initial concentration of PMS is frequently evaluated in catalytic systems in order to assess the optimum dose. Some experiment at different initial PMS concentration $( 0 . 1 \mathrm { - } 1 . 0 \mathrm { m M } )$ where conducted. Although the pH was lower as the initial PMS concentration was increased (unbuffered tests), the effect of pH is expected to be minimal as the initial pH differs in only one unit. PMS dose exerts a positive influence until reaching an optimum; after that, it starts to become negative. The higher the amount of oxidant, the better the performance registered in the targeted pollutant. However, as it is also observed in this system, after a certain dosage of PMS, a slightly negative effect is observed. In this particular case, $\mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { P M S } , 0 } > 0 . 7 5 \mathrm { m M }$ caused a partial inhibition (see Fig. 4C). It is well known that when PMS is in excess, it is auto consumed by radicals, triggering a scavenging effect in which the target pollutant and PMS compete for the generated oxidizing species (Shukla et al., 2010):

$$
\mathrm { H S O _ { 5 } ^ { - } + H O ^ { * } / S O _ { 4 } ^ { - - }  S O _ { 5 } ^ { * - } + S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } + H ^ { + } / H O ^ { - } }
$$

Nevertheless, carbocatalysis with inorganic peroxides like PMS is thought to take place by a non-radical pathway through singlet oxygen generation $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ . Similarly, PMS in excess seems to compete with the organic (DCF) for non-radical oxidant species. An overdose of oxidant affects negatively after reaching an optimum value.

An increase in the initial DCF is expected to impact positively the elimination rate of the organic, as proposed in Eq. (9). Since the effect of initial DCF concentration is hard to be analyzed from the temporal evolution of normalized concentration, the initial rate was estimated as a better tool to study this variable. Thus, as observed in Fig. 4 D1, an increase of initial DCF concentration enhanced the degradation rate of the micropollutant.

# 3.2.3. Reusability of graphene in adsorption vs. reaction with PMS

The loss of activity during the catalytic process was evaluated by reusing the catalyst after $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ of reaction. For that purpose, the solid was recovered by filtration and dried overnight. To balance the graphene dose, the volume of the reaction was readjusted according to the catalyst loss. Fig. 5 depicts the results of the evolution of the DCF normalized concentration versus time during four consecutive runs in the presence of $1 0 0 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ of graphene. Adsorption results during recycling are also accomplished to compare both processes.

As inferred in Fig. 5 the catalytic deactivation of graphene in the presence of PMS was minimal, and only after $3 ^ { \mathrm { r d } }$ and $4 ^ { \mathrm { t h } } ~ \mathrm { r u n } \sim 5 \%$ of residual DCF is observed. That effect may be related to the adsorption of intermediates or final oxidation products with a better affinity towards graphene surface. Adsorption process became inefficient after the $3 ^ { \mathrm { r d } }$ run as the active centers started to be blocked by adsorbed DCF molecules. No desorption of DCF was observed in the absence of PMS under the experimental conditions tested. The use of PMS-catalysis with graphene proved to be more efficient than the adsorption process.

# 3.2.4. Study on oxidative pathways with quenchers and PMS effectiveness compared to other oxidants

The oxidative pathway of the process that combines PMS and graphene was studied by adding scavengers that presumably suppress certain radicals or non-radical oxidative pathways. Although this technique might be controversial due to the lack of selectivity of the available quenchers towards a specific radical; it is still useful since it allows to discern the preferential pathway. In PMS aqueous chemistry, the following main Reactive Oxidative Species (ROS) are expected to participate: HO% , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ , $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \cdot \mathrm { ^ - }$ and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . Accordingly, different inhibitors of these oxidative pathways were tested. Results are depicted in Fig. 6.

Linear alcohols (i.e. methanol, ethanol or iso-propanol) react moderately fast with both hydroxyl and sulfate radicals (Chen et al., 2018a). For example, the reported values for the second-order rate constant of ethanol with hydroxyl and sulfate radicals are, respectively, in the order of ${ 1 0 } ^ { 9 }$ and $1 0 ^ { 7 } \mathrm { ~ M ~ } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ (Wacławek et al., 2017). However, not all alcohols present similar reactivity towards superoxide anion. Methanol reacts fast with superoxide (order of $1 0 ^ { 7 }  { \mathrm { M } } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ whereas ethanol and $_ i .$ isopropanol are more selective $( 1 0 ^ { 2 } \textbf { M } ^ { - 1 } \textbf { \em s } ^ { - 1 }$ and $< 0 . 1 \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively) (Hayyan et al., 2016). To distinguish the predominance of hydroxyl or sulfate radicals, tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) is usually used, as it reacts slower with $\mathrm { { } ~ } ^ { S O _ { 4 } ^ { ~ \cdot ~ - } }$ (in the order of $1 0 ^ { 5 }  { \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } } s ^ { - 1 } )$ than HO% (in the magnitude of $1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ (Chen et al., 2018a). The testing assays in the presence of ethanol or tert-butyl alcohol did not inhibit the DCF decay during the process, which may be indicative of no predominance of $\mathrm { H O } ^ { \cdot } / { S 0 _ { 4 } } ^ { \cdot - }$ species in the process.

p-benzoquinone (pBZQ) is frequently used as superoxide radical quencher in PMS oxidation technologies (Chen et al., 2018a). Even so, pBZQ is also very reactive with HO% (rate constant, in the range of $1 0 ^ { 9 }$ $\mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace s ^ { - 1 }$ (Nien Schuchmann et al., 1998) and $\ S O _ { 4 } ^ { ~ \cdot - }$ , in the magnitude of $1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { ~ M ~ } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { ~ s ~ } ^ { - 1 }$ (Neta et al., 1988a). The direct reaction of pBZQ and PMS, under alkaline $\mathrm { p H } .$ , has been recently reported as an effective technology to produce $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ due to the catalytic effect of $\mathrm { H O ^ { - } }$ anions (Zhou et al., 2015a). Since the pH of the reaction during the experiment was below 5, this latter reaction can be disregarded. From the results with EtOH and TBA, it can be accepted that if pBZQ affects the kinetics is due to the influence of superoxide radical. Fig. 6 reveals a slight influence of this species since the addition of pBZQ did not affect considerably the removal rate of DCF.

$\mathrm { N a } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N }$ has been widely applied for the inhibition of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ contribution, rate constant, $2 { \cdot } 1 0 ^ { 9 }$ (Ghanbari and Moradi, 2017b); however, it also reacts with radical species, respective rate constant with HO% and $\mathrm { { \le { O } _ { 4 } } ^ { \cdot - } }$ in the order of $1 0 ^ { 1 0 }$ (Kraljić and Trumbore, 1965) and $1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ (Neta et al., 1988b). Moreover, the results adding $\mathrm { N a } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N }$ must be cautiously analyzed due to the possibility of the reaction of the quencher with PMS molecules, as recently reported by Yang et al (Yang et al., 2018a). Furthermore, the kinetic reaction of $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \dot { ~ }$ remains still unclear (Duan et al., 2018a). Since the addition of EtOH or TBA did not inhibit the process, the scavenging effect produced by $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ in this system could only be attributed to the reaction with PMS and/or $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . As can be inferred from Fig. 6, no conclusion about a unique contribution of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ can be obtained. However, better results can be extracted when adding furfuryl alcohol (FFA). FFA is also commonly used for the study of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ role, rate constant for the reaction $1 . 2 { \cdot } 1 0 ^ { 8 } \ \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ (Chen et al., 2018a; Haag et al., 1984). FFA also scavenges HO,% $1 . 5 { \cdot } 1 0 ^ { 1 0 } \ \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 }$ (Chen et al., 2018a), whose presence have been previously proved to be negligible. The same reasoning can be extrapolated to $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ , although no available information about the kinetics with FFA is available. The addition of FFA in the PMS $^ +$ graphene system led to complete inhibition of the reaction contribution. The DCF degradation obtained was quite similar to that registered for the adsorption under the same experimental conditions.

Summarizing, from the chemical scavenging tests conducted, the main contribution of a non-radical pathway can be envisaged. The presence of $\pi$ electrons on the surface of graphene enhances the adsorption of the oxidant and organic adsorbates, promoting the oxidation without the presence of free radicals in the media.

The efficiency of PMS was compared to other common oxidants used for the oxidation of organics, commonly activated by the radical pathway. A series of assays with peroxydisulfate (PDS), hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ , and peracetic acid (PAA) were carried out (see Fig. 7) for the degradation of DCF by non-radical oxidation on the surface of graphene. # 3.3. Peroxymonosulfate-graphene system applied to a mixture of antibiotics

The study applied to diclofenac as target aqueous pollutant was extended to a mixture of three antibiotics, i.e. norfloxacin, tetracycline, and sulfamethoxazole, frequently reported as contaminants of emerging concern (Michael et al., 2013; Focazio et al., 2008; Rodriguez-Narvaez et al., 2017). Thus, Fig. 8 summarizes the results attained for the degradation during the direct oxidation with PMS, the adsorption onto graphene, and the combination of PMS and graphene.

The direct reaction of PMS after $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ resulted in $2 0 \%$ , $3 0 \%$ , and $8 5 \%$ degradation of norfloxacin, sulfamethoxazole, and tetracycline at initial contaminant: PMS molar ratio of 1:32, 1:25, and 1:44, respectively. Only tetracycline was significantly oxidized by direct reaction with PMS. This behavior has also been reported previously under high tetracycline: PMS ratio (Yang et al., 2018b). Sulfamethoxazole and norfloxacin are also expected to undergo direct oxidation with PMS according to the literature (Yang et al., 2018a; Chen et al., 2018c).

The extent of adsorption process depends on the hydrophobic interactions of organic adsorbates and $\pi$ electrons of graphene as the main route of the adsorption process (Ersan et al., 2017). The octanolwater partition coefficient $( \mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { O W } } )$ values can be used as a tool to predict and compare the adsorption affinity of the different target organics. According to the decreasing values of $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { O W } } ,$ the adsorption affinity should be sulfamethoxazole $>$ norfloxacin $>$ tetracycline (Ursu et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the observed order was norfloxacin $>$ tetracycline $>$ sulfamethoxazole. This suggests that not only the hydrophobicity is a determining factor, but also the molecular complexity, the number of aromatic rings, and their spatial distribution play a role, among others. All the amount of norfloxacin was completely adsorbed in ca. $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ whereas almost $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ were necessary for complete adsorption of tetracycline. Only $8 0 \%$ of sulfamethoxazole was absorbed in $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ , reaching the equilibrium conditions after $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ .

Considering the different reactivity towards direct oxidation with PMS and the affinity to graphene adsorption for the three antibiotics, the combination of PMS and graphene led to different results. In the case of norfloxacin, due to the high adsorption affinity, no significant improvement in the compound's kinetic removal was achieved when applying PMS and graphene. Tetracycline removal was significantly enhanced when adding PMS, leading to a faster and complete degradation in $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . For the case of sulfamethoxazole, the degradation kinetics was also improved.

# 3.4. Graphene characterization before and after oxidative treatment with PMS

The surface properties of the commercial graphene nanoplatelets were studied by means of diverse techniques before and after being in contact with PMS in similar conditions to those developed during the oxidative treatment of pharmaceuticals, i.e. ratio CPMS: $\mathrm { C _ { g r a p h e n e } = 0 . 1 m M }$ : $1 0 0 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ .

The morphology of the nanoparticles was studied by SEM and TEM microscopy. No differences between the fresh commercial graphene and the PMS treated were observed. From SEM micrographs (Fig. 9), it can be observed that the nanoplatelets are aggregated forming a bigger conglomerate, in some cases over $1 \mu \mathrm { m }$ size. In TEM images (Fig. 9), it is possible to observe the presence of transparent sheets of different shapes and sizes.

The specific surface area was determined by $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption-desorption isotherm for the fresh commercial graphene and the PMS treated sample. No significant differences before and after the use of PMS were obtained. As shown in Fig. S3, both isotherms describe a reversible IItype isotherm according to IUPAC classification (Thommes et al., 2015). This behavior of unrestricted multilayer physisorption is characteristic of non-porous materials. Moreover, from the results obtained with the BJH method for pore distribution, a sharp decay in the mesoporous range is observed. Similar results were obtained for the PMStreated sample (results not shown). The BET specific surface areas obtained were $4 9 4 \pm \ : 3$ and $4 8 5 \pm 3 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ for fresh and PMS-treated graphene, respectively. The initial value is in good agreement with that provided by the manufacturer $( 5 0 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ . It can be concluded that the material is practically stable and does not lose its $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption properties after the addition of PMS for catalytic activation (less than $1 . 8 \%$ of specific surface area loss).

Graphene XRD diffractogram is characterized by the presence of broader peaks, low angle displacement, and reduced peaks if compared to the graphite XRD spectrum, which means a larger distance between planes (Monteserín et al., 2017). In the commercial graphene, either before and after using PMS treatment, the main peak appears at ca. $2 6 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ , which is characteristic of the graphitic hexagonal structure (Saiful Badri et al., 2017). No significant difference in the XRD spectrum after PMS-treatment was registered as shown in Fig. 10. Graphene oxide usually presents a peak around ${ { 1 0 } ^ { \circ } }$ , not observed in this material, attributed to the (001) plane, corresponding to a higher distance between planes if compared to reduced graphene in which this peak disappears leading to the (002) plane (Jabbar et al., 2017). XRD spectrum suggests that the material is stable after being used in PMS catalysis with lack of oxygenated surface groups.

No oxygenated functional groups were observed in FTIR spectra before or after the addition of PMS (see Fig. S4). The lack of significant peaks in FTIR is characteristic of graphitic carbon with a very low proportion of oxygenated groups (Saiful Badri et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the presence of oxygen in the surface of graphene was further studied by XPS analysis. The high-resolution spectra of O1s region, available in Fig. 11, for the solid before and after the treatment with PMS, presented the contribution of four main oxygenated groups, e.g., carbonyl, hydroxyl, carboxylic and ester (Oh et al., 2014). The amount of chemisorbed $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ was negligible, and the deconvoluted contribution for this chemical state has not been considered in Fig. 11. Table 4 summarizes the contribution percentages of each oxygenated species. As can be observed, after adding PMS, surface hydroxylated and carbonyl groups decreased. Nevertheless, the contribution of C–O and carboxylic groups increased after adding PMS. Recently, a thermodynamics study of the PMS-adsorption into reduced graphene oxide has reported that PMS molecules present tendency to be adsorbed preferably onto $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = O }$ as a Lewis basic site (Duan et al., 2016b). It is thought that the nonbonding electron pair of oxygen in carbonyl group is more attractive to PMS molecule if compared to other oxygenated groups. Moreover, from the atomic total O in each sample, which is very similar, it is deduced that the oxygenation process proceeds through oxidation of hydroxyl to increase the percentage content of carboxylic and ketonic groups.

# 4. Conclusions

The kinetics of the adsorption of PMS onto commercial graphene can be successfully fitted to the Elovich equation. An experimental design showed that initial PMS concentration was the variable that affected the most the rate constant for the process, followed by graphene dose and then slightly by pH.

Graphene displayed a high adsorption capacity of the target organic contaminant in water. During diclofenac removal, kinetically speaking, the presence of PMS influenced positively at low doses of graphene, while the effect was less significant at higher doses. This behavior was also observed during the treatment of a mixture of antibiotics (norfloxacin, tetracycline and sulfamethoxazole). Not only the concentration of graphene is crucial in the process, but also the initial $\mathsf { p H }$ of the solution that rules the electrical interactions between adsorbates, i.e. diclofenac and oxidant, and graphene. The mechanism of the process was tentatively assessed by quenchers study which revealed the lack of radicals present in the process. The effectiveness of PMS in the catalytic activation of graphene was also compared to peroxydisulfate (PDS), hydrogen peroxide, and peracetic acid (PAA). The reactivity order for diclofenac removal was $\mathrm { P M S } \sim \mathrm { P D S } > \mathrm { P A A } > \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ .

Solid characterization of the material revealed high stability towards PMS presence. The adsorption capacity was not compromised as the measured specific surface area after treatment was not altered $( 1 . 8 \%$ of surface area loss after PMS addition). The morphology and crystallinity did not suffer significant changes. XPS technique showed an increase in the proportion of carboxylic groups coming from the oxidation of surface carbonyl and hydroxylated groups.

Further studies on the interaction of the PMS catalytic system with the transformation products and the final oxidation products are encouraged in future work. Also, limitations of this technology such as separation of the solid, its further regeneration, and suitable PMS dosage that does not compromise the release of sulfate need further consideration for real applications.
