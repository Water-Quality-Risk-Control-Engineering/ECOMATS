# A BST RAC T

With a wide range of raw materials, low cost and large specific surface area, biochar has been widely used in environmental remediation. However, the biochar has a saturated adsorption capacity when it is used as a pollutant adsorbent. Recent efforts have been made to prepare biochar and biochar-based catalysts with enhanced catalytic properties to expand their potential applications. The environmental persistent free radicals (EPFRs) of biochar could react with $0 _ { 2 }$ to induce hydroxyl radicals (OH) without the addition of oxidants. When oxidants were added, biochar and biochar-based catalysts could activate them to generate OH and sulfate radicals $( S 0 _ { 4 } \bullet ^ { - } )$ , respectively. Moreover, biochar could act as an electron acceptor to improve the photodegradation capacity of catalysts. With reference to the information regarding biochar and biochar-based catalysts, this work provides a critical review on recent research development as follows: 1) the preparations of various types of biochar and biochar-based catalysts are summarized; 2) the effects of the synthetic conditions and transition metals on the catalytic activity of biochar-based catalysts are discussed; (3) methods for characterizing the active sites of the biochar-based catalysts are described; and (4) the environmental applications of biochar and biocharbased catalysts are discussed with regards to three aspects based on the interaction mechanisms, namely, oxidation, reduction, and photocatalysis. The synthesis conditions and loading of metal/metal-free catalyst are key parameters controlling the catalysis activity of biochar and biochar-based catalysts.

This review provides new insights into the application of biochar in catalysis. Key challenges and further research directions are proposed as well.

# 1. Introduction

Biochar possesses excellent physical and chemical properties for applications in various fields to improve the eco-environmental quality (Ruan et al., 2019). Due to its small bulk density, high stability and strong adsorption capacity (Lyu et al., 2016a), biochar is widely used in the fields of agricultural (Zhang et al., 2018), environmental (Lyu et al., 2017b), and biorefinery activities, and it plays an important role in improving soil fertility, increasing soil carbon storage, and reducing atmospheric greenhouse gas emissions (Li et al., 2014; Xiong et al., 2017). The preparation and application of biochar is one of the effective methods of waste recycling, which overcomes the defect of high cost of conventional activated carbon (Lyu et al., 2016b; Xiong et al., 2018). Recently, efforts have been made to modify biochar to improve its properties for environmental application, particularly as an adsorbent for contaminant removal, including physical modification, chemical oxidation, coating, steam and ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ activation (Creamer and Gao, 2014; Tang et al., 2015; Lyu et al., 2018c; Zhang et al., 2019). However, adsorption is a nondestructive process transferring pollutants from one phase to another, making it difficult to completely decontaminate contaminants.

As an effective technology, catalytic degradation technology has become one of the important means to treat environmental pollutants (Cao et al., 2018). Transition metals such as $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ can induce the formation of reactive oxygen species for the degradation of organic and inorganic contaminants (Iskander et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019). Moreover, the photodegradation of contaminants could be achieved by catalysts under UV or visible light irradiation. Commonly used catalysts include titanium dioxide $\left( \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } \right)$ , zinc oxide $( Z \boldsymbol { \mathrm { n O } } )$ , tin oxide $( \mathsf { S n O } _ { 2 } )$ , zirconium dioxide $\left( \mathsf { Z r O } _ { 2 } \right)$ , and cadmium sulfide (CdS) (Liu et al., 2015). However, these catalysts are limited by some defects such as light dissolution and the release of harmful metal ions.

Biochar can be used as a catalyst for the degradation of contaminants due to its functional groups and external transition metals (Sharma et al., 2019). The environmental persistent free radicals (EPFRs) on the surface of biochar could react with ${ { 0 } _ { 2 } }$ to generate hydroxyl radicals (OH) (Khachatryan et al., 2011; Fang et al., 2015b). When added with hydrogen peroxide or persulfate, biochar can activate it to generate hydroxyl radicals ( OH) or sulfate radicals $( \mathsf { S O } _ { 4 } \bullet ^ { - } )$ , respectively, which were reported to efficiently degrade organic contaminants such as orange II (Wei et al., 2017), 2-chlorobiphenyl (Fang et al., 2014), sulfamethoxazole (Wang et al., 2019), and bisphenol A (Wang et al., 2017). Moreover, biochar can act as a supporting material for a photocatalyst to improve the photodegradation ability of the catalyst (Yang et al., 2018a, 2018b). The adsorption properties of biochar can purify contaminants in the dark and under-light conditions. The in situ regeneration of biochar can be achieved under illumination, which greatly reduces the cost of the regeneration process (Pi et al., 2015).

Various types of biochar-based catalysts have been developed and used in water and soil remediation. However, most of the current perspectives mainly focus on the overview of bare biochar catalysts and the role of EPFRs. However, a systematic review on the effects of modifiers on the active catalytic active sites of biocharbased catalysts and the application mechanism are still lacking.

The overall goal of this work is to promote the research on biochar and biochar-based catalysts by providing a comprehensive review of recent research development of the production, characterization, and environmental applications of biochar and biochar-based catalysts. The specific objectives are as follows: (1) summarize the fundamental approaches and principles to prepare biochar and biochar-based catalysts; (2) discuss the effects of the synthetic conditions (i.e., pyrolysis temperature and time) and transition metals on the catalytic activities of biochar and biochar-based catalysts; (3) organize methods for characterizing the properties of the biochar-based catalysts; (4) summarize the main applications of biochar and biochar-based catalysts in environmental remediation; and (5) suggest the current knowledge gaps and future opportunities for advanced investigations and new applications of biochar and biochar-based catalysts.

# 2. Recent advances in biochar

# 2.1. Preparation of biochar

Typically, biochar preparation methods for catalytic degradation and for adsorption are not significantly different (Lyu et al., 2018b). Table 1 summarizes the synthesis of various types of biochar catalysts. The most common conventional approaches are oxygenlimited pyrolysis and the hydrothermal carbonization process (i.e., hydrochar) (Regmi et al., 2012; Kambo and Dutta, 2015; Gao et al., 2018). Studies have shown that the type of feedstock and the pyrolysis temperature have an impact on the adsorption properties of biochar (Lyu et al., 2016c; Zhou et al., 2016). In the case of catalytic degradation, the pyrolysis temperature, pyrolysis time and transition metals are important factors affecting the catalytic activity of biochar (Fig. 1a). Yang et al. (2016) prepared biochars from pine wood, corn stalks, peanut shells, rice, and wheat at different temperatures (200, 350, 500, and $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } $ , and tested their ability to degrade $p$ -nitrophenol. The free radical plays an important role in the degradation of $p$ -nitrophenol. They found that increasing the pyrolysis temperature from 200 to $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ significantly increased the free radical signal. However, upon further increasing the pyrolysis temperature to $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ , the free radical signal decreased dramatically. These results suggest that the higher pyrolysis temperature favored the formation of free radicals, thereby increasing the catalytic degradation capacity of biochar. However, an excessively high temperature (e.g., $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ may cause the decomposition and reorganization of organic structures, resulting in the disappearance of free radicals and a decrease in the catalytic ability (Yang et al., 2016). Huang et al. (2019b) developed an approach to reuse the coagulation sludge to prepare biochar catalyst by oxygen-limited pyrolysis. The sludge was pyrolyzed at 400, 500, 600 and $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ under an argon atmosphere, respectively. The $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ biochar decolorized the rhodamine B completely among the four biochars tested, suggesting that the electron shuttling capacity of biochar activated potassium peroxymonosulfate to generate  OH,  OOH and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ .

In general, the effect of the pyrolysis temperature on the catalytic performance is not absolute. The catalytic capacity of biochar decreased as the pyrolysis time increased due to the gradual decomposition of the phenolic compounds associated with the catalytic capacity. A previous study revealed that $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { h } }$ was the optimal pyrolysis time for the generation of free radicals at relatively low temperatures (e.g., ${ < } 5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ ), while it was $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ for higher pyrolysis temperatures (e.g., ${ > } 5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } $ ) (Fang et al., 2015a). When applied to degrade contaminants, whether alone or in combination with persulfate/hydrogen peroxide, the biochar preparation conditions followed the above conditions. Wang et al. (2019) prepared a biochar at $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } C$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ to activate persulfate for the degradation of sulfamethoxazole. They found that the rate constant of the biochar/Fe(III)/persulfate system was 31.3 times that of the biochar/ persulfate system and 8.2 times that of Fe(III)/persulfate system. Biochar transferred electrons to $\mathsf { F e } ( \mathrm { I I I } )$ to produce Fe(II), which reacted with $S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - }$ to generate $S O _ { 4 } \bullet ^ { - }$ . Yan et al. (2014) found that biochar prepared at $3 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 6 h could effectively degrade trichloroethylene by activating persulfate.

The mass ratio of biomass to water affected the catalytic ability of hydrochar as well (Chen et al., 2017). Gao et al. (2018) reported that the electron paramagnetic resonance signals increased as the biomass to water mass ratio increased from 1:20 to 1:2.5, suggesting that a higher biomass to water mass ratio facilitated the formation of EPFRs. The interaction between water and biomass components was reduced with the use of a relatively small amount of water, and thus, the biomass was not completely pyrolyzed. Therefore, longer residence times were required to achieve the complete pyrolysis of biomass. A small proportion of fragments were dissolved from the biomass during the long hydrothermal carbonization process, which inhibited the saturation of EPFRforming components and the recombination of EPFRs.

The presence of transition metal is an important factor affecting the generation of persistent free radicals (PFRs) in biochar. During the decomposition of biomass, the phenol or quinone moieties transferred electrons to metal oxides (e.g., $\mathsf { F e } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 }$ and CuO) and then promoted the generation of PFRs (Fig. 1b) (See et al., 2007; Jia et al., 2018). Therefore, the higher redox potential of metals can facilitate the formation of PFRs. Fang et al. (2014) reported that Fein the biochar could react with $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to produce OH, and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ could react with OH to generate superoxide ions $( 0 _ { 2 } ^ { - } )$ , which contributed to the degradation of contaminants. Functional groups on the surface of biochar played an important role in the catalytic activity of biochar. Jia et al. (2018) investigated the role of metals (i.e., Fe(III), $\mathtt { C u ( I I ) }$ , Ni(II), ${ \mathsf { C o } } ( \mathrm { I I } ) .$ , and $Z \mathrm { n } ( \mathrm { I I } ) )$ in the formation of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon-induced EPFRs. The fastest EPFR formation rate was achieved in the presence of Fe(III), which was ascribed to the highest redox potential of Fe(III).

Ball milling increased the external and internal surface areas of biochar, exposed its graphitic structure, and increased its amount of functional groups, thus enhancing the catalytic activity of biochar (Lyu et al., 2017a, 2018a). Lyu et al. (2019) prepared engineered biochar through ball milling and found that ball-milled biochars had better electrocatalytic activity for the reduction of $\mathsf { F e } ( \mathsf { C N } ) _ { 6 } ^ { 3 - }$ through cyclic voltammetry and electrochemical impedance spectra. Ball milling increased the contents of oxygen-containing functional groups from 0.8 to 2.9 to $2 . 2 { - } 4 . 1 \ \mathrm { m m o l / g }$ (2-fold increase on average)(Lyu et al., 2019). Activation through KOH could improve the catalytic performance of biochar as well (Zhu et al., 2015; Nguyen et al., 2016; Cho et al., 2017). Zhu et al. (2015) reported an approach to activate biochar through KOH solution. Biochar was impregnated in KOH aqueous solution for $^ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ and dried at $1 1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ . The specific surface area of biochar increased from 40 to $1 0 5 8 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g }$ after activation. The amount of micropores and mesopores was $78 \%$ and $2 2 \%$ for activated-biochar, respectively. The presence of mesopores facilitated the reactant and products to pass in and out of the interior of the catalyst, which increased the catalytic ability of biochar.

# 2.2. Preparation of biochar-based catalysts

$\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ , ZnO, $\mathsf { S n O } _ { 2 }$ , $Z \mathrm { r } 0 _ { 2 }$ , and CdS are the most used metal photocatalysts and have lots of advantages, such as a high activity and good chemical stability (Zhang et al., 2017). However, these metal catalysts are limited by their large band gap width, and they are difficult to separate from the aqueous solution, easy to photodissolve and release harmful metal ions (Colmenares et al., 2016). The immobilization of these metal photocatalysts on biochar has been recently used to overcome the drawbacks of metal photocatalysts and enhance the removal efficiency of contaminants through the cooperation of adsorption and photocatalysis (Makrigianni et al., 2015; Jia et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2019b; Lu et al., 2019). Zhang et al. (2017) synthesized biochar-supported $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ $( \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathsf { p B C } )$ for the removal of sulfamethoxazole from aqueous solution. Biochar was purified with $1 \mathrm { m o l } / \mathrm { L }$ hydrochloric acid solution to remove impurities. The resultant acid-pretreated biochar (pBC) was immersed in $5 0 \mathrm { m L }$ absolute ethanol, followed by mixing with $2 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of tetrabutyl titanate and $2 . 5 \mathrm { m L }$ acetic acid. The mixture was stirred for $^ { 6 \mathrm { h } }$ , aged for $^ { 2 \mathrm { d } }$ , dried at $1 0 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 8 \mathrm { h } }$ and then calcined in a muffle furnace for $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . The support by biochar prevented the agglomeration of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ particle, resulting in a larger surface area $( 1 0 2 \mathrm { { m } } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { { g } ) }$ compared to the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ( 0 . 3 9 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ and a higher sulfamethoxazole removal efficiency $( 9 1 . 2 7 \%$ for $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathsf { p B C }$ compared to $5 8 . 4 7 \%$ for $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ ). It should be noted that the surface area of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathsf { p B C }$ was lower than that of BC, which is common for biochar-based composites. The metal photocatalysts occupied a portion of the pores of the biochar, resulting in a decrease in the specific surface area of the composite. However, the metal photocatalysts attached to the surface of the biochar can effectively degrade the contaminants adsorbed by the biochar, and thus, they enhanced the catalytic degradation efficiency of the contaminants. Lu et al. (2019) found that biochar acted as an electron acceptor to transport electrons from the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ surface and promote the separation of photo-induced electron-hole pairs.

As a metal-free organic semiconductor photocatalyst with a good visible light response, graphitic carbon nitride $\left( { \bf g - C } _ { 3 } \mathbf { N } _ { 4 } \right)$ has been widely applied to environmental photocatalysis. Biochar has been used to support $\mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ to overcome its low photocatalytic efficiency due to its bulk layered structure (Pi et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2016; Zhi et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019). Pi et al. (2015) obtained $\mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ modified biochar $\left( \mathsf { B C @ C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } } \right)$ by mixing melamine and biochar. Upon $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ of grinding, the mixture was transferred into a ceramic crucible, heated at $3 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ , and then, deaminated at $5 2 0 ^ { \circ } C$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ . The $\mathsf { C } _ { 3 } \mathsf { N } _ { 4 }$ and biochar retained their pristine status and were fulfilled in the composite. The resulting ${ \mathsf { B C @ C } } _ { 3 } { \mathsf { N } } _ { 4 }$ offered the highest methylene blue decolorization efficiency. However, the pyrolysis temperature of the biochar preparation was $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ in their study, which was much higher than the temperature normally used to prepare biochar $( < 7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ . Li et al. (2019) used a bio-based porous carbon microspheres and melamine to prepare biocharcoupled $\mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ nanosheets. The waste camellia oleifera shells were hydrothermally treated at $2 3 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ , and then the hydrothermal carbon was annealed at $6 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ . After being mixed with KOH, the mixture was calcined at $8 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ under an argon atmosphere. The resultant bio-based porous carbon microspheres were mixed with melamine and calcined at $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } C$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ under an argon atmosphere at a heating rate of $5 ^ { \circ } { \bf C } / \mathrm { m i n }$ to obtain biochar-coupled $\mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ nanosheets. They found that the biochar-coupled $\mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ nanosheets has a much higher reactivity towards $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ than $\mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ .

When heteroatoms such as nitrogen are added to the surface of biochar, a modified biochar catalyst with a changed carbon matrix electron distribution and increased catalytic sites can be produced. Huang et al. (2019b) reported an approach to introduce nitrogen and Fe onto the surface of biochar to produce magnetic N-doped sludge biochar through pyrolysis in the presence of $\Nu _ { 2 }$ . During the synthesis, polyacrylamide (PAM) and polyferric sulfate (PFS) acted as a nitrogen resource and Fe source, respectively. Sewage sludge, PAM and PFS were placed in a tubular reactor at a PFS/PAM mass ratio of $4 4 / 1$ . After being charged with $\Nu _ { 2 }$ , the tubular reactor was heating to 400, 600, and $8 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and maintained for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ , respectively, with a heating rate of $5 ^ { \circ } { \bf C } / \mathrm { m i n }$ . The magnetic N-doped sludge biochar had significantly increased functional groups compared to unmodified biochar because of the presence of nitrogen and iron oxides on the biochar surface (Yue et al., 2017).

# 3. Active sites of biochar and biochar-based catalysts

PFRs are a key factor affecting catalytic ability of biochar toward hydrogen peroxide/persulfate activation (Khachatryan and Dellinger, 2011; Fang et al., 2015a). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) was used to quantify the PFR concentration in the biochar (Khachatryan et al., 2014). In addition, the metal and/or metal-free catalysts can bring new properties to the biochar. The physical and chemical properties of biochar and biochar-based catalysts vary with the types of metal and/or metal-free catalysts. Many characterization methods, including photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL), scanning electron microscope (SEM), energydispersive X-rayanalysis (EDAX), energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), BET adsorption method, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), elemental analysis and ultra violet-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UVevis DRS) have been used to determine the physiochemical properties of biochar-based catalysts (Dong et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2019).

Fang et al., 2015a investigated the effects of the pyrolysis temperature and time on the PFRs in biochar. The EPR spectra showed that the intensity of the EPR signal increased with increasing pyrolysis temperatures from 300 to $6 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ (Fig. 2a). For 500 and $6 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ biochars, the PFR concentration decreased as the pyrolysis time increased from 1 to $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { h } }$ . Meanwhile, it markedly increased for 300 and $4 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ biochars (Fig. 2b). Therefore, in order to obtain the highest catalytic performance, the optimal pyrolysis time for the low temperature biochars (e.g., ${ < } 5 0 0 { ^ \circ } \mathrm { C }$ ) was $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { h } }$ , while it was $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ for higher pyrolysis temperature biochars (e.g., ${ > } 5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } $ . Using EPR measurements, Yang et al., (2016) investigated the degradation of $p$ -nitrophenol on biochars. # 4. Applications of biochar and biochar-based catalysts

Biochar and its composites have been widely studied for the remediation of both organic and inorganic contaminants in groundwater and soil, as summarized in Fig. 4 and Table 1. The most extensive environmental uses of biochar and biochar-based catalysts have been for the degradation of organic contaminants (Qin et al., 2016; Zhang and Wu, 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Mian et al., 2019), including $p$ -Nitrophenol (Yang et al., 2016), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) (Huang et al., 2018), Chlorobenzene (Zhang and Wu, 2017), and 2-chlorobiphenyl (2-CB) (Fang et al., 2014), which have been a major threat to human health and environment. Moreover, biochar and biochar-based catalysts can facilitate the reduction of inorganic contaminants to reduce their toxicity (Jeon et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2018; Zhong et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019). Biochar and biochar-based catalysts has been proven to be one of the promising materials for the remediation of contaminants due to their efficient, economic, and environmentally friendly advantages. Based on the mechanisms, biochar and biochar-based catalysts can remove the contaminants through oxidation, reduction, and photocatalysis.

# 4.1. Biochar and biochar-based catalysts for oxidation

Biochar and biochar-based catalysts can degrade contaminants, especially organic contaminants through oxidation with and/or without the addition of oxidants. First, organic contaminants can be directly degraded by biochar without the addition of oxidants (Fang et al., 2015b; Qin et al., 2017). Qin et al. (2017) studied the effectiveness of biocharpyrolyzed at different temperatures (300, 400, 500, 600, and $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ for 1,3-dichloropropene (1,3-D) degradation. They found that the moisture content of biochar played an important role in the removal of 1,3-D. The higher the biochar moisture content, the better the adsorption performance of biochar. The highest 1,3-D removal efficiency $( 3 5 \% )$ was achieved at a moisture content of $8 0 \%$ , which might be ascribed to the accelerated hydration by the adsorbed water (Huang et al., 2015). The degradation rate of 1,3-D decreased as the biochar pyrolysis temperature increased from 300 to $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } C$ which was ascribed to the decreased concentration of OH radical. Further increasing the pyrolysis temperature from 500 to $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ resulted in an increase in the degradation rate, which was contributed the enlarged pore size of $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ biochar, suggesting the role of porosity-selective adsorption. It is worth nothing that the EPFR concentrations of biochars were not positively related to the 1,3-D degradation rate (Fig. 5a). For example, $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } C$ biochar (R-500) had the highest EPFR concentration, while it has the lowest degradation rate $( 0 . 0 0 0 7 3 \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 } \cdot$ ). They concluded that the EPFRs of biochar contained external and internal radicals. Not all EPFRs in biochars were involved in the reaction. Only the external EPFRs enabling the reaction on the exposed surface could react with ${ { 0 } _ { 2 } }$ and induce  OH, which catalyzed the 1,3-D degradation. Modification method, such as ball milling, can expose the internal radicals of biochar to enhance the catalytic activity (Lyu et al., 2017a). These results were consistent with those of Fang et al., 2015b, who investigated OH generation from biochar suspensions for diethyl phthalate (DEP) degradation and found that producing one trapped [OH] molecule consumed approximately 12 spins of EPFRs. EPFRs in biochar transferred electrons to ${ { 0 } _ { 2 } }$ to produce the superoxide radical anion and hydrogen peroxide, which reacted further with EPFRs to produce  OH (Fig. 5b) (Tian et al., 2009; Fang et al., 2015b).

With the addition of different oxidants (e.g., $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , persulfate, and peroxymonosulfate), reactive oxygen species were generated by the catalysis of biochar and were used to degrade organic contaminants (Kemmou et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019a; Wang and Wang, 2019; Xu et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2019). Wang and Wang (2019) found that triclosan could be effectively degraded under the biochar activation of peroxymonosulfate. The results showed that $1 . 0 { \mathrm { g / L } }$ biochar and $0 . 8 \mathrm { m M }$ peroxymonosulfate can remove nearly $1 0 0 \%$ of $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ triclosan solution within $^ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ at an initial pH of 7.2, and dechlorination and hydroxylation were the main degradation pathways of triclosan. In the presence of tertiary butanol, ethanol, and sodium azide, the removal efficiency of triclosan decreased by $4 4 . 1 \%$ , $5 2 . 8 \%$ , and $1 7 . 4 \%$ , respectively. The quenching experiment results indicated that hydroxyl radicals, sulfate radicals and singlet oxygen contributed to the degradation of triclosan. Kemmou et al. (2018) studied the factors affecting the ability of biochar to activate sodium persulfate for sulfamethoxazole degradation. Humic acid at a concentration of $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ increased the efficiency of $2 5 0 \mu \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ sulfamethoxazole being degraded by $9 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ biochar and $2 5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ sodium persulfate. It was proposed that humic acid accelerated the oxidation of the surface by accelerating the production of $\mathrm { H S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ ions. Huang et al., 2019a, Huang et al., 2019b) reported that biochar $J \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ systems exhibited high tetracycline degradation ability. The contents of metals and phenolic compounds in biochar affected the concentration of PFRs, thus affecting the catalytic properties of biochar. Their biochars were prepared from corn stalks (CN), bamboo (BA), and pig manure (PM) at $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathsf C$ , and labeled as CN500, BA500, and PM500, respectively, based on the feedstock type. The results showed that $1 2 . 3 \%$ , $1 4 . 6 \%$ , and $1 1 . 7 \%$ of $3 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ tetracycline was removed by PM500, CN500, and BA500 through adsorption, respectively. Upon adding $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , the degradation efficiency of tetracycline increased to $1 0 0 \%$ for $\mathrm { P M } 5 0 0 / \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } 0 _ { 2 }$ , $9 6 \%$ for $\mathsf { C N } 5 0 0 /$ $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , and $8 7 \%$ for $\mathsf { B A S 0 0 } / \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 2 }$ , respectively. These results suggested that PFRs contributed to the activation of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ by biochar to produce  OH radicals for the degradation of tetracycline.

A combination of biochar and other catalysts (i.e., metal catalysts and metal free catalysts) can further improve the catalytic properties of biochar and even create new properties (Yan et al., 2014; Park et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2019). Among metal-based catalysts, Fe-based compounds were a better choice due to their low toxicity and magnetic properties, which might contribute to the magnetic recovery of the catalyst (Dong et al., 2019; Fu et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2019). Yu et al. (2019) combined the advantages of iron and nitrogen to prepare magnetic nitrogendoped biochar (MS-biochar) for the activation of persulfate to degrade tetracycline. They divided the MS-biochar into four parts, i.e., dissolved organic matter, acid-soluble substances, carbon matrix, and basal part, and calculated their relative contributions (Fig. 5c). For higher pyrolysis temperature biochar-based composites (e.g., MS-800), the carbon matrix and basal part played a dominant role in the activation of persulfate (approximately $5 0 \%$ contribution). This was ascribed to a higher graphitic degree and more abundant graphitic carbon. Meanwhile, for lower pyrolysis temperature biochar-based composites (e.g., MS-400 and MS-600), ASS took the important position for persulfate activation ( $7 0 \%$ and $6 5 \%$ , respectively). Yan et al. (2014) investigated the effectiveness of the nanoscale zero valent iron/biochar composite (nZVI/biochar) to activate persulfate to degrade trichloroethylene (TCE). Batch tests showed that at an nZVI-to-biochar mass ratio of 1:5, $4 . 5 \mathrm { m m o l / L }$ nZVI/biochar and $4 . 5 \mathrm { m m o l / L }$ persulfate reduced $0 . 1 5 \mathrm { m m o l / L }$ TCE by $9 9 . 4 \%$ in $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . The TCE degradation efficiency of biocharpersulfate was higher than that of biochar $2 5 . 3 \%$ vs. $1 4 . 6 \%$ ), indicating that the oxygen functional groups (biochar surface-COOH or biochar surface-OH) promoted the electron transfer and decomposition of persulfate. nZVI/biochar-persulfate had the highest TCE degradation efficiency $( 1 0 0 \% )$ , which was ascribed to the synergistic effect of the redox action of $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathsf { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ and biochar oxygen functional groups on $S _ { 2 } 0 _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - }$ decomposition (Pan et al., 2017). In general, oxidant (e.g., $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , persulfate, peroxydisulfate, and peroxymonosulfate) activation by biochar and biochar-based catalysts can be summarized as follows: 1) biochar and biochar-based catalysts transfer electron to $S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - }$ or $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to generate $S O _ { 4 ^ { \bullet } } ^ { - }$ or OH, respectively; 2) biochar and biochar-based catalysts transfer electrons to ${ { 0 } _ { 2 } }$ to generate $0 _ { 2 } \bullet ^ { - }$ , and then ${ { 0 } _ { 2 } }$ reacted with $S _ { 2 } 0 _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - }$ to generate $S O _ { 4 ^ { \bullet } } ^ { - }$ ; and 3) $S O _ { 4 } \bullet ^ { - }$ reacted with $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ or $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ to generate  OH, and then, contaminants were degraded by $S O _ { 4 } \bullet ^ { - }$ and  OH.

# 4.2. Biochar and biochar-based catalysts for photocatalysis

In addition to degrading organic contaminants in the presence of oxidants, biochar and biochar-based composites can photocatalytically degrade contaminants under UV and/or visible light irradiation (Dong et al., 2013; Fu et al., 2016; Li et al., 2016; Xu, 2016; Jeon et al., 2017). Lu et al. (2019) prepared a $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } |$ biochar catalyst and investigated its degradation ability for methyl orange under UV irradiation. The $0 . 2 5 \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L T i O } _ { 2 } /$ /biochar removed $9 6 . 8 8 \%$ of $2 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ methyl orange solution at a biochar to Ti weight ratio of 0.2 in $1 4 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . Quenching experiments were conducted to confirm which factor was responsible for the photocatalytic process. The results showed that holes, hydroxyl radicals, and superoxide anion radicals contributed to the oxidation of methyl orange, and hydroxyl radicals played a dominant role (Fig. 5d). Pi et al. (2015) found that biochar acted as co-catalysts to provide sufficient catalytic sites for $\mathrm { g - C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ under LED light irradiation. Compared to the pure $\mathsf { C } _ { 3 } \mathsf { N } _ { 4 }$ , the biochar $@ { \mathsf { C } } _ { 3 } { \mathsf { N } } _ { 4 }$ composite showed an enhanced methylene blue removal efficiency $91 \%$ vs. $4 1 \%$ ), indicating the important role of the photocatalytic process caused by the incorporated light-responsive $\mathsf { C } _ { 3 } \mathsf { N } _ { 4 }$ . The methylene blue removal rate increased with the increasing biochar-to- ${ \cdot } C _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ mass ratio, which suggested that the adsorption played an important role for the methylene blue removal. In general, biochar and biochar-based photocatalysts were activated in the following steps (equations (1)e(4)): 1) irradiation to move electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, 2) the formation of the electronehole pair, and 3) the formation of OH and $0 z ^ { \bullet ^ { - } }$ , and then, contaminants were degraded on the photocatalyst surface.

# 4.3. Biochar and biochar-based catalysts for reduction

Biochar and biochar-based catalysts can facilitate contaminant reduction to reduce their toxicity, especially for the heavy metal $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ (Yu et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019). Compared to $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ , which has been considered to be a top priority hazardous pollutant, $\mathrm { { C r } ( I I I ) }$ is less toxic and exhibited low risk to the ecological environment. Therefore, reducing $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ to $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathrm { I I I } )$ to minimize its toxicity and has attracted increasing attention. Xu et al. (2019) found that the oxygen-containing functional groups were involved in the reduction of $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ by biochar. For instance, upon reacting with $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ , the amounts of ${ \sf C } - { \sf O }$ (in the form of phenol, alcohol, or ether) and ${ \sf C } = { \sf O }$ (in the form of ketone or aldehyde) in biochar were reduced from $2 4 . 4 \%$ to $1 7 . 3 \%$ and from $1 1 . 6 \%$ to $9 . 6 5 \%$ , respectively. Meanwhile, the amount of -COOH increased from $5 . 7 1 \%$ to $8 . 1 9 \%$ . The results suggested that ${ \sf C } - { \sf O }$ and ${ \sf C } = { \sf O }$ were oxidized to eCOOH by $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ . In this case, biochar acted as an electron donor to reduce $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ to $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathrm { I I I } )$ . Lactate was selected as the electron donor to illustrate the role of biochar as an electron shuttle. The CrOOH and increasing $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ reduction rate were observed when lactate and biochar were added into the $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ solution, demeostrating that biochar could transfer electrons from lactate to $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ to stimulate $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ reduction. Jeon et al., (2017) found that $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ was reduced by biochar in the persence of orange G under UV irradiation, suggesting that the organic acids produced by the orange G oxidation might act as electron donors for $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ reduction. The $\mathrm { C r } ( \mathsf { V I } )$ reduction efficiency was increased through adding persulfate into the system, implying the formation of $S O { _ { 4 } } { ^ { \bullet } } ^ { - }$ activated by biochar. Therefore, biochars were found to play a key role in the reduction/ oxidation of inorganic contaminants.

# 5. Conclusions and future directions

With excellent catalytic properties, biochar and biochar-based composites have a potential future in environmental applications. Recent progress of the preparation procedures and factors affecting biochar and biochar-based catalysts were critically reviewed. Based on the results of the critical review, it can be concluded that the catalytic abilities of biochar and biochar-based catalysts strongly depend on the synthetic processes, including the pyrolysis temperature and transition metals. Overall, the environmental application of biochar in catalysis can be carried out in three ways: 1) the EPFRs of biochar reacted with ${ { 0 } _ { 2 } }$ to induce hydroxyl radicals (OH); 2) the activation of hydrogen peroxide or persulfate to generate hydroxyl radicals (OH) or sulfate radicals $( \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \bullet ^ { - } )$ , respectively; and 3) act as an electron acceptor and an adsorbent to improve the photodegradation capacity of catalysts. Biochar has been developed as a new alternative activator for contaminant degradation. However, the development and application of biochar and biocharbased catalysts are still limited by the following research challenges: 1) Biochar can be prepared using a wide range of biomass sources. Various synthetic conditions will be required to obtain biochar with a higher catalytic activity. Further investigation based on the correlations between the biochar properties (e.g., EPFR concentration) and the synthetic conditions to optimize the process of customizing biochar is essential and requires further research. 2) Current research on biochar catalysts is still limited to laboratoryscale studies, and large-scale assessments of biochar catalysts and their technical and economic applications should be encouraged. 3) Biochar-based catalysts have been developed to enhance the catalytic activity of biohar, however, few efforts have been made to investigate the effect of biochar-based catalysts on the ecoenvironment. 4) Economic cost analysis and life cycle assessment of biochar-based catalysts need to be determined.
