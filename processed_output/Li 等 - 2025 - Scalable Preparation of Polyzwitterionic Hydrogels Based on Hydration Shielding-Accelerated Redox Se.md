# Introduction

Polyzwitterionic hydrogels (PZHs) are a class of polymeric materials with unique chemical network structures containing equal amount of positive and negative charges. This structure endows PZHs outstanding hydration ability and several exceptional properties, including remarkable antifouling performance, desired antimicrobial properties, good biocompatibility, excellent lubricity, and potential ions transport capacity[1] Because of these features, PZHs have been extensively studied and applied in various fields, such as blood-contacting devices,[2,3] lubricated surfaces,[4] antibacterial coatings,[5] wound dressings,[6] biosensors[] wearable devices,[8] and antifreezing coating.[9] The design and preparation of PZHs are practically essential for achieving their wide range of applications. However, owing to possessing strong hydration ability, zwitterions can strongly bind water molecules via long-range electrostatic interaction, forming a stable hydration layer that acts as an additional steric barrier (Figure 1a (i)).[10] During the preparation of PZHs, the hydration layer impedes the formation of dipole— dipole interaction between zwitterionic units and isolates zwitterions,[11] greatly reducing their collision probability. Besides, the presence of the steric barrier implies more energy is required to polymerize zwitterions. Considering the influence of hydration layer and steric barrier, the difficulty of preparing PZHs has significantly increased.

Traditional synthesis methods for PZHs usually employ thermal or UV triggered system to construct the hydrogel network through radical polymerization. Under continuous high-temperature heating or UV irradiation, the photo or thermal initiator generate free radicals to trigger the polymerization of zwitterionic monomers, gradually forming the hydrogel network. The advantage of employing UV or thermal initiated radical polymerization to prepare PZHs is that it can partially counteract the hydration effect during polymerization with sustained high-energy input. However, these synthesis methods come with unavoidable limitations: 1) harsh reaction conditions. Traditional methods require high temperature heating (above $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ) ^ { [ 1 2 - 1 4 ] }$ or intense light irradiation[15] for adequate polymerization energy, and some systems even require inert atmospheres;[16,17] 2) time-consumption and dependence of external energy. UV/thermal systems require continuous energy input for complete polymerization of the hydrogel precursor, increasing time consumption.[18,19]Although UV triggered systems can shorten reaction times using lower wavelength UV light,5-17,202hih-energyirradiation may cause uedictable effects on the hydrogel structure.[11] Moreover, producing some functional PZHs requires a multistep reaction that utilizes various polymerization methods;(3,23] 3) high monomer concentration. Traditional synthesis methods typically utilize higher monomer concentrations to obtain desired mechanical properties.[24,25] However, excessive monomer concentration not only makes the manufacturing process uncontrollable and costly, but also triggers the antipolyelectrolyte effect, reducing the resistance to swelling and brittl.26,27] Through a systematic investigation of recent studies on synthesizing PZHs via traditional methods (Table S1), it was found that the current polymerization methods could not simultaneously achieve mild reaction conditions (without UV irradiation or heating), shorted polymerization durations, and reasonable monomer incorporation concentrations during preparing PZHs. In general, the harsh conditions and limiting factors of traditional polymerization methods restrict the use and development of PZHs in various scenarios, including emergency hemostasis and cryogenic manufacturing. On the other hand, these limitations prevent the use of current polymerization methods to eliminate the effects of hydration zwitterions and achieve rapid, facile preparation of PZHs in mild environments. In recent research, redox-catalyzed polymerization has been proved to successfully synthesize PZHs in mild environments[28,9] However, using redoxcatalyzed mechanisms to prepare PZHs still has possessed the key disadvantages of long reaction times or high monomer concentrations. Hence, the facile and rapid preparation of PZHs at very low monomer concentration in a mild environment without external energy input is still a great challenge in the field of PZHs synthesis chemistry.

Here, we develop a novel method for synthesizing PZHs, termed hydration-shielding accelerated self-catalytic polymerization (HS-A-RP). In the HS-A-RP method, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was introduced into the silver ions $\left( \mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } ^ { + } \right)$ - potassium persulfate $( \mathsf { S } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - } )$ redox catalytic system as a hydration shielding agent. It was found that PVA molecular chains can provide hydration shielding to hydrated zwitterionic monomers, thereby eliminating the hydration effect of zwitterions in polymerization. Moreover, the incorporated PVA facilitated the aggregation of zwitterions into dehydration monomers, eliminating the steric barrier and increasing their collision probability. Under the dual infulence of hydration-shielding generated from PVA and the formation of dehydration aggregated monomers, the HS-A-RP method overcame polymerization the limitations of concentration, time and temperature, achieving ultra-fast polymerization $_ { ( < 6 0 \mathrm { ~ s ) ~ } }$ at extremely low monomer concentration $( \mathbf { w t \% }$ : $0 . 3 \%$ )across a wide temperature range ( $- 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $3 7 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ without external energy input. The as-prepared polyzwitterionic hydrogels from HS-A-RP exhibited denser network structure and better mechanical properties compared to traditional method, along with excellent antiswelling, lubrication, antibacterial, and antifouling properties. HS-A-RP is applicable to scalable preparation of polyzwitterionic hydrogel coating on various substrates under extreme environment condition, significantly expanding its applications scenarios.

# Results and Discussions

Transition metal ions, such as silver ions, possess outstanding redox properties and accelerate radical polymerization in mild environments with the assistance of ammonium persulfate (APS) or polyphenols[30,31] Although the $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } ^ { + }$ assisted self-catalytic polymerization method (SCP) requires no external energy, it still demands longer reaction time and higher monomer concentrations. Importantly, reducing the zwitterionic monomer concentration $( \mathrm { w t \% } ~ \leq ~ 1 0 \% )$ highly amplifies the hydration effect during polymerization, which is difficult to overcome even by traditional thermal or UV initiation systems (Figure 1a (i)). As shown in Figure S1, [2-(Methacryloyloxy) ethyl] dimethyl-(3-sulfopropyl) ammonium hydroxide (SBMA) was employed as the model zwitterion, when the zwitterionic monomer concentration is extremely low, polymerization reaction is difficult to implement for traditional thermal and UV triggered systems, as well as SCP ((Figure 1c (left) and Figure S2)). To explore how water molecules affect the polymerization of zwitterionic monomers through hydration effect, the binding energy between different functional groups on the molecular chain of SBMA and water molecules is calculated by density functional theory (DFT) (Figure S3 (A)). As shown in Figure S3 (B)-(E), the $- \mathrm { C = C - }$ double bond and the ester group of SBMA show affinity to water molecules as similar as the sulfonate group, which suggests that they also contribute to the formation of the zwitterion's hydration layer. When the monomer's concentration is low, a large number of water molecules (relative to the zwitterions in solution) will aggregate around the SBMA. In this case, water molecules will be attracted and aggregated near the zwitterions owing to the affinity of $- \mathrm { C = C - }$ double bond and ester group for water. The presence of the amount of water molecules will significantly amplify the hydration effect generated from water to the zwitterions, which makes it difficult to overcome such a barrier to successfully polymerize zwitterionic monomers via even traditional initiation systems. Current HS-A-RP method incorporates the hydration shielding agent PVA into the redox catalytic polymerization system. Typically, PVA molecular chains, rich in hydroxyl groups $\scriptstyle ( - \mathrm { O H } )$ , possess excellent hydration abilities and carry a negative charge in aqueous solution. Hence, PVA chains could encapsulate the highly hydrated zwitterionic monomers in solution through either electrostatic interactions or physical means (Figure S4 (A)). In this case, the hydroxyl groups on the PVA chain will attract the water molecules constituting the hydration layer of zwitterions through van der Waals forces and/or electrostatic interactions (Figure S4 (B)), thus forming competitive attraction to water molecules.

The competitive binding of PVA to water molecules mitigates the strong hydration effect of zwitterions in polymerization. This proposed mechanism can also be confirmed by DFT calculations and IGM results. Specifically, for the encapsulated zwitterionic monomers, there are four theoretical structural domains that can be formed between them and PVA: hydroxyl groups-water moleculestwo sulfonate groups (from different SBMA molecules), hydroxyl groups-water molecules-one sulfonate group and one ammonium group (from different SBMA molecules), hydroxyl groups-water molecules-two ammonium groups (from different SBMA molecules), and hydroxyl groupswater molecules-one sulfonate group and one ammonium group (from the same SBMA molecule), as shown in Figure S5. The intermolecular forces for each of these four structural domains were simulated. As shown in Figure S6, the formation of tough hydration layer around SBMA is mainly due to the sulfonate group on the molecular chain can bind water molecules through intermolecular forces intermediate between weak mutual attraction and van der Waals forces. In all theoretical structural domains (Figure S6 (i)-(iv)), the hydroxyl groups on the PVA molecular chain are able to attract water from the hydration layer of encapsulated zwitterions in an analogous manner. From the DFT calculations, the binding energy of PVA to water molecules in a water-rich system is similar to that of SBMA to water molecules (Figure S7), indicating that PVA is sufficient to generate a competitive attraction to hydrated water with encapsulated zwitterionic monomers. As a result, PVA chains induce zwitterions to aggregate, ultimately forming dehydrated monomer clusters by dipole— dipole interactions|25] (Figure 1b (i)), as characterized by the dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements. The addition of PVA to the SBMA monomer solution led to a significant decrease in zeta potential (Figure S8, blue dots), neutralizing the original positive charge and confirming the effective encapsulation of zwitterionic monomers by PVA chains through electrostatic interactions. Correspondingly, the particle size of the monomer in SBMA/PVA solution exceeded $5 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , even at low monomer concentrations (Figures 2a, S9, blue dots). In contrast, the monomer in SBMA solution without PVA required increasing mass concentration to $3 0 \%$ to achieve the same particle size (Figures 2a, S9, red dots). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) were also employed to observe the microscopic morphology of particles in the SBMA system and SBMA/PVA system to further validate the DLS results. As shown in Figure S10, five representative monomer concentrations were selected as the test samples. With the monomer concentration increased, the evolution trend of particle size in solution coincided with the DLS results, and the critical polymerization concentration presented in the SCP method was also well demonstrated through the TEM bright-field images. Combined with the EDS elemental mapping, it was found that PVA possessed excellent dispersibility in solutions, generating a more excellent encapsulation and induced aggregation effect for zwitterions in the real environment. These results from DLS and TEM fully confirm the successful formation of dehydration monomers clusters. The difference in monomer particle size, with and without hydration shielding agent, confirms the formation of dehydration monomers clusters. In brief, the hydration shielding from PVA chains and the formation of dehydrated monomers weakened the effect of zwitterions' hydration layer in polymerization, reducing the distance among zwitterions and eliminating the steric barrier. This hydration shielding effect promotes the collision and reaction of zwitterionic monomers at the initiated stage of polymerization, enabling faster formation of polyzwitterionic chains. As a result, the polymerization of zwitterions via HSA-RP is no longer dependent on monomer concentration, and can occur at very low monomer concentration (Figure 1b (ii) and Figure 1c (right)). Meanwhile, the redox process consisting of silver ions $\left( \mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } ^ { + } \right)$ and potassium persulfate $( \mathsf { S } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - } )$ can further reduce the activation energy of initiator decomposition at the early stage of polymerization, thus endowing HS-A-RP with fast reaction feature and low energy input without requiring heating and UV irradiation.

The proposed mechanisms of HS-A-RP were investigated using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and $\mathbf { X }$ -ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements. As shown in Figure 2b, both distinct hydroxyl radical peaks (blue points) and sulfate radical peaks (orange points) were observed in the ${ \bf A g N O } _ { 3 }$ and APS solution, indicating that hydroxyl and sulfate radicals from $\mathrm { { A g ^ { + } / S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - } }$ redox system initiated zwitterions polymerization. Additionally, as shown in Figure 2c, the corresponding g-factor peak of silver in HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) shifted obviously upon comparing with $\mathbf { A g N O } _ { 3 }$ , suggesting that the HS-A-RP had undergone a valence transition of silver ion during the catalytic polymerization. The XPS and Auger spectroscopy were employed to further reveal the changes (Figures S11, 2d). After polymerization, the peak in MNN regions of silver ions shifted from monovalent to divalent, indicating electron transfer via redox reactions.[32] As a result, the whole catalytic polymerization mechanism of HS-A-RP is suggested in Figure 1d. Specifically, the redox reaction between monovalent silver ions and persulfate initially produces trivalent silver ions and sulfate radicals. These radicals react with water to generate the hydroxyl radicals, initiating zwitterion polymerization. Meanwhile, trivalent silver ions in the system combined with the remaining monovalent silver ions to form a divalent state.[33] Based on the self-catalytic feature of $\mathrm { A g ^ { + } / \ S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - }$ system, HS-A-RP could realize the rapid, mild and lowenergy manufacture of PZHs from low temperature to room temperature.

The enhanced hydration shielding effect of HS-A-RP breaks the concentration, time, and temperature limitations of traditional radical methods (HEAT and UV) for preparing PZHs. The polymerization efficiency of HS-A-RP was explored by modulating the contents of $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } ^ { + }$ catalyst, initiator, temperature, and the monomer concentrations (details in Tables S2S4). As shown in Figure 2e, the gelation time of SBMA monomer precursor decreased continuously as the catalyst concentration increased, reducing to less than $1 \mathrm { m i n }$ at a constant catalyst concentration of $4 \%$ . The relationship between initiator concentration and gelation time mirrored that of catalyst concentration (Figure S12). Furthermore, the effect of hydration shielding agent (PVA) on gelation time was also evaluated. As shown in Figure $^ { 2 \mathrm { f } , \mathrm { g } }$ (blue dots), polymerization reaction of the SBMA monomer precursor with hydration shielding agent implemented rapidly (average gelation time: ${ \sim } 1 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ ), independent of monomer concentrations. In contrast, the SBMA monomer precursor without hydration shielding agent required longer gelation time at the same monomer concentrations (Figure $2 \mathrm { g }$ ,brown dots). These results confirm that introducing the hydration shielding agent in HS-A-RP method breaks the harsh polymerization limitations of high critical concentration for traditional HEAT and UV methods, and significantly shortening the manufacture time of PZHs. Besides, the impact of PVA incorporating concentration and inherent molecular weights (details in Tables S5,S6) on gelation dynamics was further characterized. In Figures S13(A) and S14(A), the gelation time of zwitterions exhibited a decreasing and then increasing trend with rising PVA content as well as molecular weights. This may be attributed to a synergistic effect of two factors, the number of effective hydroxyl groups capable of generating the hydration shielding effect and the viscosity of PVA solutions. When the molecular weight is low $( \mathrm { M w } = 9 0 0 0 -$ $1 0 ~ 0 0 0$ and $3 1 ~ 0 0 0 { - } 5 0 ~ 0 0 0 )$ or the content of PVA below 10 $\mathbf { w t \% }$ , there are not sufficient effective hydroxyl groups to generate the hydration shielding effect, resulting in a longer polymerization time. In this case, the main factor affecting the gelation time is the number of effective hydroxyl groups on the PVA molecular chain. Although when the molecular weight is high $\left( \mathrm { M w } = 8 9 \ 0 0 0 { - } 9 8 \ 0 0 0 \right.$ and 148 000180 000) or the PVA content is higher than $1 0 \ \mathrm { \ w t { \% } }$ , although the number of effective hydroxyl groups is enough, the viscosity of monomer precursor increases significantly with the rise of PVA molecular weight or its content. The movement and the transfer of initial radicals and monomer radicals in solution will be hindered owing to the increase of precursor's viscosity, which in turn reduces the polymerization rate. Currently, the viscosity of precursors replaces the number of effective hydroxyl groups on the PVA molecular chain as the main factor affecting the gelation time.

To validate our hypothesis, the precursors' viscosity after introducing different molecular weights of PVA as well as different contents of PVA were measured by rheological tests, as shown in Figures S13(B) and S14(B). The dynamic viscosities of low molecular weights and contents PVA were 23 orders of magnitude different from that of high molecular weights and contents of PVA. The viscosity of PVA (10 $\mathbf { w t \% }$ , $\mathrm { M _ { w } } = 7 4 ~ \mathrm { 8 0 0 } { - } 7 9 ~ 0 0 0 $ applied in HS-A-RP method is in the middle of the viscosity range of all tested samples. The appropriate dynamic viscosity and the number of effective hydroxyl groups allow HS-A-RP method to exhibit the lowest gelation time in polymerizing zwitterionic monomers. These results strongly proved that the molecular weight and content of PVA selected for HS-A-RP was the optimal addition ratio during the synthesis of PZHs. Meanwhile, the introduction of the hydration shielding agent also densified the network structure of the formed PSBMA hydrogel (Figure 2h, right), compared to that prepared by SCP method (Figure 2h, left). In order to investigate the specific effect of PVA incorporation on mechanical performance of PSBMA hydrogels, the compression properties of PZHs were systematically evaluated as a reference. As shown in Figure S15, the compression strength and modulus of the hydrogel samples were significantly improved when a high content of PVA was selected as a hydration shielding agent to be incorporated into the PZHs. This means that PVA can also serve as a structural network to improve the mechanical properties of PZHs except for acting as a single hydration shielding agent.

Subsequently, the effect of temperature on HS-A-RP gelation time was investigated. Most of radical polymerizations methods fail at low temperatures. Benefiting from the synergy between efficient catalysis at silver ionic sites and the formation of dehydration aggregated monomer clusters, our HS-A-RP method can still work below $0 \ { } ^ { \circ } { \bf C }$ (Figure 2i), with gelation times of $9 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ and $9 . 5 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $- 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ respectively, indicating superior temperature tolerance compared to traditional HEAT and UV methods. To verify the universality of HS-A-RP method, four zwitterionic monomers (CBAA, SBMA, CBMA, and MPC) were tested for polymerization (Table S4), all exhibiting short gelation time under $2 . 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ (Figure 2j). However, the varying hydration levels resulted in different polymerization efficiencies with HS-A-RP due to charged groups and molecular structures in these zwitterionic monomers. CBAA exhibited shortest gelation time $( 0 . 0 7 ~ \mathrm { m i n } )$ , while MPC had a longer gelation time $( 2 . 5 \mathrm { \ m i n } )$ . Correspondingly, the gelation process of these zwitterions in SCP method was also characterized. As shown in Figure S16, SBMA monomer and MPC monomer exhibited both lower polymerization efficiency, while the gelation time of CBAA monomer increased from 0.07 to $4 . 6 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , which well confirmed HS-A-RP method is indeed able to accelerate the polymerization rate of PZHs. Besides, two hydroxyl-rich molecules, hydroxypropyl cellulose (HPC, with a strong hydration capacity as well as PVA) and dextran (Dex) were further employed to validate the universality of HS-A-RP mechanism (Figure S17). The Dex with different molecular weights and the HPC with different substitution degrees demonstrated similar hydration shielding acceleration effects as PVA. The gelation dynamic results from different zwitterions and different hydration shielding agents both revealed the mechanism universality of the HS-A-RP method. Finally, model monomer solutions were prepared for various polymerization methods as per experimental procedures. The gelation advantages of HS-A-RP could be displayed by recording the in situ gelation process in transparent sample bottle (Figures 2k, S18) and fast molding in complex molds (Figures 21, S19). As shown in Figure 2k, the gelation time for zwitterionic SBMA monomers solutions through HS-A-RP was only one-tenth or one-twentieth of that through HEAT method at the same concentration, and even longer by UV method where the solution was still liquid after irradiating of $4 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ Subsequently, by pouring SBMA hydrogel monomer precursors into various molds (lubrication-shaped groove, spiral glass tubes) and curing for $3 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , HS-A-RP method demonstrates greater gelation efficiency than traditional HEAT and UV methods.

Three groups of PSBMA hydrogels were prepared by using different polymerization methods: HpsBMA (HEAT), HpsBMA (UV), and HpsBMA (HS-A-RP), respectively (details in the Supporting Information). Compared to PSBMA hydrogels manufactured by traditional HEAT and UV methods, HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) possessed better mechanical performances. As shown in Figure 3a, HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) could easily lift the weight $( \approx 1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ g ~ } )$ without breaking, whereas HpsBMA (HEAT) and HpsBMA (UV) were impossible under the same loading condition. The mechanical properties of three PSBMA hydrogels were compared though tensile tests. As shown in Figure $^ { 3 \mathrm { b } , \mathrm { c } }$ , the tensile strength and modulus of $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { P S B M A } }$ (HS-A-RP) were 94.56 and $2 8 . 3 8 \ \mathrm { k P a }$ respectively, which were much higher than those of the hydrogels prepared by traditional HEAT and UV methods. Subsequently, $_ { 1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ g ~ } }$ weights were placed on the surfaces of three hydrogels to demonstrate their deformation resistance (Figure 3d). It was clearly observed that HpsBMA (HEAT) and HpsBMA (UV) demonstrated significant concave and convex deformations, whereas HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) showed no obvious shape change. Compression tests were conducted to further quantify the compressive properties of three hydrogels. As shown in Figure 3e, the compressive stress of HpsBMA (HS-A-RP), HpsBMA (HEAT) and HpsBMA (UV) were 1.24, 0.37, and $0 . 1 9 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ at $8 0 \%$ strain respectively. The compressive stress of HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) increased 3.4 times and 6.5 times, compared to HEAT and UV methods, respectively. Correspondingly, the compression modulus of HpSBMA(HS-A-RP)was $0 . 0 3 \mathrm { \ } \mathrm { M P a }$ , which was 10 times of HpsBMA (UV) and 3 times of HpsBMA (HEAT) (Figure 3f). These testing results indicated that the PZHs obtained by the HS-A-RP method have better mechanical strength than those prepared by traditional HEAT and UV methods.

The good mechanical properties of HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) originate from its inherent dense network structure. Therefore, surface and cross-section morphologies of three PSBMA hydrogels were characterized by employing scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Figure 3g,h, it was clear that the HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) formed a very dense 3D network structure, and only a few pores could be observed in the crosssection SEM images even at higher magnification. This dense structuring can be attributed to the hydration shielding effect of PVA chains and the generation of dehydrated monomer clusters, leading to highly entangled interpenetrating or crosslinked networks structures. In contrast, HpsBMA (HEAT) and HpsBMA (UV) exhibited hierarchical and porous structures, respectively. Cross-section SEM images also confirmed that the network structure of HpsBMA (HEAT) and HpsBMA (UV) were looser and sparser compared to HpsBMA (HS-A-RP). These SEM results revealed the reason why HpsBMA (HSA-RP) possessed better mechanical properties than HpBsMA (HEAT) and HpsBMA (UV). Furthermore, swelling is the primary cause of hydrogel failure during utilization. The antiswelling property of the HpsBMA (HS-A-RP), HpsBMA (HEAT), and HpsBMA (UV) samples was also evaluated in water. As shown in Figure 3i,j, the swelling ratio of HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) was maintained during the 7 days testing period, and no obvious volume change was observed. In contrast, significant volume changes were observed for both HpsBMA (HEAT) and HpsBMA (UV) (Figure S20), especially for HpsBMA (HEAT), which showed $1 6 0 \%$ swelling ratio. Overall, these characterization results illustrated that PZHs prepared via HS-A-RP method possessed denser network, stable mechanical properties, as well as remarkable antiswelling performance. These features are essential for PZHs employed as functional coatings in different fields.

Several common materials were selected as friction pairs to systematically evaluate the lubrication feature and lifespan of HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) (Figure 4a). The test results showed that the average coefficients of friction (COFs) of the HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) samples against various pairs maintained as low as $0 . 0 2 { \sim } 0 . 0 5$ upon applying different shear rates (50, 100, and $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { s } ^ { - 1 } }$ )under $3 0 ~ \mathrm { N }$ normal load (Figures 4b, S21). This effective friction-reduction was also observed across a range of normal loads from 10 to $5 0 \mathrm { N }$ at a constant shear rate of 100 $\mathbf { S } ^ { - 1 }$ (Figure 4c). These results indicated that HpsBMA (HS-ARP) could provide good hydration-lubrication performance at complex shearing and loading environments. This lubrication is facilitated by the ability of PZHs to attract water molecules via electrostatic interactions, forming a durable hydration layer on their surface. This hydration layer counteracts the van der Waals forces that arise when two surfaces come into contact, thus enabling lubrication at the contact interface.[34] Subsequently, the lubrication lifespan of the HpsBMA (HSA-RP) was evaluated. Within $1 2 ~ 0 0 0$ testing cycles based on different friction pairs, the COFs of HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) could always maintain around 0.025 under a contact pressure of $3 0 \ \mathrm { k P a }$ and a shear rate of $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { s } ^ { - 1 } }$ (Figures S22S26), indicating the robust wear-resistance of $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { P S B M A } }$ (HS-A-RP).

Considering the good lubricity of PSBMA hydrogel, HSA-RP could be utilized to modify the surface of biomedical device, especially interventional catheter device, reducing the mechanical damage and persistent pain to patients [35] (Figure 4d). Catheters with different diameters were chosen for lubrication modification on their external surfaces (Figure S27), using a hydrogel precursor solution composed of $0 . 6 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ initiator, $2 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ catalyst, and $1 0 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ SBMA. The surface-pretreated catheters were first immersed in an SBMA hydrogel precursor solution containing fluorescent dye, then slowly lifted out to perform HS-A-RP. After the polymerization, it could be clearly observed that the outer surfaces of catheters were uniformly coated with hydrogel layer (Figures 4e, S28). Correspondingly, cross-section optical microscopy was further used to examine the morphologies of the hydrogel coating on the catheters' outer surfaces (Figures 4f, S29).

Furthermore, to evaluate lubrication performance of the modified catheter, they were placed into the cavity of a soft plastic materials to simulate the relative sliding process in clinical (Figure S30 (i)), and interface friction force signals were captured during this process. As shown in Figure ${ 4 \mathrm { g } } ^ { }$ the friction force for bare polyvinyl chloride (PVC) catheter without modification was high and unstable, while it significantly reduced and became stable after being modified with PSBMA hydrogel coating. Subsequently, the inner surface of a long polyurethane (PU) gastric catheter was also modified with PSBMA hydrogel coating by HS-A-RP method. A metal guidewire was used as contact pair to simulate the insertion process of guidewire during surgery (Figure S30 (ii)). The catheter was knotted to simulate the complex geometry of the in vivo environment and increase the contact area between the contact pairs during sliding. The cross-section images of the modified catheter showed the successful generation of a uniform PSBMA hydrogel coating on the inner surface (Figure S30 (ii)). The pulling test demonstrated the decreased friction force and improved lubricity, compared to the bare catheter (Figure S31). These results suggest that HS-A-RP can be used to improve the lubricity of biomedical interventional devices.

Apart from providing hydration lubrication at the contact surfaces, the hydration layer formed by zwitterion can also perform as a physical barrier to resist the adsorption of proteins, cells, and bacteria.[1] Thus, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, Gram-positive), Escherichia coli (E. coli, Gram-negative), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ( $P A E$ Gramnegative) were employed to assess the antibacterial activity of HpsBMA (HS-A-RP). As shown in Figure S32, the difference in the number of colonies on the agar plates reflected a pronounced antimicrobial effect of $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { P S B M A } }$ (HSA-RP). The colony concentrations and antimicrobial rates of the control and PSBMA hydrogel groups were calculated (Figures S33,34). The number of three kinds of bacteria colonies for HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) samples were 0 with $1 0 0 \%$ antimicrobial rate. Subsequently, two common algae (Amphora sp. and Porphyridium sp.) were used to evaluate the antifouling properties of $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { P S B M A } }$ (HS-A-RP). As shown in Figures S35,36, unlike the control group surfaces covered with many algae, the surface of $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { P S B M A } }$ (HS-A-RP) showed little adhesion of the two algae. Correspondingly, the algae density adsorbed on sample surfaces in the control and hydrogel groups were also calculated. As shown in Figures S37,S38, HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) had significant inhibitory effect on two algae, making the adhesion rate less than $1 \%$ compared with control group. Even after $^ \mathrm { ~ 4 8 ~ h ~ }$ of incubation with the algal suspension, the algae adsorbed on the hydrogel surface was negligible, indicating that HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) had the excellent capacity of antialgal adhesion. Finally, the cut PSBMA hydrogel samples were immersed into dyed vegetable oil and blood respectively to evaluate the multifunctional antifouling performance of $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { P S B M A } }$ (HS-A-RP). As shown in Figures S39,S40, the HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) exhibited low adhesion for dyed vegetable oil, while the blood adhered onto the sample surface can be easily washed away by water. In short, the strong hydration of zwitterion well supported the $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { P S B M A } }$ (HS-A-RP) with remarkable antimicrobial activity and excellent multifunctional antifouling performance. Finally, considering the potential applications of HpsBmA (HSA-RP) hydrogel coating in biomedical, the rat fibroblasts L929 and embryonic cells NIH/3T3 were used as model cells for characterizing the biocompatibility and cytotoxicity of our hydrogel samples. As shown in Figure S41, the hydrogel samples exhibited relatively low cytotoxicity (cell viability of more than $7 5 \%$ in all cases) at different incubation time. These characteristics are extremely important for extending the potential applications of PZHs in the biomedical field.

The bulk polymerization process for preparing hydrogels via traditional methods usually releases a large amount of heat triggering the burst polymerization,[36] which commonly results in uncontrolled polymerization. Therefore, the thermal effect during polymerization of HS-A-RP was investigated by infrared camera (Figure S42). The heat-flow generated during the whole polymerization after $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ was ignorable, similar to the room temperature. This indicated that the HSA-RP method is a mild, efficient, and controllable reaction. This feature allows HS-A-RP to not only manufacture polyzwitterionic hydrogel coating on demand, but also to in situ encapsulate thermal-sensitive materials during the preparing process. As shown in Figure S43, the paraffin particles within the network of the formed HpsBMA (HS-ARP) can well remain the initial particles state in the precursor solution. Moreover, based on fast gelation feature of HSA-RP method, some heavy particles with high mass density can also be in situ encapsulated and dispersed uniformly within the network. As shown in Figure S44, the natural black sand particles deposited at the bottom of the SBMA hydrogel precursor solution before stirring. After HS-ARP reaction with stirring, the black sand particles were distributed uniformly in the solution. Finally, the heavy black sand particles were dispersed and immobilized within the PSBMA hydrogel after implementing HS-A-RP for $3 \ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ , which indicated that HS-A-RP method could be used to fix loose soil or sand. Typically, industrial coarse sand grains were chosen as the model for sand solidification and placed into the mold (Figure 4h (i) to (ii)). Then, the stained SBMA hydrogel precursor was pouring into the mold (Figure 4h (iii)). Finally, most of the sand grains were encapsulated (Figure 4h (iv)). After implementing gelation of $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ by HS-A-RP (Figure 4h (v)), sand particles were immobilized inside the hydrogel without loose sand falling off (Figure 4h (vi)). As expected, this excellent sand fixation ability of HS-A-RP can be expanded to many potential applications fields.

Finally, the scalable manufacture capacity of polyzwitterionic hydrogel coating for HS-A-RP method was investigated. First, the feasibility of large-scale manufacture of hydrogel coating via HS-A-RP method was evaluated through a home-made roll-to-roll equipment (Figure 4i (i)). Typically, a commercially available cotton thread was utilized as model material for in situ growing PSBMA hydrogel coating by HS-A-RP. The cotton thread was first slowly immersed into the catalyst cell to adsorb enough silver ions $\left( \mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } ^ { + } \right)$ , and then conveyed into the dyed monomer cell (SBMA hydrogel precursor solution) to make the SBMA monomers attach to the cotton thread and start to polymerize. Finally, the HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) modified cotton thread was obtained through wounding up the cotton thread. Compared with unmodified cotton thread (Figure 4i (ii), there was a thin dyed HpsBMA (HS-A-RP) hydrogel layer attached onto the modified sample surface (Figure 4i (ii)). Cross-sectional morphology of modified cotton thread was observed by SEM and optical microscope. As shown in Figure 4i (iv) and (v), the PSBMA hydrogel was uniformly combined with the perimeter of a single cotton fiber, demonstrating that the PZHs can form a tight interface with the fiber material. Moreover, based on the low temperature-tolerance of HS-A-RP method, largescale preparation of polyzwitterionic hydrogel coating was available on the surface of the cold metal substrate at $0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$

Typically, the commercially available stainless-steel plate (30 $\times ~ 3 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } )$ was pretreated in a freezer at low temperature to obtain the cold metal substrate (Figure 4j (i), (i)). Subsequently, the well-mixed PSBMA hydrogel precursor solution was loaded into the chamber by a spray gun, and then the precursor solution was uniformly painted on the substrate surface (Figure 4j (ii)). After implementing gelation of $1 5 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ by HS-A-RP, a uniform hydrogel coating covering an area of $9 0 0 \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ was successfully manufactured on the cold metal substrate surface at $0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ (Figure 4j (iv)). This significant advantage of HS-A-RP method is impossible to be achieved for conventional spraying-rehydration coating technology.[37,38]

# Conclusions

In summary, hydration-shielding accelerated self-catalytic polymerization method (HS-A-RP) was proposed to realize facile and scalable synthesis of polyzwitterionic hydrogels. We found that polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) chains could generate a hydration shield around hydrated zwitterionic monomers, promoting effective aggregation and rapid polymerization under the assistance of silver ions $\left( \mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } ^ { + } \right)$ -potassium persulfate $( \mathsf { S } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - } )$ redox catalyst. The suggested reaction mechanisms were proved by DFT, TEM, DLS, EPR, and XPS. HSA-RP overcame the concentration, time, and temperature limitations during the gelation process for preparing polyzwitterionic hydrogels. Typically, compared with the traditional HEAT and UV methods, HS-A-RP could realize the ultrafast $\left( < 6 0 ~ \mathrm { s } \right)$ and facile preparation of polyzwitterionic hydrogels at extremely low monomer mass concentration $( 0 . 3 \ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ within a wide range of temperature tolerance ( $- 5$ to $3 7 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ without relying on any external energy input. Based on the hydration shielding mechanism, the as-prepared polyzwitterionic hydrogels exhibited denser network structure, better mechanical properties, and antiswelling behavior, than those prepared via traditional HEAT and UV methods. At the same time, the as-prepared polyzwitterionic hydrogels also showed excellent lubrication, antibacterial and antifouling performances, and presented considerable application potential as functional coating of biomedical device. The low-heat flow generation in polymerization endows HS-A-RP with unique ability to realize in situ encapsulation of thermal-sensitive materials within network of polyzwitterionic hydrogels. The fast gelation process enables HS-A-RP to have excellent sand fixation ability. Importantly, HS-A-RP is applicable to scalable preparation of polyzwitterionic hydrogel coating on surface of diverse substrates and extreme environment condition. We firmly believe this method would open a new door for chemistry synthesis of polyzwitterionic hydrogels, and greatly expands its applications scenarios.
