# . Introduction

The discharge of antibiotic drugs into natural waters due to their widespread use in medical therapy has sparked serious global concerns. This is because most conventional biological methods, such as activated sludge, normally fail to efficiently remove them from sewage and industrial effluents (Zhou et al., 2021). Among them, ciprofloxacin (CIP), a fluoroquinolone antibiotic, is widely used to cure a wide range of microbial diseases in both animals and humans. In sewage effluents, CIP can be found in concentrations ranging from 50 to $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ (Kim et al., 2020). However, much higher concentrations of this antibiotic can be found in the effluents of pharmaceutical industries (e.g., $5 0 \mathrm { \ m g / L }$ (Sarkhosh et al., 2019)) and those originating from hospitals (e.g., 13 $g / \mathrm { L }$ in Spain (Danner et al., 2019)). Other types of pharmaceuticals can also be found in surface and ground waters. For instance, sulfamethoxazole (SMx) is a sulfonamide antibiotic that is widely used for the treatment of bacterial infections in humans, including urinary tract infections, bronchitis, and prostatitis (Wang and Wang, 2018). This pharmaceutical has been detected in concentrations ranging from 30 to $5 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ (Avramiotis et al., 2021). It has also been discussed in the literature that the migration and transformation of these antibiotic compounds in water bodies can result in the formation of toxic intermediates with the potential to pose a serious threat to both ecosystem safety and public health (Kang et al., 2020). Moreover, antibiotic compounds can exert selective pressure on microbes, resulting in antibiotic-resistant bacteria in water bodies (Michael-Kordatou et al., 2018).

In addition to antibiotics, natural waters can be polluted by other types of pollutants, such as dyes. For instance, methylene blue (MB), a highly toxic and carcinogenic phenothiazine derivative, is widely used in textile dyeing and is detected in wastewater from a wide range of sources (Vasiljevic et al. n.d.). Hence, it is critical to develop wastewater treatment techniques that can deal with a wide range of environmental pollutants, such as antibiotics and dyes, before being discharged into natural waters. To address this need, various advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have been developed in recent years (Pan et al., 2022). The basis of these methods is the generation of oxidative agents (such as •OH, $\bullet \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { - }$ , and $S O _ { 4 } { \bullet } \widetilde { \ }$ ), which can strongly attack and decompose a wide range of complex organic compounds. Among AOPs, the activation of oxidation agents (such as persulfate, peroxymonosulfate, periodate, and chlorine) using various physical or chemical techniques has received much interest in recent years (Bu et al., 2022; Han et al., 2020).

Periodate (PI, $\mathrm { I O } _ { 4 } ^ { - } .$ ) is a relatively novel oxidation agent that has recently been used for the degradation of various types of recalcitrant organic contaminants. It has some advantages over other oxidation agents, such as being stable and easy to ship and store (Zong et al., 2021). PI with a reduction potential of $+ 1 . 6 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ is able to produce a plethora of different radicals and highly reactive substances, such as $\mathrm { I O } _ { 3 } { \bullet }$ , $\mathrm { I O } _ { 4 } \bullet$ , ${ \mathrm { O } } ( { } ^ { 3 } { \mathbf { P } } )$ ,OH, and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ (Lee et al., 2016), when activated with different techniques, such as UV light (Zhang et al., 2021), ultrasound (Hamdaoui and Merouani, 2017), microwaves (Mohammadi et al., 2016) and freezing (Choi et al., 2018). However, the need for an external source of energy for most of these techniques has restricted their wider application (He et al., 2022). PI can also be activated by chemicals, such as hydrogen peroxide (Chadi et al., 2019) and hydroxylamine (Li et al., 2016). Additionally, there is an interest in the application of carbonaceous materials (such as activated carbon (Li et al., 2016)) or transition metals and metal oxides as catalysts for the generation of powerful oxidation agents from PI, with advantages such as low cost, energy savings and ease of use (Du et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020).

Copper oxide (CuO) is one of the most popular transition metal oxides, widely used in AOPs, such as the photocatalytic degradation of organic compounds (M.Li et al., 2022), and very recently used for the activation of oxidation agents (OAs), such as persulfate (PS) and peroxymonosulfate (PMS) (J. Yang et al., 2022)' to generate oxidative radicals. However, to the best of our knowledge, it has not been used for the activation of novel OAs, such as PI. Copper is a cheap, eco-friendly, and abundant element in nature that is present in the Earth's crust at concentrations ranging from $1 4 6 ~ \mathrm { g / t }$ to $6 0 ~ \mathrm { g / t }$ (Rajput et al., 2020). Furthermore, copper oxide nanomaterials normally have a large specific surface area and porous structure, which makes them ideal candidates for surface oxidative reactions (Baylan et al., 2020; El-Trass et al., 2012).

In this study, a facile precipitation method was employed to prepare CuO nanomaterials for the activation of PI. The prepared materials were used in a novel CuO/PI activation system for the degradation of pharmaceuticals, including CIP and sulfamethoxazole (SMX). The efficiency of the system was also studied for the degradation of methylene blue (MB) as a model dye pollutant. The effects of the reaction conditions, including the PI concentration, pollutant concentration, catalyst dosage, initial pH, and water matrices, on the performance of the system were critically discussed. Scavenging experiments were also performed to identify the dominant oxidative radicals, followed by analysis on a UHPLC Q-TOF MS system to identify the degradation mechanisms and pathways. Furthermore, respirometric tests were performed to assess the environmental quality of the treated effluents. Respirometry is commonly used in wastewater treatment research to characterize wastewater streams and determine their toxic/inhibitory effects on biomass (Mainardis et al., 2021). The presence of toxic compounds can cause inhibitory effects on the biomass and toxicity to aerobic biological treatment systems. As a result, the respiration rate or specific oxygen uptake rate (SOUR) of activated sludge (measured by the respiratory assay) will be reduced. Therefore, respirometric tests can be performed to assess the degree of detoxification of wastewater treatment systems, for example, before and after treatment.

A facile precipitation method was used for the synthesis of CuO nanomaterials. # 2.3. Experimental procedures

The removal of CIP was investigated in a $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ glass Erlenmeyer flask containing a $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ reaction solution. The initial pH of the solution was adjusted to the required pH (310) with diluted $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } S 0 _ { 4 }$ and NaOH solutions. Because only a small volume was required for the pH adjustment, the volume increase of the reaction volume was negligible. During the reaction, a magnetic stir bar was used to constantly stir the reaction medium. The preferred concentration of PI and the dosage of CuO were mixed in a conical flask to start the reaction. Liquid $\left( 1 . 5 \mathrm { m L } \right)$ was collected with a syringe at specific time intervals and instantly filtered by a $0 . 4 5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ membrane filter into a clear tube. To halt the oxidation reactions, $1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of the collected sample was mixed with 50 $\mu \mathrm { L }$ of $\mathrm { N a _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ (0.1 M). After that, the solution was analyzed with HPLC to determine the concentration of the remaining CIP.

# 3.2. Degradation of CIP

# 3.2.1. Degradation of CIP and identification of active species in the CuO/PI system

CuO nanoparticles were tested for their catalytic activity as a PI activator for the removal of CIP from aqueous solutions. Fig. 3(a) shows the removal efficiency of $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ CIP under control conditions (i.e., CuO alone or PI alone) and using the $\mathrm { C u O / P I }$ system within $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction. CuO alone removed only $3 7 \%$ of CIP in the absence of PI. Here, it is suggested that adsorption is the main mechanism for the removal of CIP, which is supported by the relatively large BET surface area $( 3 6 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ ${ \bf g } ^ { - 1 } )$ of the material (Yang et al., 2021). A very low degradation efficiency was also observed for PI alone $2 8 \%$ ,within $3 0 \mathrm { m i n } \dot { }$ , which can be attributed to the low oxidation potential of PI. The combination of CuO and PI, on the other hand, exhibited a significant enhancement in CIP removal. The degradation rate reached $5 3 \%$ after 2 min and $9 2 \%$ after $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , with a mineralization rate of $4 4 \%$ . The CIP removal kinetics were subsequently fitted to a pseudo-first-order model to further investigate the catalytic performance of the system (Zhang et al., 2021). The rate constant $( k )$ of the $\mathrm { C u O / P I }$ system was calculated to be 0.11 $\operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , which is much higher than that of CuO alone $( 0 . 0 1 1 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ and PI alone $( 0 . 0 2 1 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ . As shown in Fig. 3(b), the leaching rate of copper in CuO/PI system is less than $0 . 6 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , which is lower than the discharge limit of copper ion in industrial wastewaters set by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US-EPA, $1 . 3 \mathrm { \ m g / L } )$ (Arbabi and Golshani, 2016). The contribution of leached ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ to PI activation was also tested. As we can see from Fig. 3(c), $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ can activate PI and $4 9 \%$ of CIP was removed in $2 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ . After that, the degradation rate remains almost unchanged. The PI activation efficiency of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ is lower than that of CuO with the same molar mass $( 6 . 2 9 \ \mathrm { m M } )$ . We also used $0 . 6 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ $\mathrm { 2 . 0 1 \ m M }$ $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ to simulate the released ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ to activate PI. After $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , the degradation efficiency of CIP reached only $8 . 8 6 \%$ , which was similar to that of PI alone system. It shows that in $\mathrm { { C u O / P I } }$ system, the activation effect on PI can be ignored because the concentration of leached $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ is too low.

Quenching tests were subsequently conducted to determine the main active species involved in the degradation of CIP using the CuO/PI system. For this purpose, hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and ${ \mathrm { O } } ( { } ^ { 3 } { \mathbf { P } } )$ were quenched with 2-propanol, and iodate $\left( \operatorname { I O } _ { 3 } { \cdot } \right)$ , periodate radicals $\left( \mathrm { I O } _ { 4 } { \bullet } \right)$ and •OH were quenched with phenol (Ling et al., 2022). The contribution of these different active species to the degradation of CIP using the CuO/PI system is indicated in Fig. 3(d) and (e). The addition of 2-propanol had almost no effect on the CIP removal efficiency (Fig. 3(d)), implying that •OH and ${ \mathrm { O } } ( { } ^ { 3 } { \mathbf { P } } )$ do not play a role in CIP removal in the CuO/PI system. Because the interference of •OH has been eliminated by 2-propanol, phenol was added to the system to explore the effect of $\mathrm { I O } _ { 3 } { \bullet }$ and $\mathrm { I O } _ { 4 } \bullet$ . The quenching studies indicated that the kinetics of CIP removal decreased by $5 4 \%$ when phenol was added, revealing that $\mathrm { I O } _ { 3 } { \bullet }$ and $\mathrm { I O } _ { 4 } \bullet$ effectively contribute to the removal of CIP in the CuO/PI system. Extra analysis was also performed to exclude the effects of the addition of the scavenger on the adsorption of CIP and the consumption of PI, as shown in Fig. 3(f) and (g). The results indicated that the addition of phenol can inhibit a bit the adsorption of CIP by CuO probably because of occupying the available active area on the surface of CuO. There are also reports in the literature to support such a statement, for instance by Liao et al. (2022). Fig. 3(g) shows that in the presence of phenol, the consumption of PI is almost the same as no phenol added condition. Hence, the addition of phenol has no effect on the consumption of PI in presence of CuO. Although phenol has a little inhibitory effect on the adsorption of CuO to CIP, phenol has a greater inhibitory effect on the degradation of CIP in CuO/PI system. Therefore, it can be concluded that phenol has an inhibitory effect on CIP degradation by quenching iodate and periodate radicals.

The possible mechanisms involved in the degradation of CIP using the PI/CuO system were explored using the results obtained from the scavenging experiments and xPS results. The peaks for the used CuO nanomaterial undergo a slight negative shift of 4eV, revealing that CuO acts as an electron acceptor and $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ valence state decreases. It is speculated that the periodate molecules are first adsorbed onto the CuO surface. # 3.2.2. Influence of process parameters in the CuO/PI system

Fig. 4. (a) presents the effects of an increasing PI concentration on the degradation of CIP. As shown in Fig. 4(a)-4(c), when the periodate concentration increased from $0 . 0 2 \mathrm { m M }$ to $^ { 1 \mathrm { m M } }$ , the removal percentage of the pollutant significantly increased from $2 6 . 3 \%$ to $9 2 . 8 \%$ in $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and the pseudo-first-order kinetic rate constant substantially increased from $0 . { \dot { 0 0 } } 9 2 \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ to $0 . 2 6 \mathrm { ~ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Furthermore, the TOC removal rate considerably increased from $8 . 2 \%$ to $7 6 . 3 \%$ . The reason for this is that under higher initial concentrations of periodate, more active species are formed that can attack and mineralize CIP and its degradation products. This trend has also been reported previously by Li et al. (2016). Additionally, according to Fig. 4(a), although an initial concentration of 1 mM PI resulted in a slightly higher removal after $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ than $0 . 5 \mathrm { m M P I }$ , the same removal efficiencies were observed under both conditions. The two conditions also demonstrated almost the same efficiency for the removal of TOC from the effluents. Therefore, a $[ \mathrm { P I } ] _ { 0 }$ of $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ was selected for further experiments.

The influence of various dosages of CuO (0.25, 0.5, and $1 . 0 \ g / \mathrm { L }$ on the removal of CIP was subsequently evaluated at fixed PI $\mathrm { 7 } 0 . 5 \mathrm { m M }$ and CIP $\left( 0 . 0 6 { \mathrm { ~ m M } } \right)$ concentrations. The results are shown in Fig. 3 d-f. According to these figures, an increasing dosage of CuO had no significant effect on the degradation of CIP (from $0 . 1 1 1 2 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ for $0 . 2 5 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ to $0 . 1 5 7 7 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ for $1 \ g / \mathrm { L } )$ . This result can be due to the high specific surface area of the CuO nanomaterials $( 3 6 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ , which provide high active surface areas even under relatively low dosages of the catalyst. This brings an economic advantage to such oxidation systems, where less catalyst is needed to achieve an acceptable degradation of the pollutant.

Fig. 4(g), (h) and 4(i) represent the influence of the initial concentration of CIP on its degradation kinetics in the studied CuO/PI system. When the initial concentration of CIP increased from 10 to $5 0 \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ , the pseudo-first-order removal rate constant $( k _ { \mathrm { o b s } } )$ dropped from 0.28 to

$0 . 0 7 5 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . These findings are consistent with those reported by $\mathrm { Y u }$ et al. (2020), who found a reduction in $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ as the initial concentration of the pollutant (benzalkonium chloride) increased in the medium. This is attributed to the fact that under identical operating conditions, the concentration of active species formed in the medium remains constant, regardless of the initial concentration of the pollutant. A smaller percentage of iodate radicals per mole of CIP is produced under higher initial concentrations of the pollutant. Furthermore, as the initial CIP concentration increases, more degradation products are formed, resulting in competition between the original pollutant and the degradation products for the consumption of the generated active species in the medium.

# 3.2.3. Influence of pH in the CuO/PI system

The degradation of CIP was carried out at various initial pH levels (3, 6 and 10) to better understand how pH affects the removal rate of CIP in the CuO/PI system. The pH was allowed to fluctuate throughout the CuO/PI process since no buffer was used. During the $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ reaction time, no considerable pH variation was observed.

Fig. 5(c) depicts the distribution of the different CIP species at various pH levels. The protonated forms of CIP $\mathrm { C I P ^ { 3 + } }$ and $\mathrm { C I P ^ { 2 + } } )$ are dominant under acidic conditions $\mathrm { ( p H } < 3 \mathrm { ) }$ . At $\mathtt { p H } 6$ , various forms of CIP, including $\mathrm { C I P ^ { 1 + } }$ , $\mathrm { C I P } ^ { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { C I P ^ { 2 + } }$ , are present, and under alkaline conditions $\mathrm { ( p H > 1 0 ) }$ , CIP is mostly found in its deprotonated form $( \mathrm { C I P ^ { 1 - } } )$ . The $\mathrm { C u O / P I }$ degradation experiments were found to be highly affected by variations in the CIP ionization state under different pH values. At $\mathrm { p H } 3$ ,the calculated $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ for $\mathrm { C u O / P I }$ was $0 . 0 8 8 \ \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . The pseudo-first-order degradation rate $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ increased to $0 . 1 1 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ when the pH increased to 6. Wei et al. demonstrated that the deprotonation of the amine groups on the piperazine ring of CIP enhanced the $\mathsf { e } ^ { - }$ donating behavior of CIP and reduced its steric hindrance (Wei et al., 2013). This can promote the oxidation of the pollutant due to the need for fewer electrons to neutralize and then decompose the CIP molecule (Milh et al., 2021). This is supported by the findings in this study that electron transfer is the main mechanism involved in the decomposition of CIP (Fig. 3(c)) (Clennan and Liao, 2008). As the pH increased from 6 to 10, the degradation constant of CIP dropped considerably from $0 . 1 1 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ to $0 . 0 2 4 \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Additionally, the TOC removal rate under this condition decreased from $7 2 . 7 0 \%$ to $0 . 3 8 \%$ These findings indicate that the significant decrease in $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ for the $\mathrm { C u O / P I }$ system at $\mathtt { p H } 1 0$ is due to the lower affinity for catalysis of the ionized CIP form at $\mathsf { p H 1 0 }$ The zeta potential analysis of the synthetic CuO nanoparticles is shown in Fig. 5 (e). According to the results obtained, the CuO/PI system has an excellent catalytic efficiency to deal with various types of organic pollutants and hence has the potential to be used to remove pollutants in water.

# 4. Conclusion

In the present manuscript, CuO nanomaterials were synthesized by using a facile precipitation method and characterized for the first time in a CuO/PI oxidation system for the degradation of model organic pollutants from various water matrices. In a 30-min reaction time, $9 2 \%$ of CIP was degraded under ideal operating conditions (i.e., $[ \mathrm { C u O } ] = 0 . 5 \ : g /$ L, $[ \mathrm { C I P } ] _ { \circ } = 2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ $( 0 . 0 6 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ , $[ \mathrm { P I } ] _ { \circ } = 0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ , $\mathrm { p H } = 6 \mathrm { , }$ , which demonstrates the high efficiency of the developed method for such applications. Radical $\mathrm { ( I O _ { 3 } }$ and $\mathrm { I O } _ { 4 } { \cdot }$ ) and nonradical (electron transfer) pathways were both identified to have a significant contribution to CIP removal, with the latter being the most prevalent. The main degradation routes of CIP were identified as piperazine ring breakage and fluorine substitution. The CuO/PI system can also effectively treat real wastewater and different types of pollutants (SMX, CIP and MB), indicating that this system is a potential candidate to treat real wastewater in the future. The results of the respirometry experiments revealed that the toxicity of the water treated by the CuO/PI system decreased considerably, indicating that this system is environmentally friendly. This research offers new possibilities for catalytic oxidation using costeffective CuO nanomaterials and expands the use of PI-based AOPs in the treatment of recalcitrant organic contaminants.
