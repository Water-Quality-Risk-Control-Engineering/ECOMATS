# A B S T R A C T

This work systematically evaluates the application of disc-milled iron‑copper (FeCu) bimetals for activating peroxydisulfate (PDS) and peroxymonosulfate (PMS), providing insights into both systems behavior. 4-chlorophenol (4-CP) was chosen as the model pollutant to evaluate the performance of bimetals compared to traditional iron-based activators $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } )$ ). The influence of different factors was addressed, including i) bimetals composition (0, 5, and $5 0 \%$ Cu content), ii) disc-milling, and iii) bimetals concentration. FeCu bimetal with $5 \%$ Cu content showed a better synergy when combined with PMS, resulting in the highest 4-CP degradation, dechlorination, and mineralization $9 2 \%$ , $6 2 ~ \%$ , and $6 0 ~ \%$ , respectively, within $1 2 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ ). Different quenchers and PMSO were used to identify the main reactive species in the bimetallic systems, showing the production of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ with both oxidants and a relevant contribution of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ in the case of PMS. Finally, practical considerations for implementing the FeCu systems have been carefully evaluated, including the potential secondary effects on water quality due to iron and copper release, as well as the stability and reusability of these activators. Adjusting the final $\mathsf { p H }$ to 8.0 effectively precipitated dissolved metals, ensuring compliance with Italian environmental quality standards $( \mathrm { F e } \le 2 \ : \mathrm { m g / L }$ and $\mathrm { C u } \leq 0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m g / L } )$ . Moreover, Fe5Cu bimetals have shown outstanding stability in repeated uses. Thus, this study introduces a novel, and eco-friendly approach for synthesizing active FeCu bimetals capable of efficiently activating oxidants, offering a promising solution for environmental pollution remediation. While optimal treatment conditions are thoroughly discussed, further studies are needed for fullscale application.

# 1. Introduction

In recent years, developing effective and green techniques for managing persistent organic pollutants (POPs) has become a critical concern due to their persistent nature, potential to bioaccumulate, and harmful impacts on human health and the environment [1]. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are considered promising technologies for $\mathrm { P O P } s$ treatment as they can completely mineralize or convert them into nontoxic intermediates [2–6]. Among different oxidants, peroxydisulfate $( S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - }$ , PDS) and peroxymonosulfate $\mathrm { ( H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ , PMS) have recently attracted particular interest from the scientific community. These oxidants can be activated by different methods to produce highly reactive oxygen species (Table SM1 in the Supplementary Materials), such as hydroxyl (OH• ) and sulfate $( \mathsf { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet } )$ radicals (Eqs. (1) and $( 2 )$ for PDS, and

Eqs. (3) and (4) for PMS) [7–9]. In addition, other oxidant species are often involved, which may be radical (e.g., superoxide radicals $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ ) or non-radical (e.g., singlet oxygen $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ ) [10–14].

Among the different methods for PDS and PMS activation, those relying on iron materials have gained particular interest due to iron high reactivity, non-toxicity, and cost-effectiveness [15–18]. Homogeneous activators, such as $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ salts, have been extensively tested [9]. The activation mechanism is based on the oxidation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 \bar { + } }$ by PDS and PMS (Eqs. (5) and (6), respectively) [9,17]. These reactions are chemically favored, leading to rapid sulfate radicals and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ generation. However, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ is less effective than $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ in generating radicals and could also precipitate due to its narrower working $\mathtt { p H }$ range and lower solubility [8,17,19]. In addition, the homogeneous Fe activation can lead to a relevant sulfate radical scavenging and consequent unproductive oxidant consumption (Eq. (7)) [17,20,21].

Zero-valent iron powder $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } )$ has been used as a heterogeneous activator to overcome these limitations [15–17,22,23]. $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ can slowly release $\mathrm { F e ^ { 2 + } }$ in solution, limiting the sulfate radicals scavenging, and regenerating $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ (Eq. (8)) [17,23]. However, the activation by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ may suffer from the passivation of the surface due to the generation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ oxides, which decreases the activation efficiency [17,23–25]. Different solutions have been developed to reduce the passivation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , such as pretreatments, modifications (e.g., $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ sulfidation), or using porous materials as support (e.g., carbon and zeolite) [17,23,26].

Among the numerous enhancements applied to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , the addition of a secondary metal for synthesizing iron-based bimetallic particles has been recently proposed [15,17,23]. The presence of a secondary metal (typically a transition metal such as Cu, Ni, Ag, and Co) can (i) directly activate the oxidant, (ii) promote the formation of galvanic couples that may accelerate the electrochemical corrosion of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , (iii) improve the surface roughness and reactivity of the particles, and (iv) retard the precipitation of corrosion products on the surface, sustaining in this way the oxidant activation [17].

In this context, there has been recently a growing interest in the application of different iron-based bimetals to activate oxidants such as PDS [27,28] and PMS [29] for the treatment of POPs in water. However, it is worth pointing out that there are some gaps in the existing literature. Firstly, some research studies have evaluated the effects of adding multiple metals simultaneously to activate PDS [30] or PMS [31,32], but relatively few studies have been found in the literature that evaluate the synthesis of bimetals. Table SM2 summarizes the most relevant information of these studies, highlighting the type of Fe-based bimetal studied, the synthesis method employed, the pollutants, oxidant, and reaction conditions used, and the degradation rate of contaminants at the optimal conditions selected. As shown in Table SM2, iron-based bimetallic activators have been typically synthesized by chemical methods (e.g., replacement reaction) [28,33–36] that usually require harmful solvents and long operating times [37–39]. Thus, more environmentally friendly catalyst preparation methods are urgently needed [40], as well as the verification of its application in the activation of various oxidants. Additionally, the Fe-based bimetals activating these two oxidants were mostly tested separately [18,41], thus not allowing for a fair comparison of their performance under the same operating conditions or using the same activation method. As previously mentioned, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , $\mathrm { o H } ^ { \bullet }$ , $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } , 0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , etc. are all produced in the iron-based catalyst/oxidant system to varying degrees. Thus, it needs to be confirmed whether there is production of these reactive species in each iron-based catalyst/oxidant system [40]. Finally, the environmental application of Fe-based catalyst systems should be carefully evaluated, particularly regarding the problem of metal leaching and stability of the activator, aspects insufficiently evaluated in the literature on metalactivated oxidizing systems [17,42]. In this study, we systematically evaluate the effectiveness of disc-milled FeCu bimetals as innovative activators of both PDS and PMS for the degradation of POPs in water. For this purpose, FeCu bimetals were synthesized through disc milling, considered an environmentally friendly catalyst preparation method [40]. The performance of both PDS and PMS activated by disc-milled FeCu bimetals was assessed and compared to the performance of more traditional Fe-based activators (i.e., $\bar { \mathrm { F e } } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 \cdot }$ ). 4-chlorophenol (4- CP) was chosen as model compound because of its widespread use in the chemical industry, synthesis of pharmaceutical products, and as a selective solvent in refining mineral oils [43]. Additionally, it presents high stability and resistance to biological treatment [44,45]. The extent of pollutant dechlorination, mineralization, and oxidant consumption were evaluated and used as key performance indicators. The main reactive species generated in FeCu bimetals systems were identified through quenching tests and their role in the reaction was assessed, with specific reference to $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ species. Specific tests were also carried out to address the effect of key operating conditions, such as the Cu content in the bimetal and the concentration of the bimetallic activator. Ultimately, the stability, metals leaching from the bimetal activators, and the possible post-treatment aimed at mitigating this secondary effect were also evaluated, giving some practical information for a fullscale application.

# 2.2. Experimental procedure

# 2.2.1. Activators preparation

FeCu bimetals were prepared using the same methodology reported by Settimi et al. [47,48]. Namely, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ powders were mixed and milled for 1 h at $7 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ using a disc mill (Retsch RS 200, Retsch, Haan, Germany) to produce iron-based bimetals containing $1 \%$ , $5 \%$ , and $5 0 \%$ by weight (%wt) of copper as secondary metal (named Fe1Cu, Fe5Cu, and $\mathtt { F e 5 0 C u }$ , respectively). $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ was used without being previously disc milled in all the experiments, except in those tests performed for evaluating the effect of the disc milling. $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ , and FeCu bimetals were always pretreated by acidic washing, applying the procedure described by Zingaretti et al. [46] to remove oxides that may have formed from the exposure to air during their storage. After acidic washing and drying under continuous $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ flow, the activators were stored in a glass vial and placed in an $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ -rich environment to prevent oxide formation before using them in the oxidation tests.

# 2.2.2. Oxidation tests

Batch tests were carried out in Teflon FEP vials with ETFE screw caps. Firstly, 4-CP solution was added to the vials and mixed with the activator. After that, the oxidant was added to reach a final volume of 40 mL and start the reaction. Vials were then placed at room temperature and constant rotation speed $( 8 0 \mathrm { r p m } )$ for the required time. Several vials were prepared for each run to follow the reaction evolution over time and two vials were sacrificed at set time intervals (i.e., 2, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, $1 2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ ) to measure in duplicate the oxidants and 4-CP concentrations. All the tests were performed using DI water (Zeener Power II purification system).

Different experiments were performed to assess the effect of the operating conditions and the oxidizing system on 4-CP removal. The experimental conditions adopted in each run are listed in Table 1 in terms of the objective of the specific test, the type and concentration of the oxidant, the type and dosage of the activator, and the code used in the discussion of the results. The initial concentration of 4-CP was fixed at $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ $\mathrm { 0 . 1 5 6 ~ m M } )$ , a value commonly used in literature [49–51]. Most of the tests were performed using an oxidant dosage of $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ , which corresponds to 0.25 times the stoichiometric dosage required for complete 4-CP mineralization (calculated assuming 4-CP reacting with sulfate radicals). A second set of tests was then performed to identify the main reactive species generated in the case of PDS and PMS activation by Fe5Cu. Based on the procedure already published in the literature [13,27], methanol (MeOH) was used as scavenger for both hydroxyl radicals OH• $( \mathbf { k } \sim 1 0 ^ { 8 }$ $\mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } \thinspace \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ and sulfate radicals $\mathsf { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } ( \mathbf k \sim 1 0 ^ { 7 } \mathbf M ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ , while tert-butanol (TBA) was used for $\mathrm { O H } ^ { \bullet } ( \mathbf { k } \sim 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ [13,27] and chloroform (CF) was used as scavenger for superoxide radicals $0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ $( \mathbf { k } \sim 1 0 ^ { 1 0 } \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ [13,31]. Sodium azide (SA) was used as scavenger for singlet oxygen $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ $( \mathbf { k } \sim 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ , $\mathbf { S } 0 _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } ( \mathbf { k } \sim 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ and OH• $( \mathbf { k } \sim 1 0 ^ { 1 0 } \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ [52,53]. Scavengers were added to the system before the addition of the oxidant. The scavenger:oxidant molar ratios used were selected based on previously published papers [13,31,52] and were equal to 500:1 for both MeOH and TBA, 50:1 for CF and 10:1 for SA. PMSO was also used as a probe compound to identify the production of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ , according with the method proposed by Wang et al. [54].

Besides, the effect of the Cu content in FeCu bimetals (i.e., $1 \%$ , $5 \% _ { ; }$ and $5 0 \%$ ) on the process performance has been addressed in a specific set of experiments carried out with both oxidants. As reference, some tests were also performed in the absence of Cu, hence using $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ alone as an activator.

To investigate the role of disc milling on the properties of the activator, tests were carried out by using a bimetal with $5 0 \% \mathrm { w t }$ . of Cu $( { \mathrm { F e } } 5 0 { \mathrm { C u } } )$ , and results were compared to those obtained using a mixture of the pristine $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ powders at the same weight percentage without applying disc milling. For comparative purposes, the effect of disc milling was also evaluated by performing tests in which the oxidant was activated by using $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ as it is or disc milled- $\cdot \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ . Additionally, different concentrations of Fe5Cu bimetals (i.e., 0.05, 0.1, and $0 . 2 \ 8 { / \mathrm { L } } )$ were tested with both oxidants, to address the dependence of 4-CP degradation by PMS and PDS on bimetals dosage.

Finally, additional experiments were conducted to evaluate the practical application of Fe5Cu systems. The possibility of mitigating secondary effects of water quality were assessed by performing posttreatment tests, in which NaOH was added to the solutions obtained to achieve a pH of 8.0. Additionally, the stability and reusability of Fe5Cu were evaluated through three successive oxidation cycles. To this purpose, Fe5Cu particles were separated using a magnet at the end of each cycle, washed using UP water, dried, and reused in a new batch of polluted water. XRD measurements were conducted after the treatment, and the total metal ion concentration in the solution was analyzed to assess changes in the morphology of the activator and the extent of metal leaching, respectively.

# 2.3. Analytical methods

4-CP concentration was measured by gas chromatography coupled with flame ionization detector (GC-FID) applying the solid-phase microextraction (SPME) technique. The SPME extractions were performed using an $8 5  { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ polyacrylate fiber (needle size $2 3 \ { \tt g a }$ ) purchased from Supelco (Bellefonte, PA, USA). The SPME fiber was immersed by an automatic sampler (PAL System, AOC-5000plus) in the SPME vial for a fixed extraction time $\mathrm { 3 0 } \mathrm { m i n } )$ at $4 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and then desorbed for $3 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $2 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . The gas chromatograph (Shimadzu GC, GC_QP2010SE) was equipped with a Zebron ZB-5 column $( 3 0 ~ \mathrm { m } \times 0 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m m } ~ \mathrm { I D } \times 0 . 2 5 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ film thickness). The carrier gas used was helium, with a $1 2 . 6 ~ \mathrm { m L / m i n }$ flow rate. The oven temperature was initially held at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , then programmed at $1 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { m i n }$ to $1 8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ which was held for 1 min, and then programmed at $5 ~ { ^ \circ } \mathrm { C } / { \mathrm { m i n } }$ to $2 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . The detector temperature was kept at $2 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . 2-bromophenol was used as the internal standard, and hydrochloric acid was added to fix the $\mathtt { p H }$ to 1 to extract phenolic compounds (with low $\mathrm { { p K } _ { a . } }$ ) by SPME [55].

Methyl phenyl sulfoxide (PMSO) and methyl phenyl sulfone $\mathrm { ( P M S O _ { 2 } ) }$ ) were employed to determine the generation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ . High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) was employed to measure the concentration of PMSO and $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ against time. $7 0 . 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ aliquots were injected into the HPLC instrument (Agilent 1260 Infinity II), equipped with a Poroshell 120C-18 column $( 2 . 7 ~ \mu \mathrm { m } ,$ $4 . 6 \times 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ , a pump $1 2 6 0 \ \mathrm { I n } .$ - finity II Quaternary Pump (G7111B)), a UV detector (1260 Infinity II DAD WR (G7115A) set to 230 and $2 1 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , for PMSO and $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ , respectively) and a thermostatic oven set at ${ } ^ { 3 0 } \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ . The isocratic mobile phase consisted of $7 0 \%$ deionized water (containing $0 . 1 ~ \%$ acetic acid) and $3 0 ~ \%$ acetonitrile at a flow rate of $1 \ \mathrm { m L / m i n }$ . Internal calibration curves were performed in the range of $0 . 0 1 { - } 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { m M }$ .

The concentrations of PDS and PMS were measured with a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Jenway 6715) following the methods developed by Liang et al. [56] and Wacławek et al. [57] The sample was mixed with a solution of KI and $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ for $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . After that, the absorbance of the iodine yellow color was measured at a wavelength of $3 5 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . The pH was determined using a Hanna Instruments $\tt p H 2 1 3$ .

Metal cations (Fe and Cu) in the aqueous solution were quantified using a microwave plasma atomic emission spectrometer $( 4 1 0 0 \ \mathrm { M P } . $ - AES, Agilent Technology) at wavelengths of $3 7 1 . 9 9 \ \mathrm { \ n m }$ (Fe) and $3 2 4 . 7 5 \mathrm { n m }$ (Cu) and nebulizer pressures of ${ \bf 8 0 k P a }$ (Fe) and 140 kPa (Cu), respectively. The presence of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ was determined using the spectrophotometric 1,10-phenanthroline method at $5 1 0 \mathrm { n m }$ , with samples pretreated with ascorbic acid to assess total Fe [58,59]. Quantification was performed using calibration curves generated from corresponding Fe standards ranging from 0.1 to $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ .

The concentration of chloride ions measured using an ion chromatograph (Dionex ICS-1100, Thermo Scientific) coupled with a Dionex IonPac AS22 column $( 2 5 0 \mathrm { m m } \times 4 \mathrm { m m } )$ ) and a conductivity detector. The eluent solution contained $4 . 5 \ : \mathrm { m M } \ : \mathrm { N a C O } _ { 3 }$ and $1 . 4 \mathrm { m M } \mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ at a 1.2 $\mathrm { m L } / \mathrm { m i n }$ flow rate. The TOC concentration was determined with a Shimadzu TOC-V CSH analyzer using synthetic air as a carrier gas and a furnace temperature of $9 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . TOC calibration was performed with phthalate solutions $\left( 0 . 1 { - } 5 0 0 \ \mathrm { m g / L } \right)$ .

The morphology and micro-structure of activators were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (FE-SEM, SUPRA™ 35, Carl Zeiss SMT) equipped with energy X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The crystallographic structure before and after the oxidation treatment was investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (BRUKER D8 Advance Powder) using Bragg-Brentano geometry. XRD signals were collected with diffraction angles ranging from $5 ^ { \circ }$ to $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ of 2θ with a step width of $0 . 0 4 ^ { \circ }$ and a total time of 307 s.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.2. Comparison of iron-based activators

# 3.1. Characterization of zero-valent iron and FeCu bimetals

$\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and Fe5Cu bimetallic activators were characterized using SEMEDS and XRD, with the results summarized in Fig. SM1. Namely, SEM images revealed that $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ particles have micrometric dimensions (Fig. SM1-a), consistent with the specifications of the producer. Additionally, the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ particles exhibited irregular shapes. In the case of discmilled Fe5Cu bimetals, the particle size was not significantly modified (Fig. SM1-b); however, a rougher surface was observed. EDS results have shown a homogeneous distribution of Cu in the activator with a Cu weight percentage of $4 . 7 ~ \%$ , closely matching the theoretical value of 5 $\%$ .

XRD patterns of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ revealed no detectable macro-impurities within the quantification limits (Fig. SM1-c). Similarly, Fe5Cu showed only the Fe(110), Fe(200), and Fe(211) peaks of the iron crystal, indicating that the bimetal is characterized by the same central cubic phase of the body attributed to Fe. Moreover, the absence of peaks associated with Cu suggests that the secondary metal was incorporated into Fe, forming the FeCu bimetallic phase. The characterization results obtained align with those reported in previous work performed by our group on bimetals produced by the same technique [48], proving evidence of the reproducibility of the characteristics of the bimetals synthesized by discmilling.

As shown in Fig. 1, when no activator was added, none of the oxidants significantly degraded 4-CP within $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ , thus, confirming that direct oxidation is not a viable option for contaminant removal, as already reported by Lee et al. [9].

In the case of activation by $\mathrm { F e ^ { 2 + } }$ , 4-CP removal observed after 120 min with both PDS and PMS was very low $( < 2 0 ~ \% )$ , despite a high consumption of oxidants was observed (Fig. 1). This suggests that unproductive reactions probably occurred due to excess $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ in solution, leading to relevant scavenging of free radicals [17,20,21]. Better performances were obtained for both oxidants using heterogeneous activators. When $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ was used, 4-CP removal increased up to $9 5 \%$ and $6 3 \%$ using PDS and PMS, respectively. Additionally, PDS and PMS consumption rates resulted slower than with $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ (Fig. 1-b and -d, respectively).

In the case of activation with Fe5Cu bimetals, the 4-CP degradation kinetic was fast and reached a plateau after short reaction times. Accordingly, the oxidant showed a faster consumption kinetics, being completely consumed within $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . However, compared to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , 4-CP degradation at the final reaction time was only enhanced in the PMS/ Fe5Cu system (Fig. 1-c) whereas in the case of PDS, 4-CP degradation decreased by $1 8 ~ \%$ (Fig. 1-a).

The extent of 4-CP dechlorination and mineralization (estimated based on the chloride and TOC measurements according to Eqs. (11) and (12), respectively) using heterogeneous activators $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and FeCu) is reported in Fig. 2, where also 4-CP degradation results are shown.

4-CP dechlorination and mineralization in heterogenous systems showed similar trends to those reported for 4-CP removal (Fig. 2). However, the extent of dechlorination degree was smaller compared to the pollutant degradation, because of the formation of chlorinated intermediate compounds which could not be further oxidized, as tests were carried out at sub-stoichiometric conditions. At initial reaction times, a higher dechlorination degree was observed in comparison with the mineralization one (especially visible in the PMS/Fe5Cu system), while the two indicators converged to close values for higher reaction times. This behavior can be attributed to the lower carbon-chloride bond energy with respect to the carbon‑oxygen one, so that the chlorine on the aromatic ring was removed more quickly [60].

To better address the oxidation mechanism in the case of heterogeneous activation, the concentration of iron and copper solubilized during the reaction with $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and FeCu bimetals was measured (Fig. SM2). A progressive iron release was observed with both activators, and the iron concentration was higher when PDS was used with both $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and FeCu bimetals. This could be explained by the higher standard reduction potential of PDS (i.e., $2 . 1 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { N H E } } )$ ) compared to PMS (i.e., $1 . 8 2 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { N H E } } )$ [61], which may have favored iron. Additionally, when using FeCu bimetals, the iron concentration was further increased, and the dissolution proceeded faster than in the case of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , suggesting that the presence of copper in the activator accelerated the iron dissolution. Xiao et al. [17] reported that the presence of Cu in a multimetallic activator can increase the electrochemical corrosion of iron because of the formation of galvanic cells. In this regard, Ji et al. [28] already documented that in the case of PDS activated by a FeCu bimetal, iron corrosion was facilitated. Consequently, the greater availability of dissolved iron may have increased the rate of oxidant consumption, as shown in Fig. 1. However, it is noted that the faster dissolution of iron led to different contaminant removal rates, and Fe5Cu bimetal appeared to be more effective in enhancing 4-CP removal in combination with PMS rather than PDS. In this regard, it is proposed that when PDS was activated by Fe5Cu, the excessive availability of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ in the system may have contributed to reducing the final contaminant degradation with respect to the case of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ due to Eq. (7), while in the PMS system the slower iron dissolution increased the oxidant efficiency. Additionally, the presence of Cu ions leached from the Fe5Cu surface may explain the better performance of PMS compared to PDS with this activator, as it has already been shown that these ions could activate PMS more efficiently than PDS [62]. Similarly, Zhou et al. [63] observed that the 2,4-dichlorophenol removal decreased activating PDS with $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ rather than $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , whereas it increased if PMS was used as oxidant. They also reported that acidic conditions and the presence of PDS and PMS can accelerate the corrosion of $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ to $\mathrm { C u ^ { + } }$ and ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ ions. To further clarify this aspect, the effect of leached Fe and Cu ions on 4-CP degradation was evaluated through additional homogeneous experiments. To this purpose, homogeneous experiments were carried out using iron and copper concentrations equal to the representative average values of both ions previously measured in the heterogeneous PDS/Fe5Cu and PMS/Fe5Cu systems $( \mathrm { C _ { F e } } = 5 0 \ : \mathrm { m g / L }$ and $\mathrm { C _ { C u } } = 2 \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L }$ , as shown in Fig. SM2). The results from the homogeneous systems $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + } )$ , compared to the heterogeneous ones (Fe5Cu), are summarized in Fig. SM3. It can be observed that a significantly lower 4- CP degradation was obtained in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ systems in compared to the Fe5Cu ones, with a greater difference observed in the case of experiments carried out with PDS. The higher efficiency of the heterogeneous systems can be explained by: (i) the progressive $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ dissolution, which leads to reduced unproductive oxidant consumption [17,20,21], (ii) the generation of a $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ cycle in the presence of heterogeneous activators [17,64], providing more persistent reactivity for oxidant activation, and (iii) the direct oxidant activation on the Fe5Cu surface via electron transfer [17,64]. Additionally, the efficiency of the treatment is highly influenced by the form and valence state of iron and copper. The $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ presence in the Fe5Cu systems is summarized in Fig. SM4. As shown, the main Fe species present in both systems was $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ . In the same line Fang et al. [27] recently reported that the main Fe and Cu species in the FeCu/PDS systems are $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ , respectively. $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ is generated in the FeCu system, but not in the homogeneous $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } /$ ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ system [27]. As the presence of $\mathrm { C u ^ { + } }$ is of particular importance on enhancing oxidant activation and therefore $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\mathrm { o H } ^ { \bullet }$ generation [65], this could also justify the higher synergetic efficiency of the Fe5Cu system.

Overall, when bimetallic activators were used, the PMS system appeared to be more selective toward the contaminant degradation, since the fast consumption of oxidant resulted in a higher 4-CP degradation and mineralization, while using PDS appeared more unproductive. The higher efficiency of the PMS/FeCu system compared to other oxidant combinations has been recently reported, achieving complete sulfamethoxazole (SMX) degradation in $8 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ [66]. In the same line, the degradation rate of 4-CP obtained under the conditions selected in this study was found to be comparable to, or even higher than, the results reported in the literature for the degradation of organic pollutants by other Fe-based bimetallic systems (as shown in Table SM2). This suggests that disc-milled bimetals are promising activators in the field of sulfate radical-based AOPs, as their synthesis is more scalable, requires less time and fewer chemical reagents, and demonstrate high efficiency.

# 3.3. Main reactive species

In addition to the effect of dissolved metals on treatment efficiency, the generation of different radical species in each system may play an important role. Different studies have reported that in the case of PDS and PMS activation by $ { \mathrm { F e } } ^ { 0 }$ , both $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\mathrm { O H } ^ { \bullet }$ participate as dominant species in the degradation of different organic contaminants [18,67], including 4-CP [51]. However, since it has been demonstrated that different radical and non-radical species may be present in the field of heterogeneous activation of PDS and PMS, quenching experiments were performed to identify the main reactive species involved in the oxidizing systems activated by Fe5Cu bimetals, and the results obtained are shown in Fig. 3. Firstly, the role of radical species was assessed. 4-CP degradation with both PDS and PMS was significantly reduced by the addition of MeOH and TBA revealing that $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\mathrm { o H } ^ { \bullet }$ radicals may be present in the systems. $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals can be produced directly through the breakage of the peroxide bond (Eqs. (1) and (2)), while the formation of OH• radicals is typically promoted by alkaline pH [12,68]. However, several studies showed that they could also play a minor role in acidic or neutral conditions [15,18,27,69], through the reaction shown in Eq. (13) [18,23].

$$
\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } + H _ { 2 } O {  } O H ^ { \bullet } + } S \mathrm { O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } + H ^ { + } }
$$

In the same time interval $( 1 5 { - } 1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n } )$ ), the addition of CF entailed a further reduction in 4-CP removal in the PDS system, showing that $0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ may also be present. Similarly, Zhang et al. [70] used a FeCu bimetallic sulfide mineral to activate PDS and found $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , $\mathrm { o H } ^ { \bullet }$ , and $0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ in the system. These authors proposed that the production of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ can be related to $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ activation by $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ ions leached from the activator surface (Eq. (14)):

$$
\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } + \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C u } ^ { + } {  } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } + \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }
$$

In the PMS/Fe5Cu system, 4-CP removal was not significantly affected by CF, thus suggesting that the effect of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals was negligible. Nevertheless, none of these quenchers could completely stop 4-CP oxidation. Thus, it can be argued that non-radical pathways may play a key role in the degradation of 4-CP using bimetals. According to Ding et al. [11], the typical non-radical pathways for PDS and PMS activated by metal-based activators are: (i) surface activation of the oxidant, (ii) electron transfer, (iii) singlet oxygen, and (iv) high-valent metals. However, it was previously observed in Fig. 1 that electron transfer by direct oxidation proceeds too slowly, while the surfaceactivated oxidant pathway has been reported mainly for metal oxides and carbomaterials [11]. Thus, it can be proposed that in the case of activation by Fe5Cu, 4-CP degradation may occur through a combination of radical and non-radical species as $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ or high-valent metals [10,11,13]. To verify the presence of non-radical species, tests with SA and PMSO were performed. Interestingly, SA almost completely quenched the reaction in both systems, suggesting that $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ plays a key role in the reaction. Recently, it has been reported that $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ may be generated with both PDS and PMS through different mechanisms such as the disproportionation of $0 \mathbf { \dot { 2 } } ^ { - }$ and $S O _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals or PMS selfdecomposition [71]. Finally, the formation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ was addressed by measuring the formation of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ as a result of PMSO oxidation. As visible in Fig. SM5, a quick production of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ occurred showing that $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ is a reactive specie that may be formed in both oxidation systems, following Eqs. It was also noted that, even if the concentration of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ remained constant over time after its generation, the sum of PMSO and $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ concentrations gradually decreased for the first $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , showing that the PMSO conversion to $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ was not complete and different PMSO oxidation by-products were generated. This is justified by the fact that other oxidant species such as $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and OH• radicals can oxidize PMSO generating different by-products such as biphenyl and hydroxylated compounds, as previously reported in the literature [72]. Thus, results obtained suggest an instantaneous generation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ and a more gradual one in the case of other radicals such as $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\mathrm { O H } ^ { \bullet }$ (Fig. SM5). The generation of $\bf { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ was equivalent with both oxidants $( \sim 0 . 0 7 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ , indicating that a similar amount of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 4 + }$ was produced. However, the quenching effect of MeOH seemed to be more relevant in the PDS system compared to PMS, suggesting that in the latter case nonradical species played a greater role. For this reason, the greater efficiency of the PMS/Fe5Cu system can be attributed to the reduced radical scavenging due to the lower amount of iron in the system, as discussed in Section 3.2, and to a possible higher production of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ,$ which is recognized as a highly selective oxidant agent toward organic contaminants [71]. Since different reactive species appeared to be involved in the bimetallic system, EPR measurements and kinetic modeling are recommended for future studies to further confirm and quantify their presence, as well as their contribution to contaminant degradation.

# 3.4. Effect of Cu content in the bimetallic activator

The effect of using FeCu bimetals with different Cu contents (i.e., 1 $\% _ { ; }$ , $5 \%$ , and $5 0 \%$ wt.) to activate PDS and PMS in terms of 4-CP removal and oxidant consumption was evaluated. The results obtained were compared in Fig. 4 with those achieved using $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ activation.

Adding $1 \%$ wt. of Cu to the bimetallic activator led to an increase in the PDS consumption in the first $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ (Fig. 4-b) but did not lead to relevant differences in 4-CP degradation compared to the activation by $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ (Fig. 4-a), which already exhibited an effective activation capability, as discussed in Section 3.2. On the contrary, increasing the Cu content to $5 \%$ and $5 0 \%$ wt. resulted in a decrease of 4-CP removal after $1 0 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ , suggesting that the higher Cu content in the bimetallic activator led to greater unproductive consumption of PDS due to the higher amount of metal ions in the solution (Fig. SM2). Thus, 4-CP degradation during the initial stages was not significantly affected by the Cu content in the activator, because of competing radical scavenging. In the case of PMS, adding only $1 \ \%$ wt. of Cu in the bimetal increased 4-CP degradation at $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ by about $2 6 \%$ compared to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ (Fig. 4-c). The further increase of the Cu content to $5 \ \%$ and $5 0 ~ \%$ wt. showed to increase the 4-CP removal rates although only a small effect was observed in the final degradation at $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ $( 6 3 \ \% \ ( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } ) < 8 9 \ \%$ (Fe1Cu) $< 9 2 \%$ $\mathrm { ( F e 5 C u ) < }$ $9 5 ~ \%$ (Fe50Cu)). The PMS consumption showed a trend similar to 4-CP degradation. Namely, the final PMS consumption (at $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ ) increased with the higher Cu content in the bimetal (Fig. 4-d), with the fastest initial rates confirmed to be in the case of activation by Fe5Cu, in which 4-CP degradation and PMS consumption were $9 2 ~ \%$ and $9 5 ~ \%$ within only $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , respectively.

In the case of PDS activated by Fe5Cu, the rise of unproductive consumption is well correlated with an increase in iron dissolution rate compared to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ (see Fig. SM2). This can be a further indication of the predominantly radical pathway since the radical scavenging reaction (Eq. (7)) is competing with the contaminant oxidation by radicals. On the other hand, the results obtained on PMS consumption suggest that the scavenging effect is lower, which could be justified by a higher contribution of non-radical species to 4-CP degradation. In summary, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ was found to be the most suitable activator for PDS among the tested materials since it allowed to reach the highest 4-CP removal after 120 min. Conversely, Fe5Cu bimetal proved to be more effective for PMS because the initial 4-CP degradation rate was the highest observed, with no significant effect observed in the final degradation by further increasing the Cu content to $5 0 \%$ wt.

# 3.5. Effect of disc milling

The results of the tests performed with Fe50Cu bimetals prepared by disc milling are compared in Fig. 5 to those obtained using a mixture of 50%wt. $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { \bar { 0 } } }$ and $5 0 \%$ wt. $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 } ~ ( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } / \mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 } )$ . With both oxidants, Fe50Cu bimetals allowed to achieve a higher degradation compared to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } / \mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ , consuming roughly the same amount of oxidant. Thus, it can be concluded that using the disc milling technique to synthesize bimetals led to a more productive consumption of oxidants compared to using a mixture of the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ powders with the same composition.

Xu et al. [73] and Liu et al. [45] reported that after ball milling (another physical method that can be used to produce bimetallic materials), metallic powders presented heavy deformations consisting of a higher concentration of strains and defects and a smaller particle size, which increased the specific surface area (SSA). Since a higher SSA can increase the reactivity of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ [17], tests were performed to investigate if the enhancement of 4-CP removal by bimetals may be attributed to possible changes in the morphology of powders or to the presence of Cu in the crystalline structure. Therefore, disc milled- $\cdot \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ powder (milled$\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } )$ was compared to raw $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ powder for the activation of PDS and PMS, and the results obtained have been summarized in Fig. 6. As shown, in the case of PDS activated by milled- $\mathrm { \cdot } \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , 4-CP removal, and oxidant consumption followed the same trend as the ones with raw $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ as an activator (Fig. 6-a and -b). Similar results were also obtained in the PMS systems, since 4-CP degradation was not particularly enhanced by milled- $\cdot \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , and the same amount of oxidant was consumed (Fig. 6-b and -c, respectively). Therefore, it can be assumed that disc milling did not significantly affect the activation properties of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ . Since the primary metal of the tested bimetallic activators is $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ , it can be argued that the deformation of the bimetals morphology by disc milling was not so high to substantially enhance 4-CP oxidation with PDS and PMS. 4-CP removal enhancement achieved by using FeCu bimetals instead of the mixture of the pristine iron and copper powders (Fig. 5) could be then attributed to the presence of Cu in the crystalline structure of the activator that improved the electrochemical corrosion of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ [17,31,48].

# 3.6. Effect of bimetal dosage

The results of the tests carried out to assess the effect of the Fe5Cu bimetal dosage (i.e., $0 . 0 5 ~ \mathrm { g / L }$ , $0 . 1 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ , and $0 . 2 \ g / \mathrm { L }$ ) with both PDS and PMS $\mathbf { \Omega } ^ { ( 1 \ m M ) }$ are shown in Fig. SM6. In the PDS system, increasing the activator concentration from $0 . 0 5 { \gimel } \mathcal { I } .$ to $0 . 2 { \ g } / \mathrm { L }$ did not notably affect 4- CP degradation (Fig. SM6-a), whereas the effect on PDS consumption was more relevant (Fig. SM6-b). As a radical oxidation mechanism prevails in the PDS/FeCu system, higher iron dosage may have caused an increase in iron concentration in the aqueous phase, thus leading to an increase in the radical scavenging (Eq. $( 7 )$ ). Conversely, in the PMS system, 4-CP degradation raised consistently by increasing the dosage of Fe5Cu from $0 . 0 5 ~ \gimel { \mathcal { A } }$ to $0 . 2 ~ \ g / \mathrm { L }$ (Fig. SM6-c). The PMS consumption showed a similar trend, with increasing values observed at higher Fe5Cu concentrations (Fig. SM6-d). These results suggest that the pollutant removal in the PMS system is highly dependent on the concentration of Fe5Cu, and there was no evidence of a significant increase in unproductive consumption because of the higher activator concentration. This may be related to the non-radical pathway exhibited by this oxidation system. In this view, a higher amount of iron possibly prompted the production of non-radical species, thus increasing 4-CP degradation.

# 3.7. Practical considerations for implementing the FeCu systems

# 3.7.1. Secondary effects on water quality

As previously mentioned, the release of dissolved iron and copper progressively increased as the reaction progressed (Fig. SM2), with faster solubilization observed in the Fe5Cu systems, reaching high concentrations of soluble Fe $\mathrm { ( { \ge } 3 5 { \ m g / L } ) }$ and Cu $\left( 1 . 6 \mathrm { m g / L } \right)$ . The Fe and Cu concentrations should be strictly controlled within the threshold of the wastewater quality limits for discharge in surface water. Referring to the Italian legislation, these limits are highly restrictive: $\mathrm { ( F e } \le 2 \ : \mathrm { m g / L }$ , $\mathrm { C u } \leq 0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m g / L } $ [74]. To reduce the concentration of metals in the solution, a selection of the optimum treatment conditions should be made. As previously mentioned, a relatively high degradation of 4-CP $( > 7 0 \% )$ was obtained at low reaction times $\mathrm { 1 5 } \mathrm { m i n } )$ (Fig. 4) when PDS and PMS were activated by Fe1Cu and Fe5Cu. At these shorter reaction times, a lower concentration of metals in the solution was achieved (Fig. SM2), along with considerable savings in treatment cost due to the shorter retention time required, making real-scale application more attractive.

Additionally, the water pH before discharge must be kept within a reasonable range of values, which, making again reference to the Italian legislation, is between 5.5 and 9.5 [75] (p. 200). Keeping the pH in the upper range of these values could probably help in reducing the concentration of metals in the solution. To check this possibility, specific post-treatment tests were carried out by adding NaOH to the solutions obtained from the Fe5Cu and Fe1Cu oxidation systems to achieve $\mathrm { p H } =$ 8.0. As shown in Figs. SM7 and SM8, the Fe and Cu concentration was significantly reduced in both systems owing to its precipitation in the form of $\mathrm { F e ( O H ) _ { 3 } }$ and $\mathrm { C u ( O H ) _ { 2 } } ,$ as previously reported elsewhere [75]. It is worth noticing that lower metal precipitation was obtained in the PMS systems compared to the PDS one. This may be associated with a higher sulfate concentration in the PMS system which may inhibit the iron precipitation [76]. In any case, after NaOH treatment, Fe and Cu concentrations met the environmental quality standards for surface water in Italy (Figs. SM7 and SM8, respectively).

# 3.7.2. Stability and reusability

The stability and reusability of Fe5Cu bimetals for PDS and PMS activation were evaluated over three consecutive oxidation cycles. A shown in Fig. 7, different trends in 4-CP degradation were observed depending on the oxidant system. In the PDS system, 4-CP removal even increased after each successive treatment cycle (Fig. 7-a). However, in the case of the PMS system, treatment efficiency decreased from $9 2 \%$ to $8 1 ~ \%$ and $7 5 ~ \%$ after 1, 2, and 3 successive cycles, respectively. In the PDS system, the iron concentration decreased from 48.1 to 39.5 and $2 8 . 4 \ : \mathrm { m g / L }$ (Fig. 7-c), resulting in reduced radical scavenging and higher 4-CP degradation, as previously mentioned when evaluating the effect of the activator concentration (Fig. SM6-a). Conversely, the drop in degradation efficiency in the PMS system was related to a more significant decrease of the metal concentration (Fe from 36.1 to $5 . 5 \mathrm { m g / L } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ , and Cu from 1.7 to $0 . 2 \ \mathrm { m g / L }$ ), which negatively affected the activation of this oxidant. Additionally, XRD analysis was performed to investigate the crystal structures and phases of Fe5Cu bimetals before and after the application of both oxidation treatments (Fig. 7-b). According to the results, the crystalline phases detected in the material before and after the chemical oxidation treatments remained the same. Although there was no phase transformation following the PDS and PMS treatments, the intensity of the existing peaks significantly decreased (Fig. 7-b), indicating a reduction in the crystallinity of the material [77,78]. The significant decrease in crystallinity could hinder charge transport, making $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ corrosion in the bimetallic particles less feasible [77,79], explaining the lower metal dissolution obtained (Fig. 7-c and -d, respectively). In conclusion, the results obtained showed that Fe5Cu bimetals can be effectively reused in PDS and PMS for at least 3 cycles, significantly reducing the treatment cost and highlighting its suitability for practical application.

# 4. Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the degradation of 4-CP using $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and disc-milled FeCu bimetals to activate PDS and PMS. Disc milling improved oxidant efficiency compared to using $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 0 }$ powder mixtures. The presence of copper in the bimetallic crystalline structure enhanced reaction rates with both oxidants, due to the improved electrochemical corrosion of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ . Activation by bimetals resulted in a significant oxidation process improvement compared with traditional activators (i.e., $\mathrm { F e ^ { 2 + } }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } )$ , particularly in the PMS system. In the PMS/Fe5Cu system, 4-CP removal $( 1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n } )$ ) increased with a higher Cu content in the activator: $6 3 ~ \% ~ ( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } ) < 8 9 ~ \%$ (Fe1Cu) $< 9 2 \%$ $\mathrm { ( F e 5 C u ) < }$ $9 5 ~ \%$ (Fe50Cu). The highest dechlorination $( 6 2 \% )$ and mineralization $( 6 0 ~ \% )$ degree was also obtained with the selected PMS/Fe5Cu system. However, the addition of copper in the activator did not enhance 4-CP removal in the PDS system, as it caused unproductive oxidant consumption, making $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 }$ most effective for PDS activation. The higher 4-CP removal enhancement by bimetals with PMS, compared to PDS, was attributed to the reduction of radical scavenging because of the diminished iron leaching, its stronger synergy with Cu, and a possible higher production of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ as a selective oxidant species. The high reactivity of bimetals led to a rapid release of Fe and Cu into the solution. However, adjusting the pH to 8 at the end of the reaction caused metal precipitation, allowing compliance with strict Italian environmental standards for surface water discharge.

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into the development of disc-milled synthetized FeCu bimetals, highlighting their potential as sustainable and highly effective activators for the treatment of persistent compounds in water. Additionally, Fe5Cu bimetals exhibited remarkable stability over multiple uses. However, further studies are required to assess their effectiveness in activating PDS and PMS for oxidizing various pollutants as well as the technical and economic viability of their large-scale implementation.
