# Efficient generation of 1 O2 by activating peroxymonosulfate on graphitic carbon nanoribbons for water remediation

Weijiang Tang1 , Zhengjie Wang2 , Sheng Guo1 , Rong Chen1 & Fengxi Chen 1

Few-layer graphitic carbon nanoribbons (GCN) with rich defective sites were prepared by pyrolysis at $800 \%$ in $\mathsf { N } _ { 2 }$ of in situ-chelated Fe-polyaniline complexes synthesized via one-pot homogeneous Fenton-like oxidative polymerization of an acidic aniline solution. A minimal amount of iron $( 0 . 4 7 \mathrm { w t \% } )$ made a pivotal role in the nanoribbon growth and graphitization of GCN, and deposited highly dispersed iron species on GCN without post-synthesis acid leaching, which greatly simplified the synthesis procedure of GCN with improved yield. GCN exhibited high activity and stability for catalytic degradation of organic pollutants with peroxymonosulfate (PMS) mainly via non-radical pathways. The influences of various operating parameters on the catalytic performance of GCN were investigated. Scavenging tests, spin-trapping electron paramagnetic resonance spectra, electrochemical analyses, and theoretical calculations unveiled that $^ 1 { \sf O } _ { 2 }$ was the main reactive oxygen species generated from synergistic activation of PMS on GCN while GCN-mediated electron transfer made a minor contribution to organic degradation.

The extensive use of pharmaceuticals and industrial chemicals results in organic pollutants that pose serious threats to the environment and human health. For instance, acetaminophen (APAP) is widely used for its antipyretic, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory effects, and commonly detected in diverse aquatic environments owing to its resilience to complete elimination through conventional wastewater treatment methods. It can potentially produce endocrine disruption and chronic disease since it can oxidize the lipid in cells and denature the proteins with damage to their genetic code1 .

Among various treatment methods (e.g., adsorption, coagulation, and biodegradation), advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are deemed as the most effective method to degrade a broad range of bio-recalcitrant or toxic organic pollutants via reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are usually generated through activation of inorganic peroxides such as $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and persulfates (including peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and peroxydisulfate (PDS)). As compared to $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ aqueous solution, solid persulfates are stabler and easier for transport and storage. Between persulfates, asymmetric PMS molecules are more vulnerable to be activated over heterogeneous catalysts than symmetric $\mathrm { P D S } ^ { 2 }$ .

PMS can be chemically activated by either transition metal (TM)-based or carbonaceous materials3–5 . A major concern of TM-based catalysts is the possible secondary pollution from toxic metal leaching3,6 . In this regard, carbocatalysts, such as carbon nanotubes2 , nanodiamond7 , and reduced graphene oxide8 , represents a promising advancement in water remediation due to their non-toxic nature, stability, large surface area, and tunable surface properties. However, pristine carbocatalysts usually exhibit lower catalytic activities than TM-based catalysts3,6,9 . Therefore, many efforts (e.g., elemental doping and defect engineering) have been made to improve the efficiency of nanocarbons in PMS activation3,10,11.

Doping of heteroatoms (e.g., N, B, ${ \sf S } ,$ or P) into nanocarbons can enhance their performance in PMS activation by tailoring electronic and spin properties of $\mathrm { \dot { s p } } ^ { 2 }$ -hybridized carbon. Nitrogen (N) has been found to be the most promising dopant due to the creation of more functional groups and defective sites, enhancement of electron mobility, and alteration of electron density of local carbon atoms proximate to N-dopants10,12. For example, graphitic-N in N-doped carbon was found to promote the activation of PMS because it facilitates the electron transfer from carbon to $\mathrm { P M S } ^ { 1 3 }$ . Besides, N-dopants provide additional sites for metal anchoring to build emerging metal-N-coordinated carbon materials (M-N-C, M mainly for Fe, Co, Cu, Mn)14.

M-N-C materials are potential replacements of high-cost platinum group metals in some important electrochemical reactions (e.g., oxygen and $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ reduction reactions, oxygen and hydrogen evolution reactions15–17), and organic transformation18. The applications of M-N-C in PMS-based organic wastewater treatment have recently gained considerable interests14,19–24. In this regard, Fe-N-C materials would be a better choice than other TM analogs since iron is earth-abundant and has low cost and toxicity3,22,25,26. Co-doping of metal and $_ \mathrm { N }$ into carbon can regulate the electronic structure and electron-donating ability of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ -hybridized carbon and generate new active sites with synergistic effects between metal and N species, which are crucial to improve the adsorption and activation of PMS on modified carbons13,26–29.

Fe-N-C materials were generally prepared by high-temperature pyrolysis of specially designed and sophisticated iron-containing precursors, such as metal-organic frameworks (e.g., $\mathrm { F e } _ { 2 0 } – \mathrm { P C N } – 2 2 2 ^ { 3 0 }$ and Fe-ZIF- $\cdot 8 ^ { 1 5 , 3 1 - 3 3 \cdot }$ ) and macrocycles (e.g., $\mathrm { F e ^ { \mathrm { I I } } }$ -1,10-phenanthroline complex18 and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I I I } }$ -mesotetraphenylporphine chloride34). Iron may be deposited on, wrapped in, or incorporated into N-doped carbon network in the form of discrete atoms $\left( \mathrm { F e - N _ { x } } \right)$ , clusters, or nanoparticles in $_ { \mathrm { F e - N - C } } 1 8 , 3 3 , 3 5 , 3 6$ . To remove unstable, inactive, or aggregated iron species after pyrolysis, it is usually required to post-treat Fe-N-C materials with acid leaching (e.g., in $0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ - } 1 . 0 \mathrm { M }$ HCl at 30–120 °C for 6–12 h16,36,37 , in $0 . 5 \mathrm { M } \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ at $8 0 ~ \mathrm { { ^ \circ C } }$ for $8 – 1 2  { \mathrm { h } } ^ { 1 7 , 3 2 , 3 8 - 4 2 } )$ ) or by $\mathrm { N H _ { 4 } I }$ etching35. It remains a great challenge to make active and stable atomically dispersed Fe-N-C catalysts via a facile and straightforward method.

Polyaniline (PANi) with $\mathrm { N } / \mathrm { C }$ ratio of ${ \sim } 0 . 1 7$ is a promising polymer precursor to make N-doped carbons due to its simple synthesis, low cost, and chemical stability42–45. Besides, it can coordinate with iron ions via rich N-containing functional groups (amine and imine) to form Fe-chelated polyaniline complexes (named FePANi), which serve as ideal precursors to make Fe, N-co-doped carbon upon pyrolysis22,37,41–43. PANi is usually made using an interfacial polymerization method, wherein the aniline monomer is dispersed in water37,41,45. Ammonium persulfate is commonly used as an oxidant for oxidative polymerization of aniline, which generates a large quantity of sulfate by-products37,41,43–47.

A novel homogeneous Fenton-like solution polymerization technique was introduced in our study to make FePANi through the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } { \cdot } \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ oxidative polymerization of an acidic aniline solution. This method offers several advantages. Firstly, it utilizes $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ as a green oxidant, producing $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ as a by-product. Secondly, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ ions act as an activator for catalytic decomposition of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } .$ generating hydroxyl radicals (Reactions 1 and 2) that facilitate the oxidative polymerization of aniline, and serve as an iron source for one-step synthesis of FePANi. Thirdly, the strongly acidic condition employed in the solution polymerization ensures that FePANi retains only a minimal amount of iron ions that are firmly bound to the polymer backbone. Despite their low presence at $0 . 4 7 \mathrm { w t \% }$ , these iron ions make a crucial role in the growth and graphitization of carbon nanoribbons (GCN) during the pyrolysis of FePANi at $8 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in $\Nu _ { 2 }$ and deposit highly dispersed iron species on GCN without the need for post-synthesis acid leaching. GCN effectively activated PMS to generate single oxygen $( ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } )$ for nonradical degradation of various organic pollutants with good antiinterference ability and recyclability. In addition, density functional theory (DFT) calculations shed light on synergistic effects of adjacent active sites in GCN on PMS activation.

# Results and discussion Characterization of GCN

The XRD pattern of GCN (Fig. 1a) displayed two broad peaks at about $2 3 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ and $4 4 . 5 ^ { \mathrm { { o } } }$ , implying the formation of amorphous or low-crystallinity carbon materials17,48–52. The (002) peak at $2 3 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ was due to the stacking of graphitic layers with a mean interlayer spacing of $0 . 3 7 8 \mathrm { n m }$ , and the weak (100) peak at ${ \sim } 4 4 . 5 ^ { \mathrm { o } }$ was attributed to in-plane repeated units inside graphitic layers. The low degree of graphitization was due to the applied pyrolysis temperature $( 8 0 0 ^ { \mathrm { { o } } } \mathrm { { C } ) }$ just slightly above the onset of iron-catalyzed graphitization of carbon (about $7 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ) ^ { 5 3 }$ . No diffraction peak corresponding to iron or its compounds was observed, suggesting that iron atoms were highly dispersed and firmly anchored on the GCN, which restricted their mobilization, aggregation, and crystallization during pyrolysis. The iron content in the GCN was analyzed by ICP-AES to be $0 . 4 7 \mathrm { w t \% }$ . The Raman spectrum of GCN (Fig. 1b) showed the characteristic D band at $1 3 4 4 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (disordered carbon) and G band at $1 5 8 7 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (graphitic carbon)20,49,54,55. The intensity ratio of D to G band $\mathrm { ( I _ { D } / I _ { G } } \sim 0 . 9 8 6 )$ indicate the existence of abundant defect sites, which can modulate and tune the electronic structure of GCN to enhance its catalytic performances6,49.

GCN exhibited a Type I $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption isotherm with a nearly horizontal plateau, which is typical for microporous materials (Fig. 1c). The initial sharp knee of the isotherm in the low-pressure range $\left( \mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 } < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ is due to micropore filling in GCN. The flat portion in the $\mathrm { P / P _ { 0 } }$ range of $0 . 1 { - } 0 . 8 $ represents saturation uptake in the micropores, from which the micropore volume $\mathrm { V _ { m i c } }$ was estimated to be $0 . 1 4 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } / \mathrm { g }$ . For microporous materials, the conventional BET multi-layer adsorption model is not suitable to assess its surface area. The Langmuir area of GCN, which is equivalent to the monolayer-specific surface area if the amount of $\Nu _ { 2 }$ required to fill the micropores were spread in a close-packed monolayer of molecules, was estimated to be $4 2 5 . 7 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } .$ The final upward turn in the adsorbed amount of $\Nu _ { 2 }$ at $\mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 } > 0 . 9$ in Fig. 1d was attributed to the filling of interparticle mesopores. The total pore volume $\mathrm { V _ { t } }$ at $\mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 } = 0 . 9 9$ was $0 . 2 2 4 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } / \mathrm { g }$ . The mesopore volume $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { m e s } }$ was estimated from $\mathrm { V _ { m e s } = V _ { t } - V _ { m i c } }$ to be $0 . 0 8 3 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } / \mathrm { g } .$ The NLDFT-derived pore size distribution (Fig. 1d) also supported the presence of both micropores $( < 2 \mathrm { n m } )$ and mesopores $\left( 2 5 \mathrm { - } 6 0 \mathrm { n m } \right)$ in GCN. The hierarchical porosity of GCN with $6 3 \%$ of micropores and $3 7 \%$ of mesopores is beneficial for the mass transfer of molecules in catalysis.

The SEM and TEM images of GCN were displayed in Fig. 2. SEM in Fig. 2a showed that GCN was irregular particles of $0 . 2 \mathrm { - } 2 . 3 \mu \mathrm { m }$ , which were formed by the aggregation of primary nanoparticles $( \sim 5 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ according to TEM in Fig. 2b. The aggregated nanoparticles generated interparticle mesopores found in $\Nu _ { 2 }$ physisorption (Fig. 1d). The lattice fringe images in HRTEM (Fig. 2c–e) revealed that GCN possesses the ribbon-like nanostructure with 5–12 graphitic layers. Some nanoribbons are flexible enough to form multilayer nanorings (Fig. 2c). The distance between the neighboring lattice planes measured from HRTEM $\mathrm { 0 . 3 3 7 n m }$ , Fig. 2e inset at the bottom left) is very close to the ${ \mathrm { d } _ { 0 0 2 } }$ value $0 . 3 3 5 \mathrm { n m } )$ ) of graphite, supporting the local graphitization of GCN. The larger ${ \mathrm { d } _ { 0 0 2 } }$ spacing of GCN estimated from XRD $0 . 3 7 8 \mathrm { n m }$ ) can be attributed to lattice deformation over a longer length scale due to the presence of rich defective sites. The XRD and TEM together support the formation of graphitized carbon nanoribbons with rich defective sites. In comparison, iron-free NC possessed nanosheet-like morphology, and no continuous lattices planes were observed in the HRTEM image (Supplementary Fig. 1 in the Supplementary Information). It was also reported that by pyrolysis at $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in $\Nu _ { 2 }$ of the PANi/Fe(III) precursor prepared through interfacial polymerization, the obtained Fe-N/ C displayed coral-rod morphology and contained encapsulated Fe nanoparticles of about $2 { - } 7 \mathrm { n m }$ in diameter41. In interfacial polymerization, the aniline monomer is poorly dispersed, resulting in low complexation efficiency and inhomogeneous distribution of iron on PANi due to mass transfer limitations, which led to the formation of iron nanoparticles after pyrolysis. In comparison, iron species are more regularly coordinated to imine groups $\left( - \mathrm { N } \mathrm { = } \right)$ of growing PANi chains in solution polymerization56, wherein the dissolved aniline monomer and ferric ions exist in ionic or hydrated form. As reported in the ref. 56, multiple PANi chains can be crosslinked via iron ions in a bridging model (PANi-Fe-PANi) (Fig. 3), which serves as a potential precursor for the formation of ribbon-like nanostructure. The uniformly distributed iron species along the cross-linked polymer chains of PANi, although in a small amount $( 0 . 4 7 \mathrm { w t \% } )$ ), make a pivotal role in the nanoribbon growth and graphitization of GCN after pyrolysis. This testified the profound impact that different processes of making Fe-containing PANi polymer precursors may have on the iron distribution and microstructure of the resulting Fe-N-C materials.

In agreement with the appearance of the D band in Raman spectrum, GCN contained abundant defective sites along its lattice planes (Fig. 2d, e), which account for the formation of micropores in the GCN. In accordance with the XRD result (Fig. 1a), no crystalline phase of metallic iron or its compounds (oxides or carbides) was observed in the HRTEM images, which confirmed that iron is highly dispersed in GCN. The EDX measurement (Fig. 2e inset at the top right) detected a weak Fe K-line signal within the lattice planes (marked in red in Fig. 2d, e. In contrast, no iron signal was detected in amorphous regions beside the lattice planes. This corroborates the indispensable role of highly dispersed minor iron species for the nanoribbon growth and graphitization of GCN.

The surface elemental composition and chemical states on GCN were analyzed by XPS. Its survey spectrum (Fig. 4a) showed $\mathrm { ~ C ~ l ~ s , N ~ 1 ~ s ~ }$ , and O 1 s signals with the atomic ratio of $1 1 . 3 \mathrm { C } ; 1 \mathrm { N } ; 0 . 6 \mathrm { O }$ . The oxygen presence was mainly due to partial oxidation of polyaniline during oxidative polymerization of aniline with $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ . The nitrogen content in GCN was higher than that of iron-free NC $7 . 7 \%$ vs. $5 . 4 \%$ in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2), which is attributed to the stabilizing effect of iron on nitrogen via Fe-N coordination during high-temperature pyrolysis. No distinct signal was observed in the Fe 2p region due to its low content (Fig. 4a inset). The C 1 s spectrum (Fig. 4b) was deconvoluted into three peaks corresponding to ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } \mathrm { O }$ at $2 8 8 . 5 \mathrm { e V }$ , C-N/C-O at $2 8 5 . 5 \mathrm { e V }$ , and $\mathrm { C - C / C { = } C }$ at $2 8 4 . 6 \mathrm { e V } ,$ indicating that N and $\mathrm { o }$ are connected with carbon via various chemical bonds. The $_ { \textrm { N 1 s } }$ spectrum (Fig. 4c) was fitted into four components at 401.4, 400.7, 399.6, and $3 9 8 . 0 \mathrm { e V }$ , which were respectively assigned to graphitic-N or quaternary- $\mathbf { \nabla \cdot N }$ (percentage in total N: $3 1 . 0 \%$ ), pyrrolic-N $( 2 5 . 8 \% )$ , $\mathrm { F e - N _ { x } }$ $( 2 2 . 3 \% )$ and pyridinic-N $( 2 0 . 9 \% )$ according to the literature13,57. In comparison, the deconvoluted N 1 s spectrum of iron-free NC nanosheets (Supplementary Fig. 2) only had pyrrolic-N and pyridinic-N peaks due to its lower graphitization degree and lack of the iron element. The presence of graphitic-N and Fe- $\mathrm { \Delta N _ { x } }$ species in GCN is expected to enhance its catalytic performance since both were reported to be active sites for the activation of $\mathrm { P M S } ^ { 1 0 , 1 2 , 1 3 , 2 2 , 2 5 , 2 6 }$ . The deconvoluted $_ \mathrm { ~ O ~ 1 ~ s ~ }$ spectrum of GCN (Fig. 4d) showed three peaks due to the presence of C-O $5 3 3 . 2 \mathrm { e V }$ , $3 3 . 5 \%$ ), ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } \mathrm { O }$ (531.9 eV, $5 7 . 3 \%$ ), and Fe-O species $5 3 0 . 3 \mathrm { e V }$ , $9 . 2 \%$ ). These results indicated that highly dispersed iron atoms in GCN were connected with N and O possibly via HO-Fe- $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { x } }$ .

# Effects of processing parameters on catalytic performances of GCN

To investigate the effect of iron content on the catalytic performance of the resulting GCN- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { X }$ (x stands for the Fe/aniline molar ratio), the amount of the iron source $( \mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } )$ initially added during Fenton-like oxidative polymerization of aniline was varied from 0 to $4 . 5 \mathrm { m o l }$ while keeping the other parameters unchanged to make the FePANi- $\mathbf { \sigma } _ { \mathbf { X } }$ precursor. The decolorization of the OG solution was used to evaluate the catalytic performance of GCN- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { X }$ for efficient screening and optimization as the residual OG concentration can be conveniently analyzed by measuring the absorbance at $4 7 8 \mathrm { n m }$ on a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. As seen from Fig. 5a, iron doping significantly increased the activity of GCN-0.25 as compared to iron-free NC (or GCN-0)—complete decolorization of the solution of OG $( 2 5 \mathrm { m g / L } )$ with PMS was achieved in $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ over $\mathrm { G C N } { - } 0 . 2 5$ while only $8 8 \%$ obtained after $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ over NC. In control experiment without a catalyst, the OG solution was not decolorized with PMS after $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . This testified the necessity of iron co-dopants to make highly active N-doped carbocatalysts.

Figure 5b revealed that all five fresh GCN- $\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { { x } } \left( x = 0 . 0 1 3 , 0 . 0 7 5 , 0 . 1 5 , 0 . 2 5 , \right. } \end{array}$ and 0.38) samples exhibited high catalytic activities for the OG decolorization with PMS, i.e., $9 4 - 9 9 \%$ decolorization rates in $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . However, GCN- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { X }$ with varying iron dosage had different stabilities in recyclability tests. The activity of GCN-0.013 deteriorated abruptly in the 2nd run, while GCN- $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { X }$ $\mathbf { \check { x } } = 0 . 0 7 5$ , 0.15, and 0.38) gradually lost their activities in the 2nd and 3rd runs. The poor stability of GCN- $\mathbf { x }$ $\mathbf { \dot { x } } = 0 . 0 1 3 \ – 0 . 1 5 )$ with lower iron contents reflected that they are susceptible to poisoning or blocking because of limited amounts of surface iron sites. The poor stability of $\mathrm { G C N } { - } 0 . 3 8$ implied that it may contain unstable iron species (e.g., iron clusters or nanoparticles, which were usually encountered with high iron loadings18,33,35,36). In contrast, $\mathrm { G C N } { - } 0 . 2 5$ had the best activity and stability in recyclability tests. The optimal iron loading indicated that polyanilinederived N-doped carbons strongly chelated with the limited amount of iron ions to form active and stable Fe-N-codoped carbons.

Post-treatment of Fe-N-C materials by acid leaching after pyrolysis is usually required to remove unstable or inactive iron species in the literature (e.g., in $0 . 5 { \mathrm { M } } { }$ HCl at $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 0 \mathrm { { h } } ^ { 1 6 }$ or in $0 . 5 \mathrm { M H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ at $8 0 ~ ^ { \mathrm { { o } } } \mathrm { C }$ for $8 – 1 2 \mathrm { h } ^ { 1 7 , \bar { 3 } 2 , 3 8 , 3 9 } ,$ ). No difference in the activity and stability of GCN-0.25 was observed after acid leaching (Fig. 5d). Therefore, acid leaching is not necessary in our synthetic protocol of GCN, which is attributed to the strongly acidic conditions used to make the FePANi precursor during Fenton-like oxidative polymerization of aniline. This greatly simplified the synthesis procedure of GCN with improved yield.

# Catalytic degradation of organic pollutants

The performance of GCN in the activation of PMS was assessed by the degradation of six aromatic pollutants at natural pH values, including four dyes (OG, RhB, CR, and MB) and two pharmaceuticals (APAP and SMM) in Supplementary Table 2. As shown in Fig. 6a, b, all tested dyes and pharmaceuticals were effectively degraded in the GCN/PMS system, which supported the general applicability of GCN as an efficient PMS activator in dealing with common aqueous pollutants.

The stability and reusability of catalysts are important factors in practical applications. The reusability of GCN was checked by degrading APAP with PMS in five consecutive cycles. After each run, no iron leached from GCN was detected in the solution, and the used GCN was recovered by centrifugation and directly reused for the next run. Complete removal of $2 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ APAP was respectively achieved in 15, 15, 30, and $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in the first four runs (Fig. 6c), which indicates good stability and reusability of GCN. The activity of GCN deteriorated in the fifth run—about $7 8 . 5 \%$ of $2 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ APAP was degraded in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . The APAP degradation process was well fitted by pseudo-first-order kinetic model (Fig. $6 \mathrm { d } ) ^ { 5 8 - 6 0 }$ . The pseudo-first-order rate constants $\mathbf { k } _ { \mathrm { s 1 } }$ in five cycles were 0.732, 0.472, 0.136, 0.078 and $0 . 0 2 9 \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } }$ , respectively. It is noticed that GCN had higher adsorption $( \sim 5 0 \% )$ of APAP in the first two runs, which may facilitate the APAP degradation on GCN by ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ or direct electron transfer (as discussed in mechanistic investigation). The COD reduction rates $\left( 3 0 . 0 \% - 4 3 . 0 \% \right)$ in each run implied that some organic intermediates were formed, which may be adsorbed on GCN and block some active surface sites. As such, the adsorption $( \sim 1 5 \% )$ of APAP and the activity of GCN gradually decreased in the 3rd to 5th runs during its recyclability tests with APAP. However, GCN exhibited excellent stability during its recyclability tests with OG (i.e., OG solution completely decolorized in $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in three tested cycles in Fig. 5b).

The effects of common inorganic anions $\left( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - } \right.$ , $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ , $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } { } ^ { - }$ or $\mathrm { H P O } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } )$ and natural organic matter (NOM) in water on the catalytic performance of GCN were examined. According to Fig. 6e, the presence of ${ \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } } ^ { - }$ , $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ , and $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ had negligible effects on the APAP degradation in GCN/PMS system. The presence of $\mathrm { H P O } _ { 4 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ or humic acid (HA) slightly slowed the APAP degradation due to competitive adsorption of $\mathrm { P O } _ { 4 } { } ^ { 3 - }$ or HA on $\mathrm { G C N } { - } 2 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ of APAP was degraded in $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ with the addition of $\mathrm { H P O } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ (10 mM), or in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ with HA $\mathrm { ( 2 0 m g / L ) }$ ). HA may also scavenge some active intermediates (e.g., $^ { 1 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 2 } )$ or interfere with the electron transfer considering that HA and APAP share some common chemical features (e.g., $\pi$ -conjugated aromatic structure and O, N-containing functional groups). If ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ were mainly from the PMS selfdecomposition in the GCN/PMS system, the APAP degradation would display characteristics of an autocatalytic reaction (typically an S-shaped kinetic curve showing a lag period followed by a fast reaction phase) as a result of the formation of BQ. Based on the XPS analyses and literature reports, electron-deficient carbon adjacent to graphitic-N or in carbonyl groups and HO-Fe- $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { x } }$ were believed to be the major active sites on GCN. The density functional theory (DFT) calculations using the Dmol3 package were carried out to gain further insight into PMS activation on GCN (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4). The adsorption energy $\mathrm { ( E _ { a d s } ) }$ of PMS on GCN was defined as $\mathrm { E _ { a d s } = E _ { P M S + G C N } - E _ { P M S } - E _ { G C N } } ,$ , where $\mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { P M S + G C N } } ,$ $\mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { P M S } }$ and $\operatorname { E } _ { \mathrm { G C N } }$ are the energies of PMS-GCN, PMS and GCN, respectively. The negative value of $\mathrm { { E _ { a d s } } }$ means an exothermic adsorption process—the more negative $\mathrm { E _ { a d s } }$ the stronger adsorption. As shown in Table 1, entries 1-2, $\mathrm { E _ { a d s } }$ on carbon atoms adjacent to carbonyl group and graphitic-N of GCN are $- 5 9 . 4 2 $ and $- 6 9 . 0 9 \mathrm { k c a l / m o l }$ , respectively, which confirms that the adsorption of PMS on GCN is thermodynamically favorable. To understand the charge transfer process between PMS and GCN, the atomic charge of PMS was also calculated using the Hirshfeld method after its adsorption on GCN (Supplementary Fig. 4a and c). The group charge of PMS was increased by $+ 0 . 4 2 9 7$ after its adsorption on carbonyl group (Table 1, entry 1), and by $+ 0 . 2 9 4 8$ on graphitic-N (Table 1, entry 2), which indicates the electron transfer from PMS to GCN in the active surface complex [GCN-PMS\*] in accordance with the electrochemical analysis and XPS results.

Considering that GCN contains multiple active sites in its narrow nanoribbon-like structure of 5–12 graphitic layers, two active sites are drawn close to each other (i.e., $\mathrm { C } = \mathrm { O }$ and graphitic-N sites near $_ \mathrm { H O - F e - N _ { x } }$ in Supplementary Fig. 3a), which is expected to collaboratively activate PMS. For comparison, the adsorption of PMS on iron-free NC nanosheets was also calculated (Table 1, entries 3–4 and Supplementary Fig. 4b and d). $\mathrm { E _ { a d s } }$ on NC increases by $4 . 3 3 \mathrm { k c a l / m o l }$ on single ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } \mathrm { O }$ and by $5 . 1 4 \mathrm { k c a l / m o l }$ on single graphitic-N site with respect to those on GCN while the group charges of PMS decrease by 0.0122 and 0.0031, respectively. It reveals the presence of synergistic effects between ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } \mathrm { O } _ { I }$ graphitic-N and HO-Fe- $\mathbf { \cdot N _ { x } }$ sites on GCN for the adsorption of PMS and enhanced electron transfer from PMS to GCN, which is beneficial for the oxidation of PMS to generate ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ or direct electron transfer from pollutants to GCN-PMS\*.

An oxidation mechanism was proposed as follows (Fig. 9): PMS $\left( \mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } - \right)$ ) is first adsorbed at electron-deficient carbon sites adjacent to graphitic-N or ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } \mathrm { O }$ on GCN to form the active surface complex GCNPMS\* , in which PMS is oxidized to $\mathrm { S O } _ { 5 } \mathrm { ^ { \bullet - } }$ through inner-sphere charge transfer (Route R1 in Fig. 9). The self-reaction of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 5 } \mathrm { ^ { * - } }$ generates $^ { 1 } \dot { \mathrm { O } } _ { 2 }$ according to Reaction 5. ${ } ^ { \mathrm { i } } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ then oxidizes APAP via electrophilic attack. Meanwhile, APAP can be oxidized via direct electron transfer to GCN${ \mathrm { P M S } } ^ { * }$ (Route R2 in Fig. 9). As a result, APAP is degraded to small molecular compounds or fragments (e.g., BQ) via a series of reactions (e.g., hydrolysis, hydroxylation, and ring-opening oxidation) and finally mineralized into $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ $( 3 0 . 0 \mathrm { - } 4 3 . 0 \%$ of COD reduction obtained). Iron dopants facilitate the adsorption and oxidative activation of PMS on GCN via synergistic interaction with nearby ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } \mathrm { O }$ or graphitic-N active sites to form the GCN-PMS\* complex, which is pivotal to the generation of ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and direct oxidation of APAP.

In conclusion, few-layer GCN with rich defective sites was made by straightforward pyrolysis of in situ-chelated $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ -polyaniline complexes. A minimal amount of iron is crucial for the nanoribbon growth and graphitization of GCN and for deposition of highly dispersed iron species on GCN without the need for post-synthesis acid leaching. GCN possessed high activity and good stability for catalytic degradation of organic pollutants with PMS. ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { \dot { O } } _ { 2 }$ was found to be the main reactive species while GCNmediated electron transfer made a minor contribution. Moreover, DFT calculations unveiled the synergistic effects of neighboring active sites in GCN on the PMS activation. By leveraging synergistic doping and defect engineering, this study presents a promising method for developing multidoped carbocatalysts as effective PMS activators, enabling the generation of ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to degrade organic pollutants in diverse water matrices.

# Methods

# Chemicals and reagents

All chemicals used were of analytical grade or above. APAP, sulfamonomethoxine (SMM), ciprofloxacin hydrochloride monohydrate (CIP), rhodamine B (RhB), congo red (CR), methylene blue trihydrate (MB), orange G (OG), peroxymonosulfate (PMS, $\mathrm { K H S O } _ { 5 } { \cdot } 0 . 5 \mathrm { K H S O } _ { 4 } { \cdot } 0 . 5 \mathrm { K } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ,$ $2 4 7 \%$ ${ \mathrm { K H S O } } _ { 5 }$ ), p-benzoquinone (BQ), L-histidine, t-butanol and methanol (HPLC grade) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrrolidine N-oxide (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich. All aqueous solutions were prepared with deionized water (DW, $< 2 0 \mu \mathrm { S } / \mathrm { c m } )$ produced from Heal Force NW 15VF.

# Synthesis of GCN

The FePANi precursor was first prepared via homogeneous Fenton-like oxidative solution polymerization of aniline as follows: An appropriate amount of $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ , $1 \mathrm { m L }$ of aniline, and $6 . 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ $( 5 . 5 \% )$ were sequentially added to $3 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of HCl (1.0 M). After the whole mixture reacted at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ under magnetic stirring, the resulting green particles were separated by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with DW and ethanol, and dried overnight at room temperature to obtain FePANi. FePANi was pyrolyzed in a tubular furnace under $\Nu _ { 2 }$ at $8 0 0 ^ { \mathrm { { o } } } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { \textrm { 1 h } }$ at a heating rate of $5 ^ { \mathrm { { \circ } } } \mathrm { { C } } / { \mathrm { m i n } }$ to obtain GCN.

For comparison, iron-free N-doped carbon nanosheets (denoted as NC) was prepared by pyrolysis at $8 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in $\Nu _ { 2 }$ of Fe-free PANi precursor, which was made by sonication of reaction mixture comprising $1 \mathrm { m L }$ of aniline, $3 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of HCl $( 1 . 0 \mathbf { M } )$ and $6 . 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of $\mathrm { \ddot { H } } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \left( 5 . 5 \% \right)$ at $9 0 \mathrm { W }$ and $5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ .

# Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on Bruker D8 Advance Diffractometer with Cu Kα (0.15406 nm) radiation at $3 0 \mathrm { k V }$ and $2 0 \mathrm { m A }$ . Raman spectra were recorded on Thermo Scientific DXR Raman Spectrometer with a $5 3 2 \mathrm { n m }$ excitation laser source. The C 1 s peak of adventitious carbon at $2 8 4 . 6 \mathrm { e V }$ was used as charge reference. The iron content was analyzed by the 1,10-phenanthroline spectrophotometric method and inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES, Thermo Element IRIS-Advantage ICP). SEM and TEM images were respectively taken on JEOL JSM-5510LV Scanning Electron Microscope and JEM-2100 Transmission Electron Microscope at $2 0 0 \mathrm { k V }$ . $\Nu _ { 2 }$ physisorption analysis was performed on Micromeritics ASAP 2020 at $7 7 \mathrm { K }$ . GCN was degassed at $1 8 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ under vacuum before measurement. The Langmuir surface area was calculated from the adsorption data in the relative pressure $\left( \mathrm { P / P _ { 0 } } \right)$ range of 0.05–0.30. In spin-trapping studies, DMPO was used as a trapping agent for •OH and $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals in water or superoxide radical anions $( \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ in methanol, and TEMP for ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ in water. # Catalytic degradation of organic pollutants

In a typical procedure, GCN was added into $5 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of the aqueous solution of an organic pollutant $\mathrm { \langle C _ { s } = 2 5 m g / L \rangle }$ ) without adjusting its pH value. After pre-adsorbed at $2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , the concentration of organic pollutant was measured and taken as the concentration at time zero $\left( \mathrm { C } _ { 0 } \right)$ . The catalytic degradation was initiated by adding $0 . 0 1 5 { \mathrm g }$ of PMS into the solution. About $3 \mathrm { m L }$ of aliquots were sampled by syringe at pre-determined intervals. After filtering through a $0 . 2 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ PES membrane, the concentration of residual pollutant was analyzed. The concentrations of OG, CR, RhB, and MB in water were analyzed by measuring the absorbance at 478, 497, 554, and $6 6 4 \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively, on a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800PC, Shanghai Mapada Instrument). The concentrations of APAP and SMM in water were measured by reversed-phase high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu LC-2050C 3D or Thermo Fisher Ultimate 3000) equipped with an Analytical C18 column $\mathrm { 1 5 0 m m \times 4 . 6 m m }$ , packed with $5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ particles) at $3 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . The mobile phase for APAP analysis was the mixture of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O H }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ (55:45, by volume) at a flow rate of $0 . 8 \mathrm { m L } / \mathrm { m i n }$ with UV-Vis detector set at $2 4 8 \mathrm { n m }$ . The mobile phase for SMM analysis was the mixture of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O H }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ (35:65, by volume) at a flow rate of $1 . 0 \mathrm { m L / m i n }$ with UV-Vis detector set at $2 6 5 \mathrm { n m }$ .

# Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical measurements were conducted in a standard threeelectrode electrochemical cell with an $\mathrm { A g / A g C l }$ (3.5 M KCl) reference electrode, a Pt wire counter electrode, and a catalyst-modified glassy carbon working electrode (GCE, $3 \mathrm { m m }$ ) on an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Shanghai CH Instruments, China) at room temperature. Unless otherwise specified, 0.5 M ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 }$ solution was used as the supporting electrolyte. For electrode preparation, catalysts $( 2 - 5 \mathrm { m g } )$ were ultrasonicated in a mixture containing ethanol $( 0 . 7 5 \mathrm { m L } )$ ), DW $( 0 . 2 5 \mathrm { m L }$ ), and Nafion $( 5 \% , 4 0 \mu \mathrm { L } )$ for ${ \sim } 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ to obtain well-dispersed ink, and then dropped onto the surface of a pre-polished GCE and dried at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ Chronoamperometry (CA) measurements were carried out with the working electrode biased at $0 . 8 \mathrm { V }$ (vs. $\mathrm { A g / A g C l } )$ . PMS and APAP solutions $\mathrm { _ { 1 m L } }$ each) were sequentially added into $5 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of 0.5 M ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 }$ solution in the cell reactor at specified times, and the corresponding changes in current were recorded. The final concentrations of PMS and APAP were $1 . 0 \mathrm { m M }$ and $2 5 \mathrm { m g / L } ,$ respectively. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) analyses were performed in the potential region of 0 to $- 0 . 5 \mathrm { V }$ at a scan rate of $1 0 \mathrm { m V } / s$ . The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) experiments were carried out with $1 0 \mathrm { m V }$ amplitude at open-circuit voltage in the frequency range of $0 . 1 { - } 1 0 ^ { 6 } \mathrm { H z }$ .

# Theoretical calculation

The density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using $\mathrm { D m o l } ^ { 3 }$ package. A nonperiodic graphene cluster model was employed in the simulation, which was modified with some $_ \mathrm { N }$ (graphitic, pyridinic, and pyrrolic) and O (carbonyl and hydroxyl) atoms and a Fe- $\mathrm { . N _ { 4 } }$ unit. The exchange-correlation energy was described by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within generalized gradient approximation (GGA). Double numerical plus polarization (DNP) was employed as the basis set. The convergence tolerances were $1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ Hartree for energy, 0.002 Hartree/Å for maximal force, and $0 . 0 0 5 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ for maximal displacement. The DFT-D method within the Grimme scheme was used to take van der Waals forces into account.
