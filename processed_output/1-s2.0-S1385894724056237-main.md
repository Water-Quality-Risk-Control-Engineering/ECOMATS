# A B S T R A C T

Multilevel assaults on aqueous pathogenic microorganisms were realized by persulfate (PS) activation system with Bi–C based electronic metal-supported interactions (EMSI) of bismuth-embedded carbon chainmail catalyst $( { \mathrm { B i } } @ { \mathrm { C C } } )$ dominated by electron-transfer nonradical pathway. The $\operatorname { B i @ C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ disinfection system achieved approximately $1 0 0 ~ \%$ bacterial inactivation $( 6 . 5 \mathrm { l o g } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { c f u / m L } )$ ) within $3 5 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . The electron transfer from Bi to the carbon layer via Bi-C bond leading to higher work functions, thus realizing primary assault to the extracellular membrane via bacterial extracellular electron transfer (EET) function. Electron rearrangement of Bi-C is conducive to forming the intermediate complexes $( \mathrm { B i } @ \mathrm { C C } \cdot \mathrm { P S } ^ { \ast } )$ , leading to higher work functions and enhancing electron transfer pathway (ETP) process to aggravate cell destruction, while radicals (•OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ ) directly attacks the interior cell. The $\operatorname { B i } @ \operatorname { C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ disinfection system possesses excellent environmental adaptability, cycling performance, and safety. This study enriches the heterogeneous mechanism of PS activation and proposes a promising strategy for sewage effluent treatment.

# 1. Introduction

Traditional water disinfection methods have some limitations, notably incomplete disinfection, easy to produce toxic by-products and so on. For example, ozone disinfection is not only costly, but also produces excess disinfection byproducts (DBPs); Ultraviolet disinfection is affected by water quality conditions and issues such as microbial easy photoreactivation and dark reactivation; Chlorination cannot effectively inactivate chlorine-resistant microorganisms such as cryptosporidium and giardia, and it can also produce disinfection by-products containing halogens. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) based on $\mathrm { { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ are characterized by strong oxidation capability, enabling broad-spectrum sterilization against various microorganisms such as chlorine-resistant bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Furthermore, the reaction process produces only a small amount of halogenated byproducts, suggesting promising prospects for the inactivation of pathogenic microorganisms in reclaimed water, groundwater, or emergency sites [1–5]. Among the ways to activate persulfate, the radical pathway has received significant attention as it involves the efficient activation to generate highly reactive oxidative species (ROS), such as $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and •OH (with high redox potentials of 1.9–3.1 V). However, short lifetimes and limited diffusion distances lead to their low utilization efficiency, and the non-selectivity of the radicals leads to competing reactions with organic matter [6–8]. In contrast, the nonradical pathway relies on direct electron transfer (PS complex, high-valent metal-oxo species, and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } )$ exhibits advantages for oxidation reaction, such as higher selectivity, reactivity, and lower reduction of side reaction [9,10]. Most of the research mentioned that the radical pathway of PS-AOPs plays an important role in bacterial disinfection, but the nonradical pathway is always neglected. Actually, bacterial disinfection has a rate-limiting step, since the destruction of cell membranes composed of macromolecules needs to consume a large amount of ROS (oxidants). Our recent research investigated that utilizing the extracellular electron transfer (EET, nonradical pathway) effect can extract membrane electrons and accelerate intracellular damage [11,12]. This approach is possible to greatly reduce oxidant consumption. Therefore, it is of great significance to develop efficient catalysts by fully utilizing the advantages of radical and nonradical pathways and elucidating the reaction mechanism of PS-AOPs.

Compared with traditional metal-based catalysts, the carboncatalyzed nonradical electron transfer pathway (ETP) path exhibits greener and more efficient characteristics, which can extract electron oxidation cells via the EET effect, demonstrating significant bacterial inactivation potential [13–15]. For example, graphene and carbon nanotubes have been identified as promising candidates in this field [16–18]. The efficiency of PS activation by carbon materials primarily depends on the distribution of surface electrons. Pure carbon catalysts possess a homogeneous distribution of surface electrons, resulting in a relatively lower reactivity. The PS activation can be enhanced by introducing surface functional groups, heteroatoms, or surface defects [19–22]. For instance, Wang et al. observed that higher nitrogen doping in carbon nanotubes leads to stronger direct electron transfer and consequently higher PS activation efficiency [23]. Nevertheless, the limitation of doping levels still constitutes a bottleneck restraining the activity of carbon catalysts.

Studies have shown that chainmail catalysts with electronic metalsupport interaction (EMSI) structure can form an internal electric field due to differences in work function and Fermi level [24–26]. The EMSI effect achieves effective control and regulation of electrons by manipulating the electronic state and transport characteristics at the catalyst surface [27,28]. For chainmail catalysts, the EMSI effect drives electron transfer between the metal and carbon. The enrichment of carbon surface electrons induces the ETP process in PS, and the change of potential difference and work function enhancement can strengthen the electron extraction of pollutants. In the case of the Mott-Schottky heterojunction formed in Co NPs@N-GC, the electron density rearrangement occurs through electron transfer from Co NPs to the carbon layer, facilitating PS activation via a nonradical pathway [29]. Inspired by the above, it was hypothesized that the construction of EMSI-structured chainmail catalysts for water disinfection using low-toxicity and safe non-transitionmetal bismuths, which possess good catalytic activity and excellent electron-transporting ability in specific reactions, may enhance PS activation and promote the EET process by the increased work function [30–34].

In this study, bismuth-embedded carbon chainmail catalyst $( { \mathrm { B i } } @ { \mathrm { C C } } )$ with the EMSI effect was applied to the persulfate activation system with the multilevel synergistic attack dominated by electron-transfer nonradical pathway for disinfection. This study aims to reveal the efficient disinfection mechanisms, the migration process of interface electrons, and the PS activation pathway. EMSI effect is the key trigger in this system, we will explore the intrinsic mechanisms of the Bi–C bond-based EMSI structure contribution to ETP and PS activation. In addition, we evaluate the practical application potentials of the catalyst through theoretical calculations and batch experiments, and thoroughly studied environmental resistance, cycling performance and safety. The $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ PS system, dominated by the non-radical electron transfer pathway, exhibits significant bacterial inactivation potential by efficiently extracting electrons and accelerating intracellular damage. Compared with conventional methods, the B $\operatorname { i } @ \operatorname { C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ system not only activates PS efficiently, but also optimizes the electron transfer process through the EMSI effect, thus improving its disinfection efficiency and environmental friendliness. These properties make the $\mathsf { B i @ C C / P S }$ system a promising option for future water treatment systems. This study will further deepen the understanding of the mechanism of PS-AOPs disinfection system and promote its application in wastewater effluent treatment systems.

# 2.3. Batch experimental process

In a typical experiment, a predetermined amount of catalyst was added to $2 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of bacterial solution with an initial concentration of 6.5 $\mathrm { l o g } _ { 1 0 } \ \mathrm { c f u / m L }$ . Then, $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ perdisulfate was introduced for advanced oxidation bactericidal tests. The initial pH of the solution was adjusted by adding phosphate buffer solution. Samples were collected at different time intervals, immediately quenched using an equal volume of $\mathrm { N a _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 3 } }$ $( 1 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m M } )$ , and subjected to gradient dilution with sterile deionized water. After thorough vortex mixing, $5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ of the diluted solution was evenly spread onto agar plates. These plates were inverted and incubated at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in a microbiological incubator for $^ { 1 6 \mathrm { h } }$ . The concentration of bacteria in the solution was calculated according to the number of colonies. Supplementary Text S3 provides detailed information on the selection and cultivation methods used for different bacterial strains.

# 2.4. Bacterial inactivation process and biomolecular damage

Bacterial membrane damage was observed with SEM (Joel-JSM6301-F). SYTO-9 and PI staining assessed bacterial viability. Based on the reaction of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ with coumarin to form a high-fluorescence compound (7-hydroxy coumarin, $4 5 6 ~ \mathrm { \ n m }$ ), lipid peroxide (LPO) was analyzed on a Hitachi f-4500 fluorescence spectrophotometer. The A003-1 Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit evaluated lipid peroxidation during disinfection (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), and assessed the extent of cellular damage. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) (BC0170, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co, Ltd.) and catalase (CAT) (BC0200, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co, Ltd.) Activity Assay Kits were used to quantify antioxidant enzyme levels. The $^ { 2 , 7 ^ { \prime } }$ -dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe measured intracellular reactive oxygen species levels, indicated by green fluorescence intensity. The GSH Assay Kit (A006-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing) evaluated bacterial glutathione levels and antioxidant capacity. In the above experiments, the prepared sample was transferred to microplate (PakGent®), and the relative fluorescence intensity was measured using an enzymograph. Text S4 shows detailed information.

The electron spin resonance (ESR, Bruker A200 spectrometer) was employed to determine the dominating ROS in the system. The widely recognized EMSI effect greatly enhances electron transfer, leading to heightened catalytic activity and efficiency. The changes in voltage and current on the linear sweep voltammogram (LSV) can intuitively reflect the activity of the catalyst. In other catalytic fields, a smaller overpotential at the same current density on the LSV curve indicates higher catalytic activity of the material, mainly reflecting the level of external energy required. In this study, as shown in Fig. S8a, at the same current density, $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ has the lowest overpotential, indicating the smallest charge transfer impedance throughout the process. A smaller Tafel slope indicates a smaller voltage drop required for an increase in current density by one order of magnitude, indicating faster kinetic processes in the catalytic process. It also reflects faster electron transfer processes during catalysis, confirming the intrinsic reasons for the excellent catalytic performance of the catalyst. As shown in Fig. 1h, the Tafel slopes for pure C and commercial Bi are 0.596 and 0.525 respectively, while the Tafel slope for chainmail catalyst structured $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ , where Bi microspheres are encapsulated in carbon, is only 0.395. Therefore, $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ exhibits a smaller Tafel slope, indicating faster kinetic processes. Specifically, the presence of Bi microspheres enhances the electron transfer ability of carbon, and there is electron transfer mediated by Bi-C bonds between Bi and carbon [42–44]. To further validate our conclusions through electrochemical methods, in addition to the Tafel slope method and linear sweep voltammetry, we used electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) to further support our findings, as shown in Fig. S8b. As is well known, EIS is an effective method for reflecting the ease of electron transfer in heterogeneous materials. In the EIS graph, a smaller radius indicates a smaller charge transfer resistance and better electron migration capability. Therefore, the electrochemical impedance profiles of catalysts were tested. Similar to the trend of Tafel slopes and linear sweep voltammetry, $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ chainmail catalyst demonstrate superior electron transfer capabilities. This again confirms that the chainmail catalyst $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ accelerates electron migration due to the heterogeneous structure composed of Bi and carbon [45]. Electrochemical analysis showed that the EMSI phenomenon between the bismuth metal and the carbon layer significantly enhanced the electron transfer capacity of the catalyst. Consequently, this expedites the initiation of electron exchange processes and encourages the activation of PS.

To elucidate the endogenous charge migration dynamics and direction of $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ , the work functions $( \Phi )$ of metallic Bi and graphite carbon were determined based on DFT calculations. As shown in Figs. S9 and S10, the $\Phi$ of metallic Bi and graphite carbon were determined to be 3.744 and 3.961 eV, respectively. Metallic Bi possesses a lower $\Phi$ and a higher Fermi energy level, so when it is in close contact with carbon, the electrons in metallic Bi will transfer to the carbon layer until the equilibrium Fermi energy level is reached (Fig. 1i). The work function of the B $@ \mathrm { C C }$ with the formed EMSI structure has reached 4.01 eV, indicating that bacteria are more prone to the EET process (Fig. 1j). This charge redistribution is achieved based on charge transfer induced by Bi-C bonds. As shown in Fig. 1k, the differential charge density reveals the formation of Bi-C bonds between the metallic Bi and the carbon layer, with Bi acting as an electron donor center, transferring electrons to the carbon layer. The top view reveals that the carbon atom serves as the electron-rich center in this structure. In summary, the chainmail catalyst Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ exhibits the EMSI effect attributed to the strong interfacial interaction of Bi-C bonds.

# 3.2. Evaluation of PS activation performances and mechanistic investigation

The disinfection efficiency on $\operatorname { B i @ C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ was evaluated in terms of E. coli K-12 inactivation performance. We investigated the influence of catalyst preparation conditions on disinfection efficacy, including Bi content, calcination temperature, and temperature increase rate (Fig. S11a-c). Fig. S11d explores the effect of $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ and PS dosage. Comparison experiments show that PS alone has no bactericidal effect, while $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ alone shows a small amount of antibacterial effect, which indicates that there may be a direct interaction between $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ and microorganisms, such as the EET process (Fig. 2a). The introduction of PS into the $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ disinfection system led to a gradual decrease in bacterial activity, ultimately resulting in a nearly complete bacterial deactivation rate of $1 0 0 ~ \%$ within $3 5 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ ( $6 . 5 \log _ { 1 0 }$ cfu/mL). In comparison, the Commercial Bi and Pure C were much slower to sterilize bacteria, dropping only 2.3 and $2 . 1 \log _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { c f u / m l }$ , respectively. In order to compare the disinfection efficiency of different systems, the pseudofirst-order kinetics is shown in Fig. S11f. Here, ${ \mathrm { C } } _ { 0 }$ and C represent the E. coli concentration at the initial time and the time t, respectively. The k $( \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ value for B $\operatorname { i } @ \operatorname { C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ is the highest (0.442), higher overall efficiency than Commercial Bi/PS (0.179), Pure C/PS (0.152). Based on the analysis above, the $\operatorname { B i @ C C / P S }$ system demonstrates excellent disinfection performance. These suggest that the EMSI effect gives B $@ \mathrm { C C }$ a good PS activation capacity.

To explore the inactivation mechanism of the B $\mathsf { i } @ \mathbf { C C } / \mathsf { P S }$ system, the corresponding scavengers were added to the solution and assess its contribution to bacterial inactivation (Fig. 2b). Methanol is capable of eliminating both •OH and $\mathrm { { \bf S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } } }$ concurrently, whereas TBA only targets •OH. The distinction between the two curves in the middle can be attributed to the impact of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ [7,15]. According to Fig. 2b, the efficacy of sterilization was observed to decline by 0.9 and $0 . 5 \log _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { c f u / m l }$ after the elimination of $\bullet \mathrm { O H }$ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ species, respectively. Corroborating the capture experiment, the ESR spectroscopy findings derived from Fig. 2c demonstrate a weak enhancement of the signal intensity of DMPO-•OH and DMPO- $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ compared to sole PS solution. The results showed that •OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ are not the main active species in this system. Trapping experiments employing TEMPOL revealed that the contribution of $\bullet \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { - }$ to the disinfection process is limited, only $1 . 0 3 \log _ { 1 0 }$ $\operatorname { c f u } / \mathrm { m l }$ of bactericidal effect was inhibited. This observation was further corroborated by the ESR testing of DMPO- $\bullet \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { - }$ , where minimal variations in signal amplitude were observed (Fig. 2d). These findings suggest that the exceptional disinfection efficacy is not contributed primarily by $\bullet \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { - }$ . The ESR signal intensity of TEMP- $^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 } ,$ , which represents the content of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , was initially high but exhibited a slight decrease in peak intensity after the reaction (Fig. 2e). Similarly, using FFA as $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ scavenger resulted in a few changes in the efficacy of the system compared to the original disinfection system, suggesting that $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ is still not the primary active ingredient. However, the addition of $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ resulted in a significant weakening of the TEMPO-e- signal, indicating the involvement of electrons in the activation and disinfection process of PS (Fig. 2f). This conclusion was also confirmed by $\mathsf { e }$ -quenching experiments, $4 . 8 \ \mathrm { l o g } _ { 1 0 } \ \mathrm { c f u } / \mathrm { m l }$ of disinfection inhibited by quenching the electrons using $\mathrm { K _ { 2 } C r _ { 2 } O _ { 7 } }$ (Fig. 2f), indicating that e- serves as the primary active species during the disinfection process. Based on the analysis above, it can be concluded that it can be concluded that the sterilization system mainly proceeds through the non-radical pathway, which consists of the EET and ETP processes.

# 3.3. Explore the multistage assault path of the disinfection process

# 3.3.1. Primary assault of the EET process

To demonstrate the EET process between $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ and E. coli, the LPO content and fluorescence level of cells in the solution without PS were detected. As shown in Fig. 3a, the LPO content increased with the contact of Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ with the cells, implying that the cell membrane gradually underwent oxidative reaction. The appearance of red fluorescence was observed in the fluorogram, confirming that part of the cell membrane produced a certain degree of breakage. It was observed by zeta potential that the $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ catalyst and the surface of the bacteria showed positive and negative opposite potentials, in which case they would tend to be attracted to each other (Fig. 3b). We tested the hydrophobic angle and roughness of the material to check the adhesion to bacteria. It is well known that a smaller hydrophobic angle is easy to contact with bacteria in aqueous solution [46]. The hydrophobic angle of $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ is $1 0 1 ^ { \circ }$ , which is significantly lower than that of $1 3 8 . 6 ^ { \circ }$ for Pure C, suggesting that the structure of Bi-embedded carbon is more suitable for the sterilization system (Fig. 3c). Higher surface roughness also enhances adhesion. The arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) and root-mean-square surface roughness (Rq) of $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ were $2 5 . 8 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ and $1 8 . 8 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively, while the roughness of Pure C was relatively low. The SEM image of Fig. 3d shows E. coli K-12 adhering to the $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ surface. This established the foundation for electron transfer between Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ and E. coli.

In situ KPFM measurements of the surface potential and work function of cells in contact with $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ were performed to probe the interfacial interactions between B $@ \mathrm { C C }$ and $E$ coli K-12 (Fig. 3e). Work function at the bacterial location gradually increased to $4 . 2 5 2 ~ \mathrm { m V } _ { \mathrm { : } }$ , $4 . 2 7 3 \ : \mathrm { m V }$ , and $4 . 2 7 9 \mathrm { m V }$ after $0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ respectively, while the potential difference between cells and Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ gradually decreased to $2 3 . 3 2 \mathrm { e V }$ , 22.89 eV and $1 9 . 6 5 \mathrm { e V }$ . This result suggests that the $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ chainmail catalyst can extract electrons from bacteria. Similarly, LSV curves show higher current density between $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ and bacteria than Pure C, confirming the EET process between $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ and bacteria (Fig. 3f). The EET process between Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ and bacteria is caused by the EMSI effect: The EMSI effect induces a potential difference at the Bi-C interface. Upon contact with the external environment, this potential difference acts as an attractive force, facilitating the absorption and transfer of external electrons. This is demonstrated by the higher work function of the $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ in Fig. 2e.

To further investigate the interface interactions between $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ and bacterial cell membranes, the reaction active sites were analyzed using FTIR and XPS. As shown in Fig. 3g, the 1080 and $1 2 3 8 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ peaks were attributed to the functional groups $\mathrm { H P O } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ and $\mathrm { P O } _ { 2 } ^ { \cdot }$ located in the cell membrane lipopolysaccharide (LPS), respectively [47]. Upon exposure to ${ \mathrm { B i @ C C } }$ , the two peaks shifted and decayed, proving the occurrence of an oxidation reaction. The vibration of the –COOH group at $1 3 9 4 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ was also attenuated and slightly blue-shifted. In addition, P 2p XPS showed that the $3 \mathrm { i } @ \mathrm { C C } / E$ . coli system increased the binding energy by $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { { \ e V } }$ , indicating that $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ extracted electrons from phosphate groups on the cell membrane (Fig. 3h). Therefore, it can be inferred that the EMSI-induced EET process mainly oxidizes LPS on the cell membrane, with phosphate groups and –COOH as the main reaction sites. The primary assault of EET on bacteria oxidizes the outer cell membrane, causing a certain degree of damage, which is conducive to subsequent radical and nonradical disinfection [47–50].

# 3.3.2. Evaluating the nonradical pathways in the Bi@CC/PS system

To study the PS activation process, electrochemical methods were employed to measure the instantaneous potential of the reaction solution. In Fig. $4 \mathbf { a }$ , the open circuit potential of the $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ electrode displayed a rapid rise upon the introduction of PS, reaching a stable plateau. Indicating the formaton of $\mathrm { B i } @ \mathrm { C C } \cdot \mathrm { P S } ^ { \ast }$ complex facilitated by electron migration between PS and Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ [45,51]. The current increase observed for the pure C electrode was comparatively smaller, suggesting that the EMSI effect modified the electron density of the carbon layer and improved interaction with PS. Bi 4f XPS shows a valence shift of $0 . 3 8 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ towards higher binding energies, demonstrating Bi promoted the electron transfer between the carbon layer and PS (Fig. 4b). The mechanism of the PS activation through direct electron transfer as the dominating pathway in Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ has been investigated by DFT theoretical calculations. As shown in Fig. 4c-d and Fig. S12, the EMSI effect enhances the adsorption capability of PS on $\mathsf { B i @ C C }$ with an energy of $- 2 . 3 2 \mathrm { e V }$ , higher than pure C $\left( - 1 . 5 7 \ : \mathrm { e V } \right)$ , and a longer peroxide bond length of $1 . 4 \bar { 4 } 5 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ,$ which results in a relatively lower dissociation energy of peroxides into sulfates. Bader charge calculations demonstrate a higher charge transfer amount of 1.37 e in B $@ \mathrm { C C }$ , indicating that PS is more inclined to be activated on the surface of Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ to form B $@ \mathrm { C C ^ { - } }$ $\mathbf { P S ^ { \ast } }$ complexes.

Further investigation has revealed that the stronger disinfection ability is attributed to the formation of $\mathrm { B i } @ \mathrm { C C } \cdot \mathrm { P S } ^ { \ast }$ complexes with a higher work function (5.538 eV) when PS is adsorbed on ${ \mathrm { B i @ C C } }$ , compared to the $\mathrm { C } { \cdot } \mathrm { P } { \cal S } ^ { \ast }$ alone of 4.01 eV (Fig. 4e and Fig. 1i). Meaning that the $\mathrm { B i } @ \mathrm { C C } \cdot \mathrm { P S } ^ { \ast }$ complex is more likely to attract electrons from pollutants through a direct electron transfer mechanism. Coupled with the current trend in the observed current in Fig. 4a after introducing E. coli, it can be inferred that bacterial deactivation is caused by the oxidation resulting from the cell transferring electrons to the $\mathsf { B i } @ \mathsf { C C } \cdot \mathsf { P S } ^ { \ast }$ complex (Eq. (3) [23,29]. This process simultaneously activates PS and confirms that the complex has a higher oxidation potential than $E _ { * }$ . coli.

# 3.3.3. Radical and nonradical multilevel synergistic disinfection mechanisms

To investigate the mechanism of inactivation of bacteria by the B $\operatorname { i } @ \operatorname { C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ system, we assayed the enzymatic activities and biomolecular oxidation processes of the cells. The enhancement of the N1s XPS spectral peak in the low-energy region of N1s after the reaction indicates that nitrogen-containing organic substances such as amino groups were oxidized, confirming the cellular inactivation induced by oxidation (Fig. 5a). The cell membrane damage of E. coli was determined by malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (Fig. 5b). Following treatment, MDA levels rapidly rose to $2 . 7 \ \mathrm { n m o l / L }$ within $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , indicating lipid peroxidation in the cell membrane and subsequent impairment of its structure and function. As time progressed, MDA levels decreased, suggesting further oxidative reactions. The disruption of the cell membrane facilitates the entry of free radicals into the cell interior to give a more lethal attack. The antioxidant defense system within the cell membrane was activated in response, leading to the production of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and CAT. Changes in SOD and CAT levels indicated the initiation and subsequent decline of the antioxidant system. Glutathione (GSH), involved in maintaining cellular immunity, initially increased upon external attack (Fig. 5c). However, prolonged exposure time resulted in the GSH levels decreasing from $0 . 0 3 5 \mathrm { \ m m o l / L }$ to 0.007 mmol/L, indicating cellular final death [52]. We monitored intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which can damage cellular components and metabolism if excessive (Fig. 5d-e and Fig. S13). During treatment, intracellular ROS levels increased and caused oxidative stress in cells in the Bi@CC/PS system [53]. Prolonged accumulation of ROS can damage cellular components and metabolism. After $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , the fluorescence intensity weakened due to bacterial cell membrane damage and the leakage of fluorescent probe oxidation products. Fig. 5f-g and Fig. S14 depicted smooth surfaces of original E. coli K-12, whereas wrinkled and damaged cell membranes were observed after treatment [54]. The viability staining showed a gradual transition from green fluorescence to red, demonstrating the gradual breakdown of the bacterial cell membrane (Fig. 5h-i and Fig. S15). The process of cell membrane fragmentation was observed in the scanning electron micrograph (Fig. S16). These findings confirm that the Bi $@ \mathrm { C C } / \mathrm { P S }$ system has a lethal oxidative effect on cells, completely inactivating bacteria.

# 3.4. Universality and recycling ability

To better evaluate the potential application of the $\mathsf { B i @ C C / P S }$ process in disinfection, a flow reactor was designed to test the disinfection of different pathogenic microorganisms and the disinfection of real water conditions (Fig. 6a). To explore the broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties of the system, in addition to using E. coli as a model bacterium for Gram-negative bacteria, we explored the bactericidal properties of $\operatorname { B i @ C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ against Gram-positive bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus, and chlorine-resistant bacterium, BM1-1, and the results are shown in Fig. 6b. Chlorine-resistant bacterium BM1-1 and E. coli K-12 achieved comparable disinfection efficiency, while Staphylococcus aureus is relatively slow because Gram-positive bacteria have a thick and hard cell wall composed of multiple layers of peptidoglycan. The disinfection results of these three types of model bacteria are sufficient to show that $\operatorname { 3 i } @ \operatorname { C C } / \operatorname { P S }$ system has a broad spectrum antibacterial effect, and the disinfection efficiency varies according to the disinfection objects. Moreover, when applied to surface water, this activation system demonstrated remarkable disinfection capabilities, achieving complete inactivation of $6 . 5 \mathrm { l o g } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { c f u } / \mathrm { m L }$ within $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ .

To investigate the environmental resistance of the system possessed by the nonradical path, the disinfection effect was evaluated under exposure to a variety of common anions and cations. As shown in Fig. 6c, several common ions such as $\mathrm { C O } _ { 3 } ^ { 2 - }$ , $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } P O _ { 4 } ^ { - } , N O _ { 3 } ^ { - } } ,$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ , and $\mathrm { N a ^ { + } }$ and $\mathsf { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ had little effect on the bactericidal effect. Among them, $\mathsf { C O } _ { 3 } ^ { 2 - }$ had the greatest inhibition effect, and it also only decreased by $0 . 3 \mathrm { l o g } _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { c f u / m o l }$ compared with the undisturbed system, and the bacterial inactivation rate still reached $9 9 . 9 ~ \%$ . These results indicate that the PS activation dominated by the direct electron transfer pathways is significantly resistant to interference. The chainmail catalyst $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ exhibits excellent cycling performance in activating PS (Fig. 6d). The slight decrease in performance after a few cycles may be due to the accumulation of residues on the catalyst surface and the leakage of small amounts of Bi metal ions. This can be attributed to the structural stability of the Bi $@ \mathrm { C C }$ composite based on the Bi-C bond. The encapsulation of the catalyst with a carbon layer protects it from damage by oxidants, acids, and bases present in the environment. Additionally, the catalyst can be regenerated through processes such as washing and surface structure restoration, enabling multiple cycles of reuse without compromising its catalytic efficiency. The protective effect of the chainmail catalyst structure on the internal metal Bi was obtained by the Bi ion content test: the leakage content of Bi ions in the water samples after Commmercial Bi/PS and $\mathsf { B i @ C C / P S }$ treatments were 13.16 and $7 . 1 \mu \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ , respectively, which indicates that the chainmail catalyst structure protects the internal metal and prevents the leakage of metal ions (Fig. S17a-b). The preservation of the crystal phase of $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ before and after the reaction confirms the above statement (Fig. S17c). Tests were conducted to evaluate the non-toxicity of the disinfection system. Results from photobacterium Vibrio fischeri bioassay (Fig. S18a-b) indicated minimal acute toxicity with luminous inhibition rate below $1 0 \%$ for both $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ and purified water. Additionally, umu-chromo test (based on ISO 13829) demonstrated low genotoxicity in purified water (UMU induction ratio $< ~ 1 . 5$ , the genotoxicity threshold) (Fig. S18c) [47,55]. Therefore, secondary pollution in the Bi $@ \mathrm { C C } / \mathrm { P S }$ disinfection system was negligible. In summary, the B $\mathsf { i } @ \mathbf { C C } / \mathbf { P S }$ disinfection system is green, lowcarbon, and non-toxic.

# 4. Conclusions

Given the increasing global attention to water resource hygiene and safety, the development of low-carbon, efficient, and cost-effective sterilization technologies has become necessary. In the present study, multilevel synergistic attack dominated by electron-transfer nonradical pathway on aqueous pathogenic microorganisms was realized by persulfate (PS) activation system with EMSI of bismuth-embedded carbon chainmail catalyst $( { \mathrm { B i } } @ { \mathrm { C C } } )$ . The Bi–C based EMSI effect realizes the electron rearrangement from Bi transfer to the carbon layer, and the excellent charge transfer and PS activation ability of the catalyst was confirmed by a series of chemical characterizations and theoretical calculations. First, $\operatorname { B i @ C C }$ caused a certain degree of oxidative damage to bacteria through their extracellular electron transfer (EET) function. Then, the electron rearrangement favored the formation of intermediate complexes $( \mathrm { B i } @ \mathrm { C C } \cdot \mathrm { P S } ^ { \ast } )$ ) with PS, which led to a higher work function, enhanced the dominant ETP process of the system, and exacerbated cellular damage. Moreover, free radicals (•OH and ${ \bf S } { \bf O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - \cdot }$ ) directly attack the cell interior, causing deep oxidative damage. The synergistic attack of multilevel pathways dominated by the direct electron transfer pathway leads to complete bacterial death. Broad-spectrum resistance to a variety of pathogenic microorganisms was explored, confirming the adaptability, cycling performance and safety of various environmental conditions. This study reveals the inactivation mechanism of the multilevel assault paths for microorganisms, the interfacial electron transfer process, and the PS activation pathway. The proposed mechanisms of the multilevel radical and nonradical assaults provide some guidance for treating sewage pathogenic microorganisms.
