# . Introduction

Olive oil production industry has a significant impact on the environment due to the generation of substantial amounts of wastewater (known as olive mill wastewater, OMW) of approximately 30 million $\mathfrak { m } ^ { 3 }$ annually and around 20 million tons per year of solid waste (pomace) [14]. Consequently, failure to treat these effluents results in adverse environmental impacts, including odor release, alteration of natural watercourses, degradation of soil quality and vegetation growth, endangerment of aquatic ecosystems, and contamination of surface and underground water sources [5]. The presence of bio-recalcitrant and toxic compounds in the composition of OMW limits the use of biological treatment systems. Rozzi and Malpei [6] proposed different approaches for treating OMW, including physical methods (concentration by evaporation, filtration, and ultrafiltration/reverse osmosis), chemical processes (Fenton, wet oxidation, electrolytic oxidation, combustion, and neutralization with lime), and physicochemical processes (flocculation, coagulation, electrocoagulation, and ion exchange). However, each of these methods has been utilized separately to treat effluents, and it was determined that none of them was considered technically feasible or cost-effective. Therefore, it is necessary to combine techniques to effectively treat OMW [7].

Before the biological treatment, advanced oxidation processes (AOP) are commonly used to diminish toxic organic compounds that harm biological activity. These processes consist of chemical methods and are frequently referred to as AOP [8]. Fenton process is among the most effective in this variety of advanced oxidation processes, breaking down the target compounds by hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ catalyst presence. This results in the production of hydroxyl radicals essential for oxidizing the respective compounds under operating conditions that are close to ambient temperature and pressure [9,10]. The homogeneous Fenton process presents some drawbacks, such as short pH range (24), iron-rich sludge formation due to the neutralization of the effluent's pH, leading to the precipitation of the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ ion that requires additional treatment at the end of the process [11]. One of the ways to overcome these disadvantages is to use heterogeneous catalysts, which can allow working in a wider range of pH and avoid the production of sludge with a high amount of iron. Biomass residues can be a suitable precursor of carbon-based materials to promote the required strategy of wastewater remediation by treating waste with waste, taking advantage of its non-toxicity, low cost, and natural biodegradability [12]. These materials must fulfill three main catalyst support requirements 1) increase the catalyst surface area, 2) improve the hydrophobicity, thermal, hydrolytic and chemical stability of the catalyst, and 3) reduce sintering and govern the useful catalyst lifecycle [13]. The most used supports include silica, zeolite, alumina, polymers, fibers, clay and activated carbon [1215].

For Fenton process there are different possible catalytic mechanisms using carbon based materials with incorporated iron: carbon-based materials with high specific surface area and highly open pore structure allow adequate dispersion of Fe-based catalysts on their surface and can favor interfacial contact of reactants with catalytic active centers [16,17]; some carbon-based materials have a high adsorption capacity for several organic compounds from aqueous solutions due to their poly-aromatic structure and surface functional groups. This allows improving degradation efficiency concentrating organic molecules close to the reactive center of Fe-based catalysts through $\pi { - } \pi$ interaction, hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interaction (Eq. 1) [18,19]; carbon-based materials themselves can act as an activator of hydrogen peroxide to produce 'OH radicals [20]; the electronic and conductive properties of carbon-based materials could contribute to an electron transfer from reduced carbon to Fe-based catalysts and consequently promote the redox cycle of Fe(III) to Fe(II) as well as the continuous generation of \*OH radicals (Eqs. 2 and 3) [21].

Researchers have also explored alternative methods like Sulfate Radical-based Advanced Oxidation Processes (S-AOPs) as a means of mitigating issues stemming from the Fenton process [22]. S-AOPs can function akin to the Fenton process with the ability to work in a wider pH range, using $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ cations for the breakdown of persulfate (PS, $S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } ^ { 2 - } )$ or peroxymonosulfate (PMS, ${ \mathrm { H S O } } ^ { 5 - } )$ , resulting in the generation of sulfate radical $\left( S 0 \ddagger \right)$ , which is then employed to oxidize the respective compounds [2325]. Oxidizing agents such as persulfate and peroxymonosulfate are commonly used in this technique since improve the sulfate radical's production [26]. The sulfate radical activation by an iron-based catalysts is widely used. Different types of Fe catalysts [27 30] and preparation [3136], influence the catalyst activity for the sulfate radical production.

The activation of sulfate radical process by carbon-based materials can be very interesting in the degradation of recalcitrant compounds in wastewater due to the formation of different and effective reactive oxidant species (ROs). The reaction pathway is determined by the type of ROs formed: radical pathway, non-radical pathway and their combination, as well as the catalytic performance and applicability of PDS/ PMS activation. Radicals ("OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ ) have greater oxidation capacity to degrade emerging contaminants than non-radicals $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } , \mathsf { e }$ , $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I V } } { = } \dot { 0 }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \vee } \mathrm { = } 0 \dot { . }$ . The non-radical pathway is more stable than the radical and when the radical and non-radical pathways induced by carbonbased materials occur simultaneously, the persulfate based AOP process becomes highly efficient and versatile [37,38]. When this kind of material is used, it is possible to reduce the amount of metal and ensure the entrapment of metal atoms, which limits metal leaching and avoids the formation of iron sludge, thus making unnecessary a new process to remove metals as well as any harmful impact on the environment, maintaining ecological balance and allowing the catalyst reuse. When comparing the radicals obtained by the Fenton and S-AOPs processes, it becomes apparent that the sulfate radical boasts greater stability, a longer lifespan, and superior solubility in water than to the hydroxyl radical ('OH) [39]. Since the hydroxyl radical reacts in a non-selective way over several stages, the effectiveness of the Fenton process is curtailed, resulting in some cases S-AOPs can have several advantages over the Fenton process [40]. In addition to these benefits, S-AOPs function across a broader spectrum of pH values, and their solid oxidizing agent simplifies the process's logistics [39,41]. Nonetheless, like in the Fenton process, if the homogeneous catalysts of iron are used to activate the sulfate radicals iron-rich sludge is produced, and some secondary reactions occur [42].

The main objective of this work is to investigate the physicochemical properties of solid waste from olive oil mills, specifically olive stone (OS), and pomace (OP), to produce iron-modified catalysts. These materials can work as supports to immobilize iron on the surface due to a good porous structure and high surface area combined with the presence of oxygen groups on the surface responsible for the metallic phase's anchorage and dispersion and working as catalysts for the Fenton and/or persulfate process. The activity of the synthetized materials will be compared to the traditional homogeneous Fenton process and persulfate process. Performance of the catalysts will be monitored following chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal and degradation of individual phenolic compounds (from a mixture of 5 phenolic acids). This initiative promotes the implementation of new circular economy approaches within the sector, which, in turn, reduces the cost of treatment and the environmental impact of OMW.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Materials preparation

To prepare the catalysts, the waste of olive oil production (stone and pomace) was obtained from an olive mill industry in Portugal. In the mill, the olive raw material was submitted to two extraction processes: cold pressing followed by extraction with hexane. The generated waste was washed in the lab, dried at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ , and sieved to obtain particles with diameters from 1.00 to $4 . 7 6 \ \mathrm { m m }$ for OS and from 0.60 to $4 . 7 6 \ \mathrm { m m }$ for OP. Finally, the separated particles were milled to a diameter from 177 to $8 5 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ .

The biochar was prepared through the calcination of the non-milled particles in a furnace, with a static atmosphere, at $5 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at a heating rate of $1 0 \mathrm { ^ { \circ } C } . \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Then, biochar was washed with ultra-pure water during filtration to remove ashes followed by drying at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight. These samples were named BOS or BOP, related biochar from OS or OP samples, respectively.

The biomass chemical-modified was prepared from the $3 0 { \ g }$ of milled particles with $9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of $2 . 5 \mathrm { ~ M ~ N a O H }$ solution. This mixture was kept under agitation for $^ { 1 6 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at ambient temperature, followed by washing with ultra-pure water during filtration until the collected water reached neutral pH. Then, the biomass was dried at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight. These samples were called OSM or OPM, where M is related to "modified".

The chemical-modified biochar was made as described for chemicalmodified biomass, except for the replacement of drying by calcination at $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ at a static atmosphere furnace. These samples were named BOSM or BOPM.

Hydrothermal deposition (HD) and incipient wetness (WI) impregnation of iron were applied using $\mathrm { F e S O } _ { 4 }$ 7 $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ as the precursor of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ . In HD deposition, the pH of an aqueous solution of $1 1 \ \mathrm { g F e } ^ { 2 + } . \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ was adjusted to 10 with NaOH solution and kept under stirring for $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ After that, $_ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ g ~ } }$ of non-milled material was mixed with the Fe solution in a Teflon digestion vessel and autoclaved at $2 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ Then, the samples were vacuum-filtered and dried at $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { \textrm { \scriptsize 1 2 h } }$ These samples were named with the addition of FeHD, e.g. BOSM-FeHD.

The WI was made by mixing $_ { 1 2 g }$ of non-milled biomass with $1 . 7 \ : g$ of $\mathrm { F e S O } _ { 4 }$ with enough water to cover the mixture. A manual stirring was applied until the total dissolution of the Fe precursor. This final mixture was dried at $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $1 2 \mathrm { h }$ , washed with ultra-pure water several times while vacuum filtered, dried, and calcinated at $3 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 1 h }$ at a heating rate of $1 0 ~ \mathrm { ^ { \circ } C . m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ . These samples were named with the addition of FeWI.

The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) standard was utilized to characterize the biomass. # 2.4. Analytical techniques for materials characterization

The catalyst morphology was assessed through field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) using a JEOL JSM-7610 F instrument. Chemical composition was analyzed using Raman spectroscopy at ambient temperature, employing a Thermo Scientific DXR Smart Raman spectrometer equipped with a $5 3 2 \mathrm { n m }$ laser.

The BET-specific surface area (SSA) was measured using a $^ { 3 \mathrm { ~ P ~ } }$ Instrument Micro 100 sorption analyzer with $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ gas (purity of $9 9 . 9 9 9 \%$ in the temperature of liquid $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ .This equipment facilitated comprehensive data collection encompassing BET surface area, adsorptiondesorption isotherms, and detailed pore size and volume analysis. The analysis of BET surface area and pore structure for the synthesized catalysts was conducted through the examination of nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms. This approach was complemented by employing Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) techniques, determining the distribution of pore sizes and pore volume.

# 3.5. Mechanism of Fenton and Persufate reaction

Fig. 8(a) and (b) present the Fenton activity per mass of catalyst for each acid for OS and OP catalysts. Fig. 8(c) and (d) present the Persulfate activity per mass of catalyst for each acid for OS and OP catalysts.

From Fig. 8a-d is possible to conclude that the radical formed in homogeneous Persulfate reaction is more selective to degrade some acids than the radical formed in homogeneous Fenton. This is because in FH reaction all acids are completely degraded while in PSH there is preferably degradation. This observation is supported by the mechanism of the Fenton process, with the formation of hydroxyl radicals. The hydroxyl radicals are highly active oxidizing agents and attack instability points of the structure, as double bonds (refer to Table S2), which is evidenced by the slightly greater degradability of TCA compared to the other acids. The hydroxyl radical acts mainly through electrophilic addition to the chain [68] which is formed, according to Navalon [69], because the biochar-based catalysts present surface functional groups, including hydroxyl (OH), which interacts with hydrogen peroxide during Fenton process being replaced by the hydroperoxide group (OOH). The reaction of hydroperoxide with $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ regenerates the surface 'OH on the catalysts forming the radical $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ .

In the homogeneous Persulfate reaction, the 3,4-DHB is slightly more degradable than other phenolic acids. Based on the electrostatic potential map of 3,4-DHB [70] is possible to infer that the aromatic ring is more susceptible to attack by electron abstraction by sulfate [7173] than the other phenolic acids studied in this work due to the higher positive charge in the ring. The aromatic rings in both methoxybenzoic acids studied possess a smaller positive charge over the aromatic ring than 3,4-DHB and, according to activity data, the 4-HB has the smallest aromatic ring positive charge due to fewer substitutions and absence of double bonds in the main substitute chair.

Although the radicals $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ have similar redox potential, they react chemically differently. The radical $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ is more selective and tends to attack organic compounds primarily via electron transfer, while the 'OH radical is non-selective and involves electrophilic addition to $\pi$ bonds of unsaturated and aromatic hydrocarbons, H abstraction in aliphatic hydrocarbons and electron transfers in halogenated hydrocarbons [74]. The radical $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ has higher energy barriers and selectivity than 'OH due to steric hindrance associated with its related to its larger molecular size [75]. Thus, the degradation of organic products by $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ results in quite different degradation byproducts, in this type of effluent, being the degradation of organic matter via OH is more efficient.

Also, carbon nanomaterials can activate peroxymonosulfate/persulfate (PMS/PS) through different pathways one of them the non-radical pathway involving the formation of charge transfer complex, the generation of singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ , and/or direct catalysis, which may exhibit selective degradation of organic compounds. The PS activation by graphitized nano-diamonds (G-NDs), was studied by Lee [76], involves one electron transfer from phenol (electron donor) to PS (electron acceptor), in which G-NDs surface serves to bring phenol and PS together to form a complex. Even with the indication that reactive species are produced in the presence of PMS/PS and carbon nanomaterials for the degradation of organic compounds, there is no clear interpretation of the activation mechanism.

# 4. Conclusion

Olive mill waste was used to produce catalysts for application in the Fenton and Persulfate processes to promote a synthetic OMW degradation. Iron-based biochar from OS and OP removed all five phenolic acids and almost all COD in Fenton process with activity of $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m g _ { a c i d } } . s ^ { - 1 }$ . gcat1. In heterogeneous Fenton, the Trans-cinnamic acid is the most degradable and the 4-Hydrobenzoic is the least, while in heterogeneous Persulfate, 3,4-Dyhydrobenzoic is the most degradable. The catalysts can also adsorb the acids, with the maximum adsorption capacity being $9 3 \%$ . This kind of approach reveals potential to be used as a sustainable alternative for catalyst production which can be relevant in terms of the circular economy perspective. The use of S- AOP process as an OMW treatment could be an alternative to the Fenton process because it overcomes one of the main limitations of the Fenton process, which is the need to operate at acidic pH. However, the mineralization performance of compounds in S-AOPs is a concern. The radicals $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ have similar redox potential but react show different chemical behaviour. Thus, the degradation of organic products by 'OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ results in quite different degradation byproducts, in this type of effluent, the results show that degradation of organic matter via 'OH is more efficient.
