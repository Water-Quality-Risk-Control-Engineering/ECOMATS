# 1 | Introduction

Sulfamethoxazole (SMX) is a broad-spectrum antibiotic widely used in medical and livestock farming applications for the treatment of various bacterial infections. However, its extensive use has led to growing concerns about environmental residues. SMX is chemically stable and difficult to degrade in natural environments, easily entering water bodies and soil through surface runoff, wastewater treatment plant effluent, and other pathways. Studies have confirmed the presence of SMX residues in effluent from urban wastewater treatment plants and surrounding water bodies [1]. Therefore, the development of efficient technologies to remove residual SMX and other antibiotics from water bodies is urgently needed to mitigate their potential risks to the ecological environment and human health. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) offer advantages over traditional pollutant treatment methods such as membrane separation and adsorption, including lower treatment costs, better removal efficiency, and broader application scope [2]. Their core mechanism involves generating highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \mathrm { ^ { \bullet - } }$ , ${ \mathrm { O } } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , and •OH, significantly enhancing the degradation efficiency of pollutants [2]. Among numerous oxidants, peroxymonosulfate (PMS) has become one of the main peroxides used in AOPs due to its relatively low price and efficient reaction process in the treatment of organic pollutants [1]. The technology primarily involves two reaction pathways: One is the radical pathway, dominated by sulfate radicals $( \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } { } ^ { \bullet - } )$ and hydroxyl radicals (•OH), where $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \mathrm { ^ { \bullet - } }$ exhibits a higher redox potential (2.53.1V) and longer half-life $( 3 0 - 4 0 \mu \mathrm { s } )$ [3, 4], demonstrating strong oxidative capacity; the other is the nonradical pathway, primarily involving singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ and direct electron transfer [4]. Its advantages include higher pollutant selectivity, stronger tolerance to aqueous media, and reduced formation of disinfection byproducts [5, 6].

During the advanced oxidation process of activated PMS, photocatalysis, ultraviolet light, ultrasonic activation, and thermal activation are commonly used techniques. However, these methods often have the drawbacks of harsh activation conditions and high energy consumption [7]. In contrast, the use of transition metals as PMS activators offers the advantages of simple operation and low energy consumption. Additionally, due to their good chemical reactivity with PMS, they enable faster reaction rates. However, metal materials tend to agglomerate in advanced oxidation systems, leading to reduced reaction activity [8]. By introducing carbon-based materials as carriers in material synthesis, metal particle agglomeration can be effectively prevented [9]. Compared with expensive materials such as carbon nanotubes or graphene, low-cost biochar (produced by pyrolysis of biomass) has a unique defect structure and rich oxygencontaining functional groups (such as $\mathrm { - C = O }$ ,-OH, -COOH) on its surface, which can combine with transition metals to form stable structures [10, 11]. When used as an excellent carrier, its porous structure facilitates the adsorption and enrichment of pollutants, while its surface functional groups can participate in reactions and enhance electron transfer capabilities, thereby synergistically improving catalyst performance [12].

In the field of advanced oxidation, the combination of iron (Fe), cobalt (Co), and biochar has unique advantages and plays a significant role. Fe is widely available and cost-effective and can be synthesized into iron-containing materials with different morphologies and structures using methods such as chemical precipitation and solgel techniques. In reactions, it often serves as a catalyst, generating highly oxidative •OH radicals through Fenton or Fenton-like reactions to initiate oxidative degradation. Co possesses excellent catalytic activity and stability, and its structure can be precisely controlled through methods such as hydrothermal synthesis and coprecipitation. In catalytic systems, it effectively activates PMS through redox cycling, generating active species such as $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \mathrm { ^ { \bullet - } }$ and $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ ,thereby enhancing oxidative capacity [13, 14]. As a multifunctional carrier, the pore structure of biochar improves the dispersion and stability of active components such as Fe and Co, while its surface characteristics promote the synergy between adsorption and chemical reactions, thereby enhancing the degradation efficiency of pollutants.

In this study, CFSBC (magnetic iron/cobalt codoped biochar catalyst) catalysts were prepared using a one-step calcination method. Various characterization techniques, including scanning electron microscope (SEM), X-ray diffractometer (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR), a microstructural TEM, Brunauer Emmett-Teller (BET), and X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS), were employed to systematically analyze the surface morphology and structure of the catalysts, successfully confirming the preparation of the material. The experimental results show that in a sulfamethoxazole aqueous solution with an initial concentration of $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , the reaction system involving this catalyst can achieve a pollutant removal rate of $9 3 . 0 9 \%$ , demonstrating excellent degradation performance. A comprehensive investigation was conducted into the effects of various factors, including catalyst dosage, PMS dosage, initial pH value, TC concentration, and coexisting substances, on the catalytic performance of CFSBC. The stability and universality of the catalyst were verified through degradation experiments of other antibiotic pollutants, actual water body experiments, and cyclic experiments. Combining quenching experiments with electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) analysis, the active substances ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ were identified as playing a major role in the reaction process. Additionally, using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and linear voltammetry scanning (LSV), supplemented by Bader charge analysis, it was confirmed that a nonradical pathway mediated by electron transfer exists in the CFSBC/PMS/SMX system. Finally, liquid chromatographymass spectrometry (LC-MS) was employed to analyze the reaction intermediates, proposing possible degradation pathways, and the toxicity of the degradation intermediates was assessed using T.E.S.T software. In summary, to address the environmental persistence of SMX, an advanced oxidation system based on biochar-loaded Fe/Co synergistically activated PMS was developed. Through the synergistic action of radical and nonradical pathways, this system provides a cost-effective, stable, and efficient sustainable technological solution for addressing antibiotic pollution.

# 2.2 | Preparation Process of CFSBC

Use oil pine cones as the raw material to prepare biochar. After collecting the pine cones, wash and dry them, then place them in a crusher to grind them into powder. Pass the crushed pine cone residue through an 80-mesh sieve to obtain pine cone powder. Detailed steps for synthesizing CFSBC: First, add 2-mmol $\mathrm { C o ( N O _ { 3 } ) } _ { 3 } { \bullet } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } ,$ 2-mmol $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 } { \bullet } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ , and 5-mmol $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S }$ to $8 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of deionized water. Stir thoroughly with a magnetic stirrer for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and then sonicate for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Add $2 \mathrm { g }$ of pine cone powder and repeat the stirring and sonication steps to ensure the precursor is completely mixed and homogeneous, then place the black solution in an electric heating constant temperature drying oven at $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and dry for $2 0 \mathrm { h }$ .The dried material was ground into powder and placed in a ceramic crucible. It was calcined at $8 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in a muffle furnace for $4 \mathrm { h }$ , cooled to room temperature, and then washed three times by alternating centrifugation with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol. Finally, it was thoroughly ground in a mortar to obtain a black magnetic carbon-based composite material, denoted as CFSBC. The catalyst preparation process is shown in Figure 1a.

By adjusting the molar ratio of $\mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 } { \bullet } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ , $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 } { \bullet } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { \ }$ and $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S }$ ,CFSBC with different ratios were synthesized, denoted as CFSBC $( \mathrm { C o } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { S } _ { 5 } )$ , CFSBC $( \mathrm { C o } _ { 1 } { : } \mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } { : } \mathrm { S } _ { 5 } )$ , CFSBC $( \mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } { : } \mathrm { F e } _ { 1 } { : } \mathrm { S } _ { 5 } )$ , CFSBC $( \mathrm { C o } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { S } _ { 2 . 5 } )$ , and CFSBC $( \mathrm { C o } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { S } _ { 7 . 5 } )$ . Additionally, by removing $\mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 } { \bullet } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 } { \bullet } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ , and $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S }$ ,FSBC $( \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { S } _ { 5 } )$ , CSBC $( \mathrm { C o } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { S } _ { 5 } )$ , CFBC $\left( \mathrm { C o } _ { 2 } { : } \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } \right)$ , SBC $( \mathrm { { S } } _ { 5 } )$ , and BC were prepared.

# 2.3 | Experimental Procedures

The experimental setup used a $1 5 0 \mathrm { - m L }$ stoppered conical flask containing $1 0 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of SMX solution. The flask was placed in a light-shielded constant-temperature shaker $2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ b $2 5 0 \mathrm { r p m } )$ . Variables including catalyst dosage, initial SMX concentration, initial pH, PMS dosage, and coexisting substance concentration were adjusted to evaluate their regulatory effects on PMS activation efficiency. Sampling for degradation kinetics occurred at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Collected samples were filtered through $0 . 2 2 \mathrm { - } \mu \mathrm { m }$ membranes and analyzed via UV-Vis spectrophotometry at $2 5 7 \mathrm { n m }$ to measure absorbance. SMX concentrations were calculated using the standard curve equation (Figure S1), with all data validated through triplicate experiments. To assess catalyst durability, particles were recovered post-reaction via centrifugation, then alternately washed with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol for three cycles before vacuum drying. The recycled catalyst was tested for four consecutive cycles under identical initial reaction parameters (dosage, pH, temperature) to evaluate performance degradation. Degradation curves were fitted using a pseudo-first-order kinetic model, and catalytic reaction rate constants $( k _ { \mathrm { o b s } } )$ were determined via linear regression according to Equation (1).

# 3.1 | Characterization of CFCBC

As shown in Figure 1b-e, this study used SEM/EDS mapping technology to systematically characterize the microsurface morphology and elemental distribution of CFSBC and other reference materials. Figure 1e shows that, at the 1- to $1 0 \mathrm { - } \mu \mathrm { m }$ scale, the surface of BC materials synthesized without the introduction of metal elements and sulfur elements exhibits some pores but has an overall dense structure lacking distinct porous features. In contrast, Figure 1b-d indicates that after introducing sulfur, iron, and cobalt as loading compounds, the synthesized catalyst surface exhibits a distinct honeycomb-like porous structure at the 1- to $2 0 \mathrm { - } \mu \mathrm { m }$ scale. This structure provides the catalyst with a larger specific surface area and more active sites, thereby significantly enhancing the reaction efficiency of the CFSBC/ PMS system [15]. Based on the mapping characterization results shown in Figure 1g,h, it can be observed that the elements C, O, S, Fe, and Co are uniformly distributed on the surface of the CFSBC catalyst, confirming the successful loading of the active components. Additionally, the EDS analysis in Figure 1f shows that the ratio of Co to Fe elements on the material surface is $\mathrm { C o } { \mathrm { : F e } } = 2 . 4 1 { : } 2 . 8 9$ , which is close to the initial feedstock molar ratio of $\mathrm { { m C o / m F e } }$ (1:1), indicating that CFSBC has a high raw material utilization rate during synthesis, providing important evidence for the large-scale preparation of the catalyst.

Figure 2a-f shows the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the CFSBC catalysts, revealing their microstructural features in multiple dimensions. The $5 0 \mathrm { - n m }$ resolution image further reveals a layered stacked structure inside the catalyst with a complex surface texture, which may be attributed to the coexistence of polycrystalline phases and surface defects [16]. The 5-nm resolution image focuses on the lattice structure of the catalyst. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) plots of the three materials, CSBC, FSBC, and CFSBC, are presented in Figure 3b. All three materials (CSBC, FSBC, and CFSBC) have absorption peaks near the 3440- to $3 4 5 0 \mathrm { - c m ^ { - 1 } }$ wavelength range, and this characteristic peak is usually attributed to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups (-OH), which may originate from hydroxyl groups in water molecules physically adsorbed on the surface of the material, or hydroxyl groups present in the material's own chemical structure, such as metal hydroxides (e.g., Fe-OH and Co-OH). This hydroxyl-related chemical environment suggests a hydrophilic and proton transfer capacity of the material surface, which may be involved in proton transfer reactions during catalysis and thus influence the pathway of reactive oxygen species generation [17]. The FT-IR curves of the three materials also show obvious absorption features near the wavelength range of $1 6 1 7 { - } 1 6 3 0 \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , and the absorption peaks in this region are usually associated with the telescopic vibration of $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { O }$ bonds or the H-O-H bending vibration. If attributed to the $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { O }$ bond, it may originate from the carbonyl functional group on the surface of the material, such as the $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { O }$ structure in the carbon-containing oxides or organic ligands; if attributed to the H-O-H bending vibration, it further confirms the presence of adsorbed water molecules on the surface of the material. During PMS activation, the $\mathrm { C } { = } \mathrm { O }$ bond may regulate the electron transfer process by interacting with ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ , which in turn affects the generation efficiency of reactive oxygen species (e.g. $S O _ { 4 } { ^ { \bullet - } }$ and •OH) [18]. There is a distinct absorption peak observed at $6 1 0 \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ in the CFSBC and FSBC curves, which, in combination with the composition of the catalysts (FeS vs. ${ \mathrm { C o S } } _ { 2 } )$ and the literature, may be attributed to the stretching vibration of the Fe-S and Co-S bonds in FeS and $\mathrm { C o S } _ { 2 }$ .In sulfide or sulfur-doped materials, the M-S (metalsulfur bond) vibrational peaks may be shifted from $4 0 0 { - } 5 0 0 { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { - 1 }$ to higher wavelengths of $6 1 0 \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ due to changes in the local coordination environment or differences in the sulfur oxidation state [1921]. The absorption peak in the range of $4 1 0 { - } 4 1 5 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ may be related to the vibration of metal-oxygen (M-O) bonds, which may correspond to the characteristic vibration of Fe-O or Co-O bonds. These metal-sulfur or metal-oxygen bonds may act as active sites during the activation of PMS by the catalyst and participate in the electron transfer process through the redox cycling of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } /$ $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ or $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C o } ^ { 3 + }$ , which promotes the activation of PMS and the oxidation reaction [20, 22].

Figure 3c demonstrates the Raman spectra of CFSBC, CSBC, and FSBC catalysts. Among them, the D peak $( \sim 1 3 5 0 \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } } )$ reflects the degree of defects on the catalyst surface, while the intensity of the G peak $( { \sim } 1 5 8 0 \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } } ) _ { , }$ characterizes the degree of graphitization of the material. By analyzing the Raman spectra with Gaussian fitting, the $I _ { \mathrm { D } } / I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ values were found to show the following trend: $_ { \mathrm { D } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } \ ( \mathrm { C S B C } ) { > } { I _ { \mathrm { D } } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } \ ( \mathrm { C F S B C } ) { > } { I _ { \mathrm { D } } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } }$ (FSBC). The introduction of the Co component significantly enhanced the defect degree of the catalyst, while the contribution of the Fe component to the defect degree was relatively limited [23]. In addition, the characteristic peak observed at $6 7 0 \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ can be attributed to Co monomers, confirming the presence of Co monomers in CSBC. The introduction of Co and Fe together increases the defect density on the catalyst surface, and these defect sites can significantly enhance the catalytic efficiency of the catalysts in PMS activation by accelerating the electron transfer process at the edges of carbon lattices [24].

The pore structure of the magnetic catalyst was characterized by nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms, and the results are shown in Figure 3d-e. The isotherms of all catalysts showed typical type IV adsorption characteristics accompanied by H2- type hysteresis loops, indicating the presence of a hierarchical pore structure dominated by mesopores in the materials [25, 26]. In the low relative pressure interval $( P / P _ { 0 } { < } 0 . 1 )$ , the adsorption rises sharply, indicating the rapid filling effect of the micropores $( < 2 \mathrm { n m } )$ ; when the relative pressure rises to 0.10.4, the adsorption curve shows a gradual increase, which corresponds to the process of capillary condensation of nitrogen in the mesopores $\left( 2 \mathrm { - } 5 0 \mathrm { n m } \right)$ Notably, the desorption branch showed a steep drop at $P / P _ { 0 } { > } 0 . 4$ , which, combined with the closing feature of the hysteresis loop and the pore size distribution plot (inset), further confirmed the homogeneity and openness of the mesopore structure [27]. Comparison of the structural parameters in Table S1 showed that CFSBC exhibited significant advantages in pore structure, and its specific surface area $( 3 6 3 . 9 2 0 6 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ and pore volume $( 0 . 0 9 6 8 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } / \mathrm { g } )$ were among the highest among the four groups of catalysts. The introduction of metal components not only effectively regulated the pore distribution of the carbon skeleton and significantly enhanced the mesopore ratio, but also induced the reorganization and optimization of the pore structure through the metal-carbon interface effect. This synergistic effect of high specific surface area and hierarchical pores not only provides abundant adsorption sites for PMS molecules, but also enhances the local concentration and mass transfer efficiency of reactive oxygen species through the domain-limiting effect, thus significantly improving the catalytic performance [28].

# 3.2 | Catalyst Optimization and Performance Testing

# 3.2.1 | Experiments on the Degradation of SMX by Different Systems

Based on the experimental results shown in Figure 4a, under the initial conditions of sulfamethoxazole (SMX) concentration of $1 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , catalyst injection concentration of $0 . 0 5 \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ , $\mathrm { p H } = 7 ;$ and PMS injection amount of $1 \mathrm { m M }$ , the different reaction systems exhibited significantly different catalytic efficiencies. The basic experiments showed that the single PMS system only achieved $3 4 . 3 4 \%$ SMX removal in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , while the BC/PMS system modified with biocarbon (BC) increased the removal rate to $5 3 . 9 5 \%$ , which still did not reach the ideal catalytic level.

The $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ removal of SMX by elemental doping modification was found to be $5 4 . 5 3 \%$ and $5 4 . 1 3 \%$ for the SBC/PMS and FSBC/ PMS systems, respectively, indicating that the introduction of a single sulfur/iron element did not produce a significant catalytic enhancement effect. It is worth noting that the synergistic introduction of cobalt significantly improved the catalytic performance: the SMX removal rates of CSBC/PMS and CFBC/PMS systems reached $8 4 . 5 8 \%$ and $8 7 . 2 0 \%$ , respectively. The enhancement was about $4 0 \%$ compared with the base system and about $3 0 \%$ compared with the monolithic system.

Especially noteworthy is the iron-cobalt-sulfur ternary composite doping system (CFSBC/PMS), which achieves $9 3 . 0 7 \%$ SMX removal in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , an enhancement of about $7 \%$ compared with that of the binary doping system, and an increase in kobs value of about $0 . 0 1 \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } }$ , and at the same time, it demonstrates the technical advantage in the optimization of cobalt loading. This multi-metal synergistic effect not only significantly improves the catalytic efficiency, but also reduces the unit dosage of cobalt, a precious metal, through the electronic synergistic effect between elements. Based on its excellent catalytic performance and cost-effectiveness, CFSBC was selected as the core catalyst for further investigation in this study.

While the degradation properties of different catalysts were investigated, their adsorption properties were also examined in this study as shown in Figure S2. During the $6 0 { \cdot } \mathrm { m i n }$ reaction time, the adsorption rate of SMX by each catalyst showed significant differences, and the order from low to high was as follows: CSBC $( 6 . 6 0 \% )$ , BC $( 8 . 8 4 \% )$ , SBC $( 1 2 . 9 6 \% )$ , and CFSBC $( 1 6 . 2 8 \% )$ . CFSBC was significantly higher than that of CSBC, SBC, and BC, which confirms that the synergistic modification of multimetal can enhance the density of active sites on the surface of the material with the adsorption capacity. The adsorption rate of FSBC was $2 1 . 0 4 \%$ and that of CFBC was $2 4 . 4 4 \%$ . Although the CFBC system had the highest adsorption rate $( 2 4 . 4 4 \% )$ , its degradation efficiency $( 8 7 . 2 0 \% )$ was still lower than that of the CFSBC system $( 9 3 . 0 7 \% )$ , which can be concluded that the adsorption performance is not the dominant factor of the catalytic efficacy, and that there may be a spatial competition between adsorption and catalytic active sites. Combining the previous experimental results, it can be seen that the CFSBC system achieves an optimal balance between catalytic efficiency and adsorption capacity, and its multiple active site design effectively avoids the mass transfer limitation caused by purely relying on adsorption.

This suggests that a large number of hybridized ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } 0 / { \mathrm { C } } { - } \pi$ can act as Lewis active sites to give electrons to the nucleophilic PMS, which leads to its decomposition and generation of ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ [59]. The O-M peak at position $5 3 0 . 1 8 \mathrm { e V }$ corresponds to the metal-oxygen interaction bond (O-M), and the content of O-M decreased from $1 9 . 3 8 \%$ to $1 8 . 6 8 \%$ after the reaction, indicating that the surface metal oxides were also involved in the degradation process [60].

As shown in Figure 8d, in the pre-reaction Fe 2p spectra, the peaks with binding energies of 710.9 and $7 2 4 . 6 5 \mathrm { e V }$ are attributed to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ , and the peak generation of 713.69 and $7 2 7 . 1 3 \mathrm { e V }$ is attributed to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ . The dynamic evolution of Fe 2p XPS spectra can further reveal the key role of Fe species in CFSBC in the ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ -dominated nonradical pathway. The relative intensities of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ were $6 8 . 3 \%$ and $3 1 . 7 \%$ , respectively, before the reaction, and after undergoing PMS activation $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ rose to $7 0 . 0 5 \%$ , while $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ decreased to $2 9 . 9 5 \%$ . Combined with the predominance of ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ in the system as detected by EPR and the presence of electron transfer as confirmed by LSV and the small change in valence state of Fe species in the system, it suggests that the catalyst surface does not undergo a large amount of intense metal valence cycling as in the traditional radical chain reaction, but rather, the selective activation of the PMS is achieved more through the following mechanism: $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ acts as an electron donor, and breaks the O-O bond of the PMS by electron transfer The formation of ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ [61, 62]; there is an electron buffer effect at the metal-carbon interface, and the graphitized carbon skeleton compensates for part of the oxidative demand of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ through interfacial electron transfer, suppressing its excessive accumulation and explaining the small decrease in the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ content phenomenon [63]. This dynamic equilibrium exhibited on Fe 2p also influences the production and transformation of reactive species such as $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ [64].

Based on the experimental results and the multidimensional characterization data of XPS with EPR and LSV, this study proposes the core reaction mechanism of the CFSBC/PMS system: the reaction starts with the redox reaction of metal ions with PMS. The process is rapid and efficient, which can produce large amounts of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ , $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 3 + }$ and ${ \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ in a short time, accompanied by a small amount of ${ \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ and •OH, as shown in Equations (5 [69,70]. $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ , $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 3 + }$ participate in the redox cycle by reacting with $\mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } { } ^ { - }$ as in Equations (9, 10) [7, 64]. # 3.5.4 | Theoretical Calculation

In order to deeply investigate the electron transfer mechanism during the degradation of SMX by CFSBC-activated PMS, we combined differential charge density analysis with Bader's charge quantification calculations to reveal the electron transfer pathways in the system and their relationship with the generation of reactive oxygen species. The adsorption modeling calculations shown in Figure 9a indicated that the adsorption energies (Eads) of SMX and PMS alone at the FeS site on the CFSBC surface were $- 2 . 0 6 5$ and $- 0 . 2 8 8 3 \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively, which showed a stronger intrinsic adsorption tendency of SMX. However, when SMX was co-adsorbed with PMS on the CFSBC surface, the Eads significantly decreased to $- 1 . 1 9 1 \mathrm { e V } .$ This result combined with the differential charge density analysis clearly indicated that the direct adsorption of SMX on the catalyst surface was greatly inhibited due to the preferential occupancy of PMS and its solid adsorption on the catalytic site (FeS) in the reaction.

As shown in Figure 9b-d, in order to quantify the electron transfer behavior, further Bader charge analysis showed that (1) PMS gained 0.858lel of electrons when PMS adsorbed on CFSBC. (2) SMX lost 0.093lel of electrons when SMX adsorbed on CFSBC alone (transfer to the catalyst). (3) In the key CFSBC/PMS/SMX system, SMX consistently loses 0.035lel of electrons, while PMS gains 0.879lel of electrons.

Combined with the experimental assay results, the above DFT calculations reveal the following key mechanisms. Although PMS-preferential adsorption restricts the direct contact of SMX with the catalyst surface, the SMX/CFSBC interface shown by the LSV assay possesses the strongest current exchange intensity, suggesting that the direct electron transfer from SMX to CFSBC is the most active channel. Bader charge data confirm that SMX continues to contribute a small amount of electrons to the catalyst (0.035lel) in the presence of PMS. More importantly, PMS consistently acts as an electron acceptor to acquire a significant amount of electrons from the catalyst (0.879lel at co-adsorption). This clear electron transfer pathway $( \mathrm { S M X } \to \mathrm { C F S B C } \to \mathrm { P M S } )$ is consistent with the "electron bridging" effect mentioned in the previous section; that is, the electron transfer process is an important basis for the generation of ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ in the system $( ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ can be formed through PMS activation pathways such as ${ \mathrm { P M S } } ^ { * }$ decomposition or the evolution of adsorbed oxygen species on the catalyst surface). Ultimately, EPR tests confirmed that ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ is the main reactive oxygen species for SMX degradation in this system. Thus, the core reaction mechanism is manifested as a preferential direct electron transfer from SMX to the catalyst (CFSBC), and these electrons are subsequently efficiently transferred to the adsorbed PMS molecules, which ultimately drive the production of ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ and dominate the SMX degradation process.

# 3.5.6 | Toxicity Prediction and Analysis of Intermediates

Based on the comprehensive analysis of the toxicity effect assessment of typical aquatic organisms and pollutant migration and transformation law, the degradation process of SMX by CFSBC/PMS system can effectively regulate the ecological risk. As shown in Figure 11a-c, the acute and chronic toxicity assessment of Daphnia, green algae, and fish by Ecosar modeling indicated that the EC50/LC50 and CHV values of the degradation products P6-P12 reached the harmless level in Table S3 [79], and the toxicity abatement might be originated from the disruption of the key sites of the molecular structure. However, SMX parent and intermediates such as P1, P3, P5, and P13 still showed strong toxicity to the three types of organisms, especially the inhibition of photosynthesis of green algae, which was enhanced in a dose-dependent manner, which required a targeted process for in-depth transformation. In Figure 11d-f, the conformational analysis by T.E.S.T software showed that the bioaccumulation factors of all the intermediates were significantly lower than the baseline value of 17.11 for SMX, revealing that the degradation process could effectively weaken the bioaccumulation potential of the pollutants. Mutagenicity test results showed that all compounds were negative, and in terms of developmental toxicity, the toxicity values of the intermediates were generally lower than those of SMX, and nearly half of them were no longer characterized by developmental toxicity. The comprehensive results showed that the toxicity risk of the products after the degradation of SMX by CFSBC/PMS system is generally controllable, but the subsequent treatment of a few highly toxic intermediates still needs to be concerned.

# 4 | Conclusion

In this work, magnetic CFSBC catalysts were developed by high temperature calcination based on pine cone biocarbon. By systematically optimizing the metal doping molar ratio $m _ { \mathrm { C o } } / m$ . $_ \mathrm { F e } / m _ { \mathrm { S } }$ , the ratio ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 2 } { : \mathrm { F e } } _ { 2 } { : \mathrm { S } } _ { 5 }$ was determined to be the optimal configuration, and its removal of typical pollutant SMX was as high as $9 3 . 0 9 \%$ in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . The study examined the effects of key operating parameters on SMX degradation and found that the degradation efficiency increased with increasing catalyst and PMS dosage, with excellent acid-base adaptability. Elevated initial pollutant concentrations and the presence of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ and HA inhibited the degradation. The catalyst showed good stability and reusability, and the SMX removal rate was maintained at $8 5 . 2 4 \%$ after four consecutive uses. Quenching experiments and EPR tests clarified that the main reactive oxygen species in the system was ${ } ^ { 1 } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ , and LSV confirmed that the nonradical pathway of direct electron transfer was also involved in the degradation process. In-depth reaction mechanism studies showed that the excellent performance of CFSBC originated from the effective synergy between the $\mathrm { C o ( I I ) / C o ( I I I ) }$ and Fe(II)/Fe(III) redox pairs on its surface, as well as the electron modulation provided by the sulfur-containing sites, which together efficiently activate the PMS to generate reactive oxygen species. The presence of electronic bridging in the reactive system is demonstrated by differential charge density and Bader charge analysis. The computational use of LC-MS identified 14 major intermediates generated during SMX degradation, revealing that the degradation pathways mainly consist of hydroxylation, deamidation, and demethylation reactions, which ultimately lead to the realization of small molecules. Further toxicity evaluation (using Ecosar model and T.E.S.T. software) showed that the CFSBC/PMS system could significantly reduce the acute/ chronic toxicity, bioconcentration capacity, mutagenicity, and developmental toxicity of SMX; however, it should be noted that several types of intermediates generated during the degradation process still pose certain biotoxicity risks. In summary, the present study optimized the preparation process of magnetic cobalt-iron-sulfur-doped biocarbon catalyst, significantly reduced the synthesis energy consumption and improved the stability, clarified the efficient activation mechanism of PMS and its pathway of SMX degradation and toxicity reduction, and demonstrated the good value of its application. In the future, we can further explore the deep molecular mechanism of the catalyst in the degradation process and verify the feasibility of its large-scale application.
