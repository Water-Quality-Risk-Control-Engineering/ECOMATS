# a b s t r a c t

Although metallic glasses (MGs) with unique disordered atomic structure have increasingly attracted great research interest as one of the most innovative heterogeneous catalysts in water remediation, few works focused on the synergistic role of different elemental constituents to well understand their superb catalytic performance. Herein, Co atoms were selected to partially substitute for the Fe elements in Febased MGs to study the corresponding synergistic catalytic function in dye degradation. Experimental results demonstrated that the $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 6 } C o _ { 3 6 } S i _ { 4 . 8 } B _ { 1 9 . 2 } N b _ { 4 } }$ MG with a kinetic rate constant of $k = 0 . 0 6 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ degrades rhodamine B (RhB) dye 20 times faster than the $\mathbf { F e } _ { 7 3 . 5 } \mathbf { S i } _ { 1 3 . 5 } \mathbf { B g C u } _ { 1 } \mathbf { N b } _ { 3 }$ MGs with $k = 0 . 0 0 3 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Three types of dye including RhB, methylene blue (MB) and malachite green were investigated to draw attention to the broad, practical applications. Various chemical parameters, such as catalyst dosage, persulfate concentration, and dye concentration, were also studied. Quenching experiments indicated that the highly active hydroxyl (,OH) and sulfate $( 5 0 _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - } )$ radicals are largely produced from persulfate by the activation of the $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 6 } C o _ { 3 6 } S i _ { 4 . 8 } B _ { 1 9 . 2 } N b _ { 4 } }$ MG catalyst. This critical work uncovers a new paradigm to establish an effective approach for alloy design in widespread catalytic applications.

# 1. Introduction

Metallic glasses (MGs) with their intrinsic disordered atomic structure and unique physicochemical properties have gained significant attention in various applied research fields [1e3] due to their superior strength [4] and elasticity [5], advanced anticorrosion behavior [6], excellent wear resistance [7] and toughness [8], superb fatigue performance [9], as well as their characteristic soft magnetic properties [10]. Very recently, the unique atomic-scale short-range ordered and isotopically long-range disordered structure in MGs have presented great opportunities to alter their electronic structures and enable property enhancements for catalytic applications [11e15]. For example, the unique PdeCu atomic configuration in PdNiCuP MGs delivers an optimum hydrogen proton adsorption and desorption energy for an efficient hydrogen evolution reaction [16]. However, breakthroughs have not been limited to noble metal catalysts; indeed, FeNiP MGs have been found to offer abundant unsaturated active sites to improve the oxygen evolution reaction [17]. Additionally, ball-milled FeSiBNb [18] and $\mathtt { M g Z n C a }$ [19] MG powders exhibit 200- and 1000- times higher dye degradation efficiency than their corresponding crystalline counterparts. Besides that, our recent report demonstrated the role of $\mathrm { P }$ atoms play in FeSiBPC MGs for effectively tuning the electronic structure at the Fermi level to promote electron delocalization for enhanced dye degradation activity [20]. To highlight the importance of the amorphous structure in relation to the catalytic activity, we compared the performance of annealed and partially crystallized FeSiB MGs with fully amorphous glassy ribbons for various dye purification applications [21e23]. The results indicated that the MGs with less ordered atomic coordination relative to their crystalline counterparts present better catalytic activity due to the electrons experiencing less constraint for activation [22]. Although full crystallization has been shown in some cases to partially recover some of the catalytic performance via a different mechanism [21], the amorphous structure of MGs clearly contributes to the alteration of the electronic structure and enhanced performance relative to crystalline catalysts. However, the further development of MG catalysts will require an understanding of how to regulate their electronic structure through compositional control of the different metal elements, which is still poorly understood.

Recent reports have demonstrated Fe- [24], Co- [25], Ni- [26], Mg- [19], and Al-based [27] MGs with superb catalytic performance including efficiency, stability and low metal leaching effects for various water treatment application. Accordingly, the wide range of base chemical components presents an important opportunity for MGs. However, with respect to the synergistic function of different metal element combinations in MGs, the current understanding is still very limited. For instance, it was found that the addition of Ni and Cr into the FeeSieB MGs can largely reduce their acid orange II degradation ability [28]. In contrast, the methylene blue (MB) degradation efficiency was enhanced by the slight addition of Co into FeCoPCCu MGs [29]. Therefore, further research efforts for the development of MG catalysts should be concentrated on how to precisely regulate their atomic compositions to improve their catalytic activity for water treatment.

As water quality is deteriorating worldwide, particularly in urbanized industrial centers, the development of effective catalytic technologies for water treatment has attracted significant recent research attention. When considering the wide range of catalytic methods, such as photocatalysis [30e33], ozone oxidation [34e36], etc., Fenton/Fenton-like approaches [37e40] are emerging as excellent candidates to purify various types of toxic organic compounds in water because of the fast activation of peroxides. In addition to the traditional iron salt and hydrogen peroxide Fenton system, persulfate (PS) activation [41e43] with a fast generation of hydroxyl radicals (,OH) and sulfate radicals $( \mathsf { S } 0 _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - } )$ is also regarded as an effective alternative in catalytic water treatment [44,45]. In this work, we developed a new MG catalyst with a nominal elemental composition of $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 6 } C o _ { 3 6 } S i _ { 4 . 8 } B _ { 1 9 . 2 } N b _ { 4 } }$ for efficient PS activation to degrade various organic dyes in water. The initial purpose is to emphasize the synergistic function of different transition metal elements through the substitution of Fe to Co element (\~half percent) in $\mathbf { F e } _ { 7 3 . 5 } \mathbf { S i } _ { 1 3 . 5 } \mathbf { B g C u } _ { 1 } \mathbf { N b } _ { 3 }$ MGs. To achieve this goal, three organic dyes with different chemical structures were selected as target pollutants. Moreover, the influence of various parameters on dye degradation were systematically investigated. The catalytic mechanism of PS activation was also studied by quenching experiments.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Materials

Raw materials comprising of Fe, Co, Si, B, Nb, Cu with a high purity of $9 9 . 9 \%$ were used for the preparation of master alloy ingots. The target organic dyes, including rhodamine B (RhB), methylene blue (MB), and malachite green were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Ammonium persulfate $( ( \mathrm { N H } _ { 4 } ) _ { 2 } \mathrm { S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } )$ was supplied from BDH Chemicals Ltd. Ethanol (EtOH) and tertiary butanol (TBA) at an analytical grade with a concentration of $\mathbf { 0 . 5 \ : M }$ were employed for quenching tests. Milli-Q water $. 1 8 . 2 \mathrm { M } \Omega \mathrm { c m } .$ was used throughout this work for diluting the aqueous dye solutions and other highly concentrated chemicals.

# 2.2. Materials preparation

Two MGs with nominal atomic compositions of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 6 } \mathrm { C o } _ { 3 6 } .$ $\mathsf { S i } _ { 4 . 8 } \mathsf { B } _ { 1 9 . 2 } \mathsf { N b } _ { 4 }$ and $\mathbf { F e } _ { 7 3 . 5 } \mathbf { S i } _ { 1 3 . 5 } \mathbf { B g C u } _ { 1 } \mathbf { N b } _ { 3 }$ were manufactured by our previously reported physical metallurgical method [46,47], and a brief summary of the relevant procedures will be given here. The two types of MGs are denoted as FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb, respectively, throughout this work. The addition of $1 \% \ \mathrm { C u }$ in the $\mathbf { F e } _ { 7 3 . 5 } \mathrm { S i } _ { 1 3 . 5 } \mathrm { B g C u } _ { 1 } \mathrm { N b } _ { 3 }$ MGs was considered to enhance the glass forming ability. We assumed that such a tiny addition of Cu has a limited function for the catalytic performance, and thus, the synergistic function of Fe and Co in the FeCoSiBNb MG will be the focus for this investigation. In addition, in comparison with FeSiBCuNb MG, the higher atomic composition of B in FeCoSiBNb MG may also play important contributions to the catalytic activity. Master alloys $\left( \sim 2 0 \mathrm { ~ g ~ } \right.$ mass) with the desired elemental compositions were initially prepared by arc-melting under a Ti-gettered high-purity Ar atmosphere. To ensure the raw materials achieved a homogeneous distribution, the prepared master alloys were re-melted at least for four times. The master alloy was then cut into several pieces to fit in a quartz crucible and further re-melted at a temperature of ${ \sim } 1 5 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } C$ under an Ar atmosphere. Afterwards, the metal liquids were immediately ejected under pressure onto a single Cu wheel surface with a rotating speed of ${ \sim } 3 0 \mathrm { m } / s$ to achieve rapid cooling to room temperature. The manufactured MGs with $2 0 - 3 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ thickness and $0 . 2 \mathrm { c m }$ width of were further cut into $\sim 2 \mathrm { c m }$ lengths for the subsequent catalytic experiments in this work.

# 2.3. Materials characterization

The microstructure of the as-spun MG was investigated by a high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (JEOL TEM 2100F) equipped with selected area electron diffraction (SAED) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The TEM specimens were prepared by the lift-out method and annular milling in a FEI Scios focused ion beam/scanning electron microscope (FEI FIB/SEM). A scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JEOL JSM-820) coupled with EDS was employed for observing the surface morphology of the MG samples both before and after catalytic experiments. The amorphous structure of the as-spun MGs was confirmed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Rigaku SmartLab) with $C u \mathrm { \ K } \mathfrak { a }$ radiation and differential scanning calorimetry (PerkinElmer Diamond DSC) under purified Ar flow. The surface elemental valence states of were determined for the as-spun MGs using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) coupled with $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { r } ^ { + }$ depth-profile sputtering (VG ESCALAB 220i-XL) and Al $K _ { \alpha }$ radiation. Polarization curves were measured and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed for the as-spun MGs using a standard three-electrode electrochemical station (AutoLab PGSTAT $3 0 2 ~ \mathrm { N }$ Potentiostat) comprising of the MG catalyst as the working electrode, a Pt sheet as the counter electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference, respectively, in a simulated dye solution (RhB concentration of 20 ppm and persulfate (PS) concentration of $2 \ \mathrm { m M }$ ).

# 2.4. Catalytic activity

The catalytic investigation throughout this work was based on the Fenton-like oxidation reaction. Specifically, the as-spun MGs performed as the catalysts to activate persulfate for generating highly reactive transitory radicals, and eventually, for the degradation of organic pollutants such as RhB, MB and malachite green dye. During the catalytic process, a thermostatic water bath was employed to control the reaction temperature (e.g. 298, 303.0, 313.0, 323.0, and 333.0 K). A $2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ beaker containing $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ aqueous dye solution was placed in the water bath under continuous mechanical stirring. Except where mentioned to be different, the standard experimental conditions included a catalyst dosage of $0 . 5 { \mathrm { g } } / { \mathrm { L } }$ , a PS concentration of $2 \mathrm { m M }$ , a dye concentration of 20 ppm, and a reactive temperature of $2 9 8 \ \mathrm { K } .$ To enable determination of UVevis absorption spectra, $4 \mathrm { m L }$ of dye solution was removed from the beaker at time intervals of 0, 5, 10, 15, 30, 45, 60 min. Each experiment was repeated three times with fresh ribbon samples to ensure repeatability. The maximum absorbance peaks of the three dyes were at $5 5 4 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ of RhB, $6 6 4 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ of MB, and $6 1 7 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ of malachite green, respectively. Experiments were also conducted to determine the loss of performance during reuse of the catalysts. For these experiments, prior to reusing the MG catalyst samples they were ultrasonically washed by ethanol and Milli-Q water for three times and preserved in absolute ethanol until retesting. # 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Microstructures and surface morphology

Fig. 1 a and b show HRTEM images with the corresponding SAED patterns (insets) of the as-spun FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb MG ribbons, respectively, indicating their fully amorphous structure. This was also confirmed by the XRD measurements (Fig. 1c) which showed broad diffraction peaks and by the DSC results shown in Fig. 1d. According to the DSC results, two exothermic peaks were detected for the FeSiBCuNb MG, corresponding to crystallization processes at temperatures $\left( T _ { c } \right)$ of 525.4 and $5 4 5 . 1 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . However, only one exothermic peak at $T _ { c } = 5 8 8 . 6 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ was observed for FeCoSiBNb MGs. Fig. 2 presents the surface morphology and the corresponding EDS elemental mapping results for the two as-spun MGs demonstrating that they both had very smooth surfaces and homogeneous elemental distributions.

# 3.2. Surface chemical valent states

To reveal the surface chemical valent states in relation to the catalytic performance, XPS analysis of the as-spun MGs was performed as shown in Fig. 3. The full range XPS spectra in Fig. 3a present the coexistence of Fe, Co, Si, B, Nb, and adventitious C elements through alloying in both FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb MGs.

![](images/aea751b16930f7645ffc7658c869751bacc06926b404eb7bd54aaef440a7e134.jpg)  
Fig. 4. a) Polarization curves and b) EIS measurements of as-spun FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb MGs.

The Cu signal in FeSiBCuNb MGs is relatively weak due to its small quantity of atomic compositions with a low distribution on the ribbon surface. The Fe 2p high-resolution XPS spectra can be divided into three primary peaks at binding energies of $7 0 7 . 0 / 7 0 6 . 9$ , 710.8/710.9 and 713.2/713.6 eV, which are typically assigned to be $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 0 } ,$ , $\dot { \mathrm { F e } } ^ { 2 + }$ , and $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ , respectively, indicating that both FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb MGs coexist the metallic Fe and oxidized Fe species on their surface [23,46] (Fig. 3b). It is noteworthy that the $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ binding energy of the FeCoSiBNb MGs presents a slightly negative shift compared to that of FeSiBCuNb MGs, suggesting the Fe sites in FeCoSiBNb MGs are prefer to accept the hydroxyl in water/air that likely promotes the catalytic activity [17]. Fig. 3c shows the Co 2p XPS spectra in the FeCoSiBNb MGs. The peaks at 778.2, 781.1 and $7 8 5 . 2 \mathrm { e V }$ correspond to metallic ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 0 }$ and the oxidized $C 0 ^ { 2 + }$ and $C 0 ^ { 3 + }$ species on the free surface, indicating electron transfer phenomenon of oxidized Co species originated from oxidation of ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 0 }$ [52]. This result further implies that the coexistence of Fe and Co elements in FeCoSiBNb MGs through alloying is likely to synergistically promote the electron transport for finally enhancing the catalytic performance. With respect to the chemical states of Si and B elements (Fig. 3 d and e), it is noted that the bonding energies of SieSi and BeB on FeCoSiBNb surface exhibit a slightly negative shift compared to those of FeSiBCuNb MGs, e.g., from 99.5 to $9 9 . 2 \mathrm { e V }$ for SieSi bond and from 187.8 to 187.6 eV for $\mathtt { B - B }$ bond, respectively. The slight difference of the binding strength through alloying might give rise to alter the electronic structure on the catalyst surface that to improve the reaction kinetics for dye degradation [53]. The Nb 3d for FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb MGs with the binding energies at 203.2/203.0, 205.8/206.2, 207.0/207.4 and $2 0 9 . 8 / 2 1 0 . 0 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ are corresponding to the oxidized Nb species (Fig. 3f). In addition to the aforementioned alloying effects on the peak shifting, it is found that ${ \tt N b } _ { 2 } { \tt O } _ { 5 }$ is the major oxidative layer on the FeCoSiBNb surface, however, $\mathsf { N b O } _ { 2 }$ is the main layer on the FeSiBCuNb MGs, depending on the integral areas of the $\mathsf { N b } ^ { 4 + }$ and $\mathsf { N b } ^ { 5 + }$ positions. The Cu 2p XPS spectra in Fig. $_ { 3 \mathrm { g } }$ indicate that the Cu element on FeSiBCuNb surface is mainly in metallic zero valent state $( \mathsf { C u } ^ { 0 } )$ at the binding energy of $9 3 2 . 7 \mathrm { e V }$ [16], demonstrating the electrons around $\mathtt { C u }$ elements are less active than the Fe atoms, and further evidencing that the function of Cu in FeSiBCuNb MGs is for improving the glass forming ability in this work. The O 1s XPS spectra with peaks at 529.9 and $5 3 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ corresponds to the oxides and hydroxides, respectively (Fig. 3h). The higher integral area at the position of $5 3 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ on FeCoSiBNb surface indicates the better synergistic adsorption function of Fe and Co elements for hydroxides, which usually implies as the active sites for catalysis [54].

Large current density and low charge transfer resistance of a catalyst is always a great indicator to predict the catalytic activity. Fig. 4 a and b show the polarization curves and EIS measurements for the as-spun MGs. Notably, the FeCoSiBNb MG with the Co element addition presents a significant enhancement of the current density compared with the FeSiBCuNb MG at similar potential (Fig. 4a). In addition, the EIS curve of the FeCoSiBNb MG exhibits a much smaller semicircle diameter compared to the FeSiBCuNb MG in the simulated aqueous dye solution, revealing the electron transfer ability is enhanced because of the synergistic function of Fe and Co elements and higher compositional value of B.

# 3.3. Catalytic performance

To elucidate the enhanced catalytic performance of FeCoSiBNb MG, we employed aqueous RhB solution with a concentration of 20 ppm as the target pollutant to comparatively study the catalytic activity between FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb MGs. As shown in Fig. 5a, the FeCoSiBNb MG demonstartes nearly $1 0 0 \%$ RhB decolorization efficiency at $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , which represents a significant catalytic improvement compared to the FeSiBCuNb MG with only $2 0 \%$ RhB decolorization efficiency at $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . It is presumed that the $1 \%$ addition of Cu to improve the glass forming ability has a very limited effect on the catalytic activity compared to the transition metal elements of Fe and Co. Consequently, through the comparison of experimental results in Fig. 5a, we clearly demonstrate an important synergistic effect of Fe and Co elements in the FeCoSiBNb MG for RhB decolorization. The function of Co incorporation in Febased MG suggests strong electronic interactions [55] and interdiffusion effects [56], providing valuable information in selection of metal elements in MG catalysts design. In addition, it was reported that amorphous boron catalysts have excellent peroxides activation activity in wastewater treatment [57,58], demonstrating a significant contribution of higher boron contents in FeCoSiBNb MG for RhB degradation in this work. The UVeVis spectra of RhB dye decolorization using the FeCoSiBNb MG is shown in Fig. 5 inset. The maximum absorbance peak $\left( \lambda _ { m a x } \right)$ of the RhB dye corresponding to the chromophore bond is located at $\lambda _ { m a x } = 5 5 4 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ under the visible spectrum region. It was found that the $\lambda _ { m a x }$ intensity gradually declined until it was completely eliminated after $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of the catalytic process, indicating that the organic structures were destroyed by the strongly oxidizing radicals. In order to provide a comprehensive understanding of RhB decolorization behavior for the FeCoSiBNb MG, its dependence on different catalytic parameters was further investigated, with results shown in Fig. 5 b-e. As shown in Fig. 5b, increasing the FeCoSiBNb MG dosage from 0 to $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { g / L }$ dramatically enhanced the RhB decolorization efficiency at $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ from up to $\sim 1 0 0 \%$ . The effect of PS concentration on RhB decolorization is shown in Fig. 5c. It is worth noting that the catalytic performance without the addition of PS is almost negligible as indicated by a RhB decolorization efficiency of less than $1 0 \%$ even after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ during the dye degradation experiment. However, a minor PS addition of $0 . 5 { - } 2 . 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ markedly improves the RhB decolorization efficiency from $7 0 \%$ to $1 0 0 \%$ at 60 min. Such results indicate that the function of MG catalysts is to activate PS for the radical generation. The dye concentration is another important parameter affecting the dye decolorization efficiency. As shown in Fig. 5d, the FeCoSiBNb MG maintains excellent decolorization efficiency in a highly concentrated RhB dye solution of 20 ppm that is comparable to the low concentration solution of 5 ppm, indicating the great potential for practical industry applications. Fig. 5e shows the effect of the reactive temperature on RhB decolorization efficiency. It can be seen that increasing the reactive temperature speeds up the catalytic reaction kinetics as the external thermal energy assists the chemical reaction to overcome the reaction barrier. As shown in Fig. 5f, the $E _ { a }$ values obtained for the FeCoSiBNb and FeSiBCuNb MGs in this work are 33.35 and $3 7 . 4 9 ~ \mathrm { k J / m o l }$ , respectively, which are much lower than the nanocrystalline zero-valent Fe (NZVI) with an $E _ { a }$ value of $6 5 . 2 8 ~ \mathrm { k J / m o l }$ , demonstrating the MG catalysts require less energy for the catalytic reaction compared to the NZVI Fenton catalysts. To reveal the great potential of the FeCoSiBNb MG for a broader range of industrial applications, various other organic dyes were also tested in this work. As shown in Fig. 6, the FeCoSiBNb MG present excellent catalytic activity for RhB, MB and malachite green dye degradation. It is worthwhile to note that malachite green dye with a concentration of 20 ppm can be completely decolorized under mild reactive conditions within $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ (catalyst dosage of $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L } ,$ PS concentration of $2 \ \mathrm { m M }$ and reactive temperature of 298 K). The catalytic performance of our FeCoSiBNb MG is compared with that of other Fe-based MGs under various reactive conditions as summarized in Table 1. By comparison, our FeCoSiBNb MG presents a faster kinetics with a larger value of $k$ for various dye decolorization, further indicating the significance of the synergistic function of the Fe and Co metal elements and higher B composition in the FeCoSiBNb MG.

Fig. 7a presents the catalytic durability of the FeCoSiBNb MG for RhB degradation. It was observed that the RhB decolorization can be well-maintained at around $80 \%$ at $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ even after the 4th use of the FeCoSiBNb MG. To further understand the excellent stability, XRD and SEM analysis was performed to evaluate the structural and surface morphological evolution for the reused FeCoSiBNb MG. As shown in Fig. 7b, the XRD patterns for both the FeSiBCuNb and FeCoSiBNb MGs still shows a broad diffraction peak without any Bragg crystalline diffraction, confirming that the amorphous structure remains after being reused four times. SEM revealed an evolution in the surface morphology for the FeCoSiBNb MG, showing a large number of long cracks are formed on the surface of the reused MG samples (Fig. 8). According to our previous work [46,59], such cracks are owing to the formation of ${ \sf N b } _ { 2 } { \sf O } _ { 5 }$ surface layers during the dye degradation experiments. An intact stable ${ \sf N b } _ { 2 } { \sf O } _ { 5 }$ layer will decrease the contact area between PS and the active metal elements so that the catalytic activity reduces; however, cracking provides fresh contact to active metal sites and enables the MG to maintain $8 0 \%$ effectiveness even after 4 runs.

To study the corresponding catalytic mechanism in more detail, TBA and EtOH with a concentration of $\mathbf { 0 . 5 \ : M }$ were employed as the ,OH and $S O _ { 4 } { \cdot } ^ { - }$ scavengers, respectively, during the dye decolorization process [41] (Fig. 9). Clearly, the RhB decolorization efficiency is severely reduced after the addition of either TBA or EtOH, from $\sim 1 0 0 \%$ to $5 2 \%$ or $4 8 \%$ , respectively, at $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Furthermore, only $1 8 \%$ of RhB decolorization can be achieved after the addition of both TBA and EtOH. Such results provide evidence that the generation of ,OH and $S O _ { 4 } { \cdot } ^ { - }$ radicals play significant roles in the degradation of organic structure in dye pollutants. Additionally, it was found that the RhB decolorization efficiency only has a slight decline after adding the EtOH in the initial $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , however, a remarkable influence is observed very quickly after TBA addition. It is noteworthy that both of the metallic Fe and Co elements on the FeCoSiBNb MG surface are capable to act as an electron donor for the PS activation through a four-electron transfer mechanism [60], while in the FeSiBCuNb MG, only Fe element is contributed to the PS activation. The electrochemical measurements including polarization curves and EIS results in Fig. 4 indicate that the FeCoSiBNb MG presents a better electron transfer ability compared to the FeSiBCuNb MG, demonstrating the synergistic function of the Fe and Co elements is an intrinsic treasure in the FeCoSiBNb MG. In addition, as shown in Fig. 5, separate addition of PS or MGs only cause a negligible RhB degradation even at $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , indicating the produced radicals are the only oxidants for chemical bonds degradation. Accordingly, the significant improvement of RhB degradation by using FeCoSiBNb MGs is mainly owing to the promoted radical production rates caused by the synergistic function of Fe and Co elements. Several similar reports have been demonstrated the synergistic function by different bimetallic complex for the enhanced catalytic performance. For example, the binary Fe and Cu elements could facilitate the $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 2 + } /$ $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ cycle and excite the $\mathsf { C u } ^ { 2 + } / \mathsf { C u } ^ { + }$ redox for promoting the ,OH radicals generation [61]. # 4. Conclusion

In this work, we explored a multicomponent and low-cost $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 6 } C o _ { 3 6 } S i _ { 4 . 8 } B _ { 1 9 . 2 } N b _ { 4 } }$ metallic glass (MG) as an efficient heterogeneous environmental catalyst for water remediation. The $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 6 } C o _ { 3 6 } S i _ { 4 . 8 } B _ { 1 9 . 2 } N b _ { 4 } }$ MG with a fully amorphous structure was prepared by a simple and scalable physical metallurgical method. Compared to the $\mathrm { F e _ { 7 3 . 5 } S i _ { 1 3 . 5 } B g C u _ { 1 } N b _ { 3 } \mathrm { M C } }$ , the $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 6 } C o _ { 3 6 } S i _ { 4 . 8 } B _ { 1 9 . 2 } N b _ { 4 } }$ MG with partial substitution of Fe elements by Co atoms and higher boron content presents 20 times higher kinetic rate $( k )$ for rhodamine (RhB) dye degradation, which is mainly attributed to the effective alteration of electronic structure by the synergistic function of Fe and Co metal elements and increment of boron content. Moreover, the $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 6 } C o _ { 3 6 } S i _ { 4 . 8 } B _ { 1 9 . 2 } N b _ { 4 } }$ MG catalyst also exhibits superior catalytic performance for the purification of various organic pollutants with only a slight addition of persulfate, indicating its great potential for practical applications. The results in this work provide a new innovative strategy for metallic catalyst design for widespread catalytic/electrocatalytic applications.
