# Article Recommendations

ABSTRACT: Photocatalysis is an effective and environmentally friendly approach for degrading organic pollutants, particularly in scenarios where sunlight can be utilized as the energy source. Opportunities are emerging to apply materials and methods from photocatalytic pollutant degradation to address the challenge of fouling. Membrane fouling, attributed to organic foulants, is a prevalent problem for all membrane-based technologies and represents a major deleterious impact on membrane performance. Integration of tactics developed in photocatalysis more broadly to membranes reveals new strategies for membrane fouling control—an approach taken by an increasing number of researchers. This review summarizes key developments in photocatalytic materials and methods in water treatment and presents recent progress in the development of processes for photocatalytic alleviation of membrane fouling, including photocatalyst design and modification strategies aimed at enhancing photocatalytic efficiency, as well as different configurations of photocatalysis—- membrane systems (PMS). Perspectives on future research and development opportunities for photocatalytic membrane fouling control are also provided.

# 1INTRODUCTION

Photocatalysis is an appealing and sustainable approach to contaminant decomposition in water, as it is environmentally friendly, using oxygen and water as raw materials and, even, potentially sunlight as the energy source. Moreover, the photocatalytic products are often harmless, thereby avoiding the generation of secondary pollution.' In parallel, membranebased separations have attracted increasing attention since they first appeared in the early 20th century, becoming one of the most promising technologies in wastewater treatment, as well as purification and refining of industrial products.2 Among membrane-based separations, porous polymer membrane materials play an important role because of their good processability and low cost.6 The separation scale of membranes can reach the molecular level, and there is typically no need for a phase change or additive use during the process, greatly reducing overall energy consumption and allowing for relatively simple scale-up.7 However, during the separation process, as feed solution is filtered by the membrane, large impurities are rejected and at the same time pollutants (e.g. dissolved organic matter, soluble inorganic compounds, suspended particles, and microorganisms) are trapped and accumulated on the membrane surface or within membrane pores, causing membrane fouling.7,8 Membrane fouling is arguably the most intractable challenge in membrane separation processes, and a typical outcome is reduction in membrane perm-selectivity, lowering separation efficiency, and shortening membrane lifetime.

Membrane fouling can be categorized as reversible fouling or irreversible fouling. The cause of the former is usually the formation of a loose cake layer and concentration polarization at the membrane surface, and the loss of transmembrane flux can be recovered by physical or chemical cleaning. The latter occurs when foulants adsorb in membrane pore channels or on the membrane surface through strong interactions with membrane materials, and flux usually cannot be restored physically or chemically.8 Although process intensification, physical/chemical cleaning, and replacement in inefficient membrane modules have been applied to mitigate membrane fouling, each of these is associated with significant increase in capital costs. Thus, it is crucial to find low-cost alternative routes to alleviate membrane fouling. Advances in photocatalysis provide us with opportunities to integrate selfcleaning property into membrane operation, during which accumulation of foulants could be degraded, thereby enabling long-term operation.6

The process of photocatalytic mitigation of pollutants is as follows (Figure 1): first, electrons in the VB are excited to the CB when a photocatalyst is irradiated by photons with higher energy than its band gap, leaving holes in the VB; then, the negatively charged electrons and positively charged holes separate and move to the surface of the photocatalyst to participate in reactions with $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ or $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and generate various ROS, which include hydroxide radicals, superoxide radicals, and hydrogen peroxide; ultimately, foulants attached to the surface of the photocatalyst are degraded by those generated ROS, ideally into $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ and $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and mineral acids.10 During the processes of photocatalytic degradation, recombination of electron-hole pairs and the limitation of solar energy utilization are two factors that hinder the high-efficiency degradation of pollutants, which jeopardize the mitigation efficiency of membrane fouling. Therefore, boosting the efficiency of photocatalytic degradation of pollutants is of great importance in membrane-based technology.

2.1. Conventional Photocatalysts Based on $\mathbf { \bar { I i O } } _ { 2 }$ . Titanium dioxide $\left( \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } \right)$ is currently the most widely studied photocatalyst due to its low cost, stable chemical structure, as well as physical and chemical properties, compared with alternatives such as CdS and ${ \mathrm { Z n O } } _ { \cdot }$ which have serious $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ has been successfully introduced into membrane systems via two tactics: PMS with suspended $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and PMS with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ membranes. In both routes, the photocatalytic degradation of contaminants for membrane fouling alleviation with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ has achieved positive results, as shown in Table 1. In addition to oxidative degradation of foulants by photogenerated ROS, the lightinduced hydrophilicity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ also contributes to the antifouling property of membranes.15 When a membrane is coated with a $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ film, oxygen vacancies are created under UV irradiation at the surface in which chemically adsorbed water molecules dissociate to form hydroxyl ions, enhancing the hydrophilicity of the membrane. However, narrow light absorption due to the wide band gap and high electron—hole recombination rate of pure $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ limit its large-scale application.16 Even pure $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ can exist with various morphologies having different band gaps and efficiencies of charge separation. $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanosheets have higher conduction band minimum compared with nanotubes and nanoparticles, for example, so that more electrons could be involved in $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ reduction and produce more reactive species for the redox reaction of foulants.17 Moreover, $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanosheets and nanotubes often have longer lifetime of photogenerated electrons and holes than $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles. Therefore, the photocatalytic activity of low-dimensional nano- $\cdot \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ typically ranks in the order of nanosheets $>$ nanotubes $>$ nanoparticles. The phototoxicity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ with different nanostructures to bacteria is in the opposite order.18 These findings emphasize the importance of morphological choice in specific applications of photocatalysis. To further improving the photocatalytic efficiency of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ for pollutants decomposition, several modification methods have been proposed and developed, which will be discussed in this subsection.

2.1.1. Loading with Noble Metal Nanoparticles. Incorporating noble metal (e.g., Au, Ag, Pb, Pt) nanoparticles with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ is an effective way to boost its photocatalytic efficiency. For instance, Li et al. demonstrated that the rate constants of perfluorinated compound decomposition by Pt, Pd, and $\mathbf { A g - }$ modified $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ are 12.5, 7.5, and 2.2 times higher than that by pure $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ respectively.28 The origin for this enhancement has two aspects: the formation of a Schottky barrier and the SPR effect.29-31 Specifically, the Fermi level of noble metals is usually located at more positive position than the CB of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ so the photogenerated electrons in $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ are likely to be transferred to metal nanoparticles, facilitating the inhibition of charge recombination in the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ . As a result, more electrons and holes are available to participate in the oxidative degradation of foulants. Since noble metals act as intermediaries in electron transfer, the Fermi level of which plays a vital role in interfacial electron transfer, a noble metal with higher Fermi level would be expected to have stronger enhancement of photocatalytic efficiency.32

When the size of noble metal nanoparticles, which have a large number of free electrons on their surface, is controlled at the nanoscale, they can absorb UV-visible light and exhibit SPR, which can enhance the electric field near the metal nanoparticles, as well as produce local heat. The enhancement of electric field can improve band gap absorption of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ creating more electron—hole pairs, while the local heat effect boosts the rate of thermocatalytic reactions.33 Moreover, the resulting polarization effect enables more molecules to be adsorbed on the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ surface, which also increases the overall photocatalytic activity. As the size of noble nanoparticles increases, their SPR is enhanced, thus the electric field enhancement effect and local heat effect are significantly enhanced, which is advantageous for the improvement of photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ However, another effect induced by SPR, SPR sensitization, is detrimental to photocatalytic activity. SPR sensitization takes place when the electrons in the noble metal excited by SPR transfer to the CB of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ which would hinder the charge separation of electron-hole pairs generated by band gap excitation and result in a decrease of photocatalytic efficiency. To overcome this issue, Yoo et al. modified $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ photocatalyst with $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ coated Au nanoparticles.3 The introduction of a thck $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ shell can prevent the nonideal transfer of electrons from Au nanoparticles to $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ Thus, SPR sensitization is suppressed and the local heating effect and electric enhancement effect become the dominant factors in alteration of photocatalytic activity.

In addition to size, the photocatalytic performance of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ is also dependent on the shape of noble metal nanoparticles. Soua-Castillo et al investigate the effect of shape anisotroy of Au nanoparticles on the photocatalytic activity of Au$\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ 35 Compared to Au nanospheres and nanorods, Au nanostar-loaded $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ exhibits higher photocatalytic efficiency, which is attributed to its ability to enhance the electromagnetic field around the spikes, leading to an increased generation of hot electrons on the interface between Au and the semiconductor. Those hot electrons can activate the oxygen molecules on the surface of the photocatalyst and thus promote oxidation reactions.

$\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g }$ nanoparticles are generally considered as an effective modifier that can improve activity of photocatalysts, not only because their Fermi level is located near the CB of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ which enables them act as electron traps to promote charge tranr,butandb properties.37 In aqueous solution, $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g }$ nanoparticles partially dissolve and release silver ions. Those silver ions subsequently interact with the bacteria adsorbed on membranes, breaking their cell structures and ultimately inactivating them.38,39

Consequently, $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g }$ nanoparticles show remarkable performance in improving photocatalytic efficiency of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and inhibiting the growth of proliferative bacteria. Salazar et al. fabricated a photocatalytic membrane by incorporating Ag-functionalized $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles into poly-(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene)(PVDF-HFP) matrix through solvent casting and electrospinning methods, respectively.40 The as-prepared PVDF-HFP $\mathrm { \Delta } / \mathrm { A g } { - } \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ membrane showed $8 0 . 7 \%$ degradation efficiency of norfloxacin under visible light because of the reduced band gap by $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g }$ modification, while the unmodified $\mathrm { P V D F - H F P / T i O } _ { 2 }$ exhibited only $1 0 \%$ .Moreover, Li et al. reported a bimetallic $\mathrm { A u / A g { - } T i O _ { 2 } }$ membrane with enhanced photocatalytic degradation performance for antibiotics and improved antifouling property (Figure 2).41 The degradation rate of tetracycline by the $\mathrm { A u / A g { - } T i O _ { 2 } }$ membrane was higher than that of the either of the respective monometallic nanoparticle-modified $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ $\left( \operatorname { A u } - \operatorname { T i O } _ { 2 } \right.$ or ${ \mathrm { A g - T i O } } _ { 2 } ,$ membranes under visible light irradiation. The bacterial viability of Escherichia coli incubated with $\mathrm { A u / A g { - } T i O _ { 2 } }$ membranes was reduced to $1 . 5 \%$ after irradiation by visible light for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n } ,$ which was ascribed to the intrinsic bactericidal property of $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g }$ nanoparticles and the photogenerated ROS.

Noble metals have been demonstrated as efficient catalytic materials, suggesting promise in membrane applications. However, their scarcity leads to high cost and limits to scalability. To address this issue, a number of researchers have explored alternative methods to improving $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ performance. 2.1.2. Doping with Nonmetal Elements. Compared with metal doping, which risks thermal instability of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ photocatalysts and provides carrier recombination centers, doping with nonmetal elements such as N, S, B, P, C, and F has become a popular research focus because of its advantages, including low-cost, nontoxicity, and thermal stability $^ { 2 1 , 4 2 - 4 \dot { 5 } }$

Generally, doping with nonmetal elements can improve the photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ by forming a new localized state between its VB and CB, which reduces the required energy for electron excitation, narrowing the material's effective band gap. Such an "extra band" can also slow the recombination rate of electron-hole pairs and prolong carrier lifetime, increasing quantum efficiency.16 Doping of nonmetal elements into the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ lattice can be achieved through two different modes: (1) so-called interstitial doping, where the nonmetallic atom is placed in the interstitial site; doping through this approach results in lattice distortion, whereby a slight shift occurs in the absorption edge of the doped photocatalyst and (2) substitutional doping, in which the nonmetallic atom replaces Ti or O to form a $\mathrm { \Delta X { - } T i { - } O }$ structure (X represents nonmetallic elements), which generally has higher thermal stability than materials prepared via interstitial doping.46

Nitrogen is among the most common choices for doping $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ to enhance photocatalytic activity because of its comparable atomic size with oxygen, high stability, and low 2001 that the degradation rate of MB by N-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ under visible light irradiation was significantly increased compared to undope $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ modifying the electronic band structure of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ by mixing N

2p orbitals with O 2p orbitals, narrowing the bandgap of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ since electrons can be excited from $\textnormal { N } 2 \textnormal { p }$ instead of $\textnormal { O } 2 \tt p$ to Ti 3d. Cho et al. designed a type of 3D-structured N-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ film using nanostructured epoxy as a substrate and TiN as the nitrogen source.49 Numerous scattering centers in such a 3D structure increase light absorption opportunities for the catalyst and synergize with the enhanced visible light absorption by $_ \mathrm { N }$ doping, endowing the material with 2.5-fold efficiency enhancement for photodegradation of MB relative to undoped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ or $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ films using flat epoxy as substrates. Moreover, electron-deficient N atoms can trap electrons, which effectively promotes charge separation and suppresses the recombination of electron-hole pairs. In principle, the photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ will be enhanced with increasing amount of N-doping. However, Xing et al. reported the positive correlation between photocatalytic activity and nitrogen content is only valid when the molar ratio of $\mathrm { N } / \mathrm { T i }$ is less than two.50 Beyond this ratio, the photocatalytic rate will drop, because high $\mathrm { N } / \mathrm { T i }$ ratio results in more nitrogen adsorbed on the surface of the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ catalyst, which obscures active sites.

In addition to the N content, various N sources have different effects on improving the photocatalytic response of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ .Senthilnathan et al. prepared a series of N-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ catalysts with different N-containing organic compounds, including triethylamine, ethyl amine, urea, and ammonium hydroxide.51 They found that the triethylamine-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ showed higher photocatalytic activity than the others. This is because triethylamine is a tridentate ligand, which can form a more stable complex with Ti, promoting the stability of nanoparticles and avoiding the aggregation of catalysts. Lee et al. synthesized a conformal and thickness-controllable N-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ film using titanium tetrachloride and ammonium hydroxide as precursors for ALD.47 The as-prepared film showed significantly enhanced visible light response and successfully degraded organic pollutants under simulated solar illumination. Recently, Zhang et al. exploited these ALD N-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ films on ceramic membranes to create the first in operando self-cleaning membranes (Figure 3a).21 Previously, photocatalytic antifouling methodologies generally involved removal of the fouled membrane from the filtration system to apply illumination to degrade the foulants.52,53 In this work, the light source was coupled with the filtration apparatus, and the photocatalysis could therefore take place in conjunction with separation. The results showed the N-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ membrane exhibited 24-fold higher self-cleaning rate than undoped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ membranes under visible light illumination (Figure 3b-3c).

Sulfur-, carbon-, and fluorine-doping also have great potential in improving photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ Niu and coauthors reported a S-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ that had stronger capacity for phenol degradation than undoped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ The sulfur incorporated into the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ lattice replaced Ti, and since the energy level of S is lower than that of Ti, this leads to a narrowed band gap and visible light absorption.54 Because of the high thermal stability of carbon, it has attracted significant attention for improving photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ Carbon doping can enhance anatase stability and prolong the lifetime of carriers, boosting photocatalytic efficiency.16 Zhang et al. prepared a C-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ with anatase and rutile mixed phase, which displayed a stronger ability for visible-light-activated photocatalysis.44 In the modified catalyst, C coexists as graphite-like carbon, interstitial carbon, and substitutional carbon. Graphite-like carbon and interstitial carbon serve as photosensitizers and enable more visible light absorption, while substitutional carbon introduces a new state located above the VB of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ leading to band gap narrowing. These catalysts can inactivate microorganisms and destroy their proliferation ability.55 Therefore, such catalysts have promise in the control of organic and microbiological membrane fouling.

Yu et al. reported that the photodegradation rate of MB by F-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ can reach $91 \%$ ,while that by undoped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ is only $3 2 \%$ under visible light irradiation.45 This dramatic enhancement is attributed to the fact that fluorine increases surface acidity, so that more molecules can be adsorbed on the catalyst surface through hydrogen bonds. Furthermore, as a product of the $\mathrm { F }$ doping, more oxygen vacancies are formed and serve as active sites for the generation of various ROS, which are responsible for mineralization of organics.56 In addition to single-element doping, multi-nonmetallic elements, as well as metal/nonmetal codoping have also shown positive results for enhancing photocatalytic performance of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ owing to the synergy between the different elements.5759

2.1.3. Heterojunction Construction. Since the rapid separation of charge carriers and their fast transfer to the surface of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ is one of the determining factors for highefficiency photocatalysis, the construction of photocatalytic heterojunctions has been developed to mitigate electron—hole pair recombination thus enhancing photocatalytic ability. $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ -based heterojunctions are formed by integrating with other substances (e.g., other semiconductors or carbon materials). Generally, there are two different types of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } -$ semiconductor heterojunction: p-n type and non-p-n type. When $\mathbf { n }$ -type $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ contacts with a p-type semiconductor, because of the Fermi level of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ being higher than that of the p-type semiconductor, the flow of electrons is from $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ to the other semiconductor, while holes transfer in the opposite direction until both semiconductors are at same Fermi level. Accordingly, a space-charge region at the interface of the p-n heterojunction is formed and creates a built-in electric field. One can take advantage of this field to achieve effective separation of charges and their rapid transfer, as well as the heterojunction by coupling n-type $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and p-type $\mathrm { C u } _ { 2 } \bar { \mathrm { O } }$ 60 The as-prepared composite catalyst exhibited enhanced photodegradation performance of RhB because of (1) the narrowed band gap energy of ${ \mathrm { C u } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } }$ (about $2 . 1 7 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ increasing visible light absorption and that (2) the CB of ${ \mathrm { C u } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } }$ is more negative than that of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ so the photogenerated electrons in $\mathrm { C u } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ flow to the CB of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ which effectively inhibits electron—hole pair recombination.

Non-p-n heterojunctions can be further classified into three categories according to the relative positions of the bands of the two semiconductors, as shown in Figure $4 \mathsf { a } - 4 \mathsf { c } \colon$ (i) type-I heterojunctions (straddling gap type), in which both electrons and holes tend to transfer to the semiconductor with a small band gap, making it a recombination center, which is detrimental to photocatalytic performance and (ii) type-II heterojunctions (staggered gap type), which is the most commonly applied in photocatalysis. In this type, when two kinds of semiconductors are closely connected to form a heterojunction, the Fermi level on both sides of the heterojunction will tend to be consistent, supporting the flow of electrons and holes and thus preventing the recombination of charge carriers. Therefore, more electrons and holes can be directly or indirectly involved in redox reactions with contaminants, and the photocatalytic activity is enhanced;29 (ii) type-III heterojunctions (broken gap type), in this system, there is no charge transfer between the two semiconductors because there is no overlap between their band gaps.61

A PVDF membrane modified with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { Z n O }$ nanocomposite was prepared by Li et al., and it displayed a $7 3 \%$ improvement in removal of humic acid compared to pristine membranes.22 This enhancement is attributable to the formation of a type-II heterojunction between $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ and $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ . The photoinduced electrons transfer from the CB of $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ to that of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ meanwhile, the photogenerated holes in the VB of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ would shift to the VB of $\mathrm { Z n O }$ The effective separation of these charge carriers facilitates redox reactions with humic acid. Moreover, the modified membrane exhibited photoinduced superhydrophilicity; the water contact angle decreased by $8 2 . 6 \%$ after irradiation by visible light for $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ thus the antifouling property of the membranes was improved. Similarly, Zangeneh et al. fabricated a light-driven self-cleaning membrane by integrating L-histidine-doped $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ CdS nanoparticles into PES matrix via phase inversion.23 The electrons in CdS would transfer to the CB of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and the holes are left in the VB of CdS during the photocatalytic process, increasing the population of charge carriers involved in the generation of ROS and oxygen vacancies. The produced ROS participate in the degradation of pollutants adsorbed on the membrane surface, while the oxygen vacancies interact with adsorbed water molecules to form hydroxyl groups on the membrane surface. These hydroxyl groups can further adsorb more water molecules through hydrogen bonds, and finally, a hydration layer will be formed, which can weaken the interaction between membrane and foulants, thus enhancing antifouling performance.

Bard et al. proposed a novel electron transfer pathway in a type-II heterojunction system.62 The excited electrons in the semiconductor with less negative CB recombine with the holes in the semiconductor with less positive V; thus, the remaining electrons and holes have stronger redox capability. As the shape of the charge transfer path resembles a $" \boldsymbol { Z } ^ { \flat }$ , the name of such a system is a Z-scheme heterojunction (Figure 4d). $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ is a semiconductor with high oxidation potential, so it is usually coupled with a semiconductor with higher reduction potential to form the Z-scheme heterojunction. For instance, Low et al. demonstrated that Z-scheme $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { C d S }$ composite photocatalyst has stronger photocatalytic activity than pure TiO2 and pure Cds.63

Another type of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ -based heterojunction photocatalyst is constructed by coupling with carbon materials, including AC, carbon nanotubes CNTs, and graphene-based materials. Jamil et al. demonstrated that the introduction of AC into $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ photocatalytic systems can improve the mineralization rate of ys. The main reason underlying he improvement is that AC has large surface area and strong adsorption capability, which increase the adsorption of dye molecules on $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and contribute to the contact between them, thus improving the photodegradation rate. Moreover, AC can also suppress the transition of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ from anatase to (less-active) rutile phase.65

CNTs have been widely used in photocatalytic applications due to their outstanding electronic and mechanical properties, as well as high specific surface areas and stability. In $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } -$ CNT heterojunctions systems, CNTs, as a support of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ provide larger surface area, which increases the contact sites between $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and contaminants. Moreover, similar to the metal $- \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ system, a Schottky barrier forms at the interfaces between $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ and CNTs, and CNTs can easily trap the photoinduced electrons from $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ because of their large electron-storage capacity, effectively promoting charge separation. All of these advantages endow $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } \mathrm { - C N T }$ heterojunctions with enhanced photocatalytic activity. Mohamed et al. synthesized a Ag-modified ${ \mathrm { T i O } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { - C N T } }$ heterojunction catalyst for degradation of RhB dye.6 Change of the electronic structure of $\mathrm { \bar { T i O } } _ { 2 }$ from introducing CNTs reduces its band gap, and the catalyst shows good photocatalytic response to visible light. The formation of $_ { \mathrm { T i - O - C } }$ bonds contributes to a decrease of charge recombination rate, which also improves photodegradation performance.

Graphene-based materials, including GO and rGO, also have great potential to boost photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ Because graphene-based materials often have high conductivity, large surface area, and excellent electron mobility, the photocreated charge carriers from $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ can be transferred freely though the material sheets, slowing the recombination of electron—hole pairs.67 Zhang et al. synthesized a $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } -$ graphene nanocomposite photocatalyst using GO and commercial $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ catalysts (P25).68 The P25-graphene heterojunction catalysts exhibited superior photodegradation performance relative to pure P25 of MB dye under both UV and visible light because of the shortened band gap. Photocatalytic activity of the modified catalysts was also higher than that of P25-CNT heterojunction catalysts. This higher performance is due to the two-dimensional structure of graphene sheets providing a platform for both dye adsorption and charge transport. Indeed, adsorption efficiency of dyes on catalyst surfaces improved with the increase of graphene content. Furthermore, the photogenerated ROS are altered with the introduction of graphene-based materials. In rGO$\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ heterojunction systems, oxygen vacancies are the dominant ROS to oxidize organic molecules, whereas hydroperoxyl radicals take on the responsibility in pure $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ photocatalytic processes under UV light irradiation. Xu et al. prepared a $\mathrm { G O - T i O } _ { 2 }$ PVDF nanocomposite membrane on the basis of phase inversion.53 Compared to the GO-modified PVDF membrane and the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ -modified membrane, this heterojunction-modified membrane fouled BSA exhibited higher flux recovery rate after irradiation by UV light, indicating that it possesses self-cleaning property attributed to the synergy between GO and $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ (Figure 5).

2.2. Photocatalysts Based on Other Emerging Materials. 2.2.1. Graphitic Carbon Nitride-Based Photocatalysts. $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ is based on a stacked two-dimensional graphite carbon structure where nonadjacent carbon atoms are substitue byens  uenc  exet thermal and chemical stability, ecofriendliness (metal-free), visible-light response $( E _ { \mathrm { g } } \sim 2 . 7 ~ \mathrm { e V } )$ , controllable electronic structure, and other advantages, $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ is an emerging photocatalytic material for water treatment.74 Upon light illumination, electrons and holes are formed in the CB and VB of $\mathrm { g } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } ,$ respectively. Holes are not involved in the generation of ROS with this system because of the particular band gap structure of $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ Only electrons in the CB can directly react with dissolved oxygen to produce superoxide radicals by one-electron reduction reactions or generate $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ by two-electron reduction, then the superoxide radicals and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ further consume electrons to produce hydroxyl radicals with stronger oxidizing capability.75,76 The photoexcited electrons in $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ and its derived ROS would transfer to the surface and participate in decomposition reactions of organic molecules. Moreover, these reactive species can also destroy the cell structure of microorganisms, leading to the leakage of their DNA, as well as the loss of the cytoplasmic nutrients necessary for their growth and reproduction.77 As such, $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ has potential applicability to membrane organic or biofouling control. Some examples of $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ based photocatalysts for contaminant degradation, which have potential for application in membrane fouling alleviation, are presented in Table 2. However, the photocatalytic activity of bulk $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ is restricted because of its small surface area, limited visible-light absorption (absorption edge is ${ \sim } 4 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , and ineffective separation of electron—hole pairs. Therefore, various strategies have been proposed to improve the photocatalytic performance of $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ .

Incorporating photocatalytic activity traditionally used for organic compound degradation into membrane technology can significantly mitigate membrane fouling. For instance, Huang et al. prepared a honeycomb-like $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ material and immobilized it into a PVDF membrane. 80,81 Taking advantage of enhanced photocatalytic activity of the modified photocatalyst because of the enlarged surface area and the accelerated electron—hole pair separation performance, the membrane exhibited photocatalytic-enhanced self-cleaning property under visible light irradiation.

Similar to $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ the photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ can also be improved by noble metal decoration, element doping, and heterostructure construction with other semiconductor or carbon materials. As mentioned in section 2.1.1, the SPR effect caused by coherent electron oscillations on the metal surface is beneficial to visible light capture by photocatalysts and consequently enhances their photocatalytic activity. Xue et al. loaded Au and $\mathrm { P t }$ onto ${ \bf g } { \bf - C } _ { 3 } { \bf N } _ { 4 }$ using photodeposition and explored its photodegradation performance of tetracycline hydrochloride in visible light. 89 The results showed the degradation rates are in following order: $\mathrm { A u / P t / g  – C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } \ > }$ $\mathrm { A u / g  – C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } > P t / g – C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } > g – C _ { 3 } N _ { 4 } }$ These results proved that the photocatalytic activity of $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ can be improved by noble metal, and moreover, bimetallic coloading is more effective than monometallic modification owing to the synergy of the two metals. SPR of Au was excited and produced hot electron—hole pairs upon visible-light illumination. Driven by potential energy, the hot electrons would be injected into the CB of $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } ,$ and then further transferred along with the excited electrons in $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ to Pt because of the high conductivity of $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } .$ The electrons on Pt would react with $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to produce superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, while the holes left in Au oxidize $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ or hydroxyl groups to generate hydroxyl radicals. Thus, the photocatalytic activity of pure $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ was limited. Moreover, the timely electron transfer in the $\mathrm { A u } / \mathrm { P t } / \mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ photocatalyst system inhibited the recombination of charge carriers, which has a positive effect on photocatalytic performance.

Introducing metal or nonmetal ions into the $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ lattice via chemical or physical approaches can reduce grain size and thus increase specific surface area of ${ \bf g } – { \bf C } _ { 3 } { \bf N } _ { 4 }$ catalysts, resulting Hu et al. prepared a phosphorus-doped $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ photocatalyst and coated it on an alumina substrate, and then integrated it into an alumina hollow fiber membrane module to create a photocatalytic membrane reactor.92 This photocatalytic membrane reactor exhibited high efficiency and stability for the decomposition of organic molecules, because phosphorus can fill the carbon vacancy, repairing structural defects of ${ \bf g } _ { - }$ ${ \mathrm { C } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { N } } _ { 4 } ,$ thus reducing charge recombination. Jiang et al. designed a phosphorus and sulfur codoped $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ using thermal copolymerization.3 The removal efficiency of tetracycline and MO by this decorated $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ was 5.9 and 7.1 times higher than that of pure $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } ,$ respectively. Introduction of $\mathrm { P }$ and S can significantly alter the electronic properties of $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ and inhibit photogenerated charge recombination. Moreover, the specific surface area of $\mathrm { P / S / g \mathrm { - } }$ $\mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ was greatly enlarged due to the suppression of crystal growth by the codoping of $\mathrm { P }$ and S atoms into the $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ lattice. The $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ with larger specific surface area has more active sites for photoreaction, which increases the light absorption and broadens the visible light response region. Furthermore, it has been reported that sulfur materials exhibit hig antibacterial activity through produced ROS.94 Consequently, these modified $\mathrm { g } – \mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ can be used for photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants or microorganisms attached to the membrane during the separation process to alleviate fouling.

2.2.2. MOF- and COF-Based Photocatalysts. As photoresponsive porous materials, MOFs and COFs have attracted attention for photocatalytic degradation of contaminants. MOFs are porous crystalline solid materials assembled by metal and organic ligands, while COFs are porous crystalline organic polymer composed of light elements (e.g., C, H, O, N) connected  covalent bonds The properties hh porosity, large specific surface area, tailorable structure, and feasible functionalization endow MOFs and COFs with potential for design of superior photocatalytic activity with rapid production of a large amount of ROS for decomposition of pollutants and, potentially, membrane fouling mitigation. Table 3 lists some examples of MOFs/COFs based photocatalysts applied in contaminants degradation, which also can be utilized for membrane fouling control. In the COF-based photocatalysis system, the ordered conjugated structure of COFs is not only conductive to light harvesting, but also helpful for the transfer of excited electrons, avoiding charge recombination.97 He et al. prepared a series of imine-based COFs using different nitrogen-containing precursors that were applied for degradation of MO and phenol, enabling structure-property-performance relationships to be evaluated.99 COFs with more active centers (triazine rings) and higher conjugation exhibited higher decomposed rate of organic molecules.

In the MOF-based photocatalysis system, MOFs can go through a similar photoresponsive process to conventional semiconductors, in which VB and CB are described as the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), respectively.98 Upon light irradiation, MOFs absorb photon energy and produce electrons and holes. The electrons are excited to the LUMO while the holes remain within the HOMO, followed by further transfer to the surface of MOFs for photocatalytic reduction and oxidation reactions, respectively. Therefore, increasing light absorption, improving electron—hole pair generation and separation efficiency, as well as enhancing charge-carrier utilization efficiency are important strategies to increase the photocatalytic activity of MOFs. Among numerous types of MOFs, Fe-based MOFs are considered one of the most promising photocatalysts because of their $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } – \mu _ { 3 } – 0 8 0$ clusters that can promote the generation of hydroxyl radicals and accelerate degradation of contaminants by photo-Fenton reactions. Xie et al. reported a PVDF/MIL-88 (Fe) mixed matrix membrane with self-cleaning property for oil-water separation.102 The membrane flux that declined initially due to oil fouling recovered completely after light exposure, which was attributed to the photo-Fenton activity of MIL-88 (Fe) catalysts (Figure 7).

Compared to traditional semiconductor photocatalysts, one of the greatest strengths of MOFs and COFs as photocatalysts for membrane fouling mitigation is their porous structures, which are conducive to foulant adsorption and degraded product transport. Zhou et al. prepared a PVDF/MIL-125 (Ti) composite membrane by phase inversion for dye removal.105

The membrane showed higher degradation efficiency of RhB under natural light illumination compared to PVDF pristine membranes because of the enhanced light absorption by dye sensitization as well as the increased active sites created by MIL-125 (Ti) incorporation. Moreover, this membrane displayed superior antibacterial property due to generated ROS. It has been reported the ROS generated by MOFs upon light stimulation can destroy organic substances in the structure of bacteria, and the sterilization capacity of MOFs can even exceed that of ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ and $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ 106 In addition to their porous structure, tailorable and easily functionalized molecular frameworks of MOFs and COFs enable them to be precisely designed to maximize photocatalytic performance in the context of membrane self-cleaning.

The modification approaches suitable for traditional photocatalysts, such as metal or nonmetal doping and heterostructure formation with other photoactive materials, are also effective for MOFs or COFs to improve their photocatalytic activity.107 Furthermore, in recent years, co-MOF/COF nanocomposite catalysts have become promising materials to improve photocatalytic performance for contaminant decomposition and are further applied in membrane fouling alleviation. Peng et al. constructed MOF-COF core—shell hybrid materials for visible-light-driven degradation of RhB.108 Lv et al. synthesized two types of MOF-COF hybrid catalysts using step-by-step assembly and investigated their photodegradation activity under visible light.109 The result showed the degradation efficiency of bisphenol A was $8 1 . 1 \%$ after $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ irradiation. Strong photocatalytic performance was attributed to the heterostructure formed between COFs and MOFs promoting charge separation and extending the spectral absorption region, as well as the hierarchical porous structure of the hybrid catalyst enlarging the population of attachment sites between substrate and photocatalyst. Moreover, Li et al. reported a MOF-COF hybrid catalyst connected with amine covalent bonds, which boosted the visible-light photocatalytic activity and exhibited higher stability than the simple MOFCOF heterostructure connected by weak interaction forces.110 This novel visible-light-responsive porous hybrid photocatalyst may also be applied for stable antifouling during long-term membrane operating processes.

2.2.3. Other Potential Photocatalysts. Other Metal-OxideBased Photocatalysts. Beyond $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ some other metal oxide nanostructures such as zinc oxide $\mathrm { ( Z n O ) }$ 111 iron oxide $\left( \mathrm { F e } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 } \right) , ^ { 1 1 2 }$ tin oxide $\left( \mathsf { S n O } _ { 2 } \right)$ ,113 and tungsten oxide $\left( \mathrm { W O } _ { 3 } \right) ^ { 1 1 4 }$ have also been explored for photocatalytic degradation of contaminants and applied in membrane fouling mitigation. Common features sought after when selecting semiconductors for photocatalysis include being inexpensive, nontoxic, and physically and chemically stable. In addition to their catalytic functionality, blending metal oxide nanoparticles into organic membrane matrices not only improves membrane hydrophilicity but also can enhance their mechanical properties. Rajeswari et al. blended ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ into polymeric membranes, and the photocatalytic membranes had higher flux due to the hydrophilic nature of $\mathrm { Z n O }$ 115 They also investigated the influence of $\mathrm { p H }$ on the photocatalytic efficiency of $\mathrm { Z n O - }$ blended membranes. Since $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ will react with acid to form salts at acidic condition while at high $\mathrm { p H } ,$ it will form complexes, thus neutral $\mathrm { p H }$ is the optimal condition for ${ \mathrm { Z n O } } .$ based photocatalysis. In this implementation, the photocatalyst-membrane coupling system with suspended $\mathrm { Z n O }$ acted as a pretreatment for membrane filtration processes. A fraction of foulants can be photodegraded by suspended ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ before the feed solution enters the membrane module. Therefore, photocatalytic behavior can alleviate membrane fouling and thus reduce flux decline. Importantly, however, $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ aggregation in the photocatalysis unit will decrease photocatalytic activity. Polymer capping can effectively prevent photocatalysts from aggregation. Desa et al. synthesized a series of ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ nanoparticles with polymer decoration and compared their activity in membrane photocatalytic reactors for dye degradation. The results showed polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)- and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified ${ \mathrm { Z n O } }$ had better dispersion, which was attributed to their higher hydrophilicity. Moreover, the minimal particle size and maximal surface area of ZnO-PEG contributed to more ROS generation to attack foulants. Therefore, the alleviation of membrane flux decline is in an order of $\mathrm { Z n O } < \mathrm { Z n O } \mathrm { - P V P } <$ $_ { \mathrm { Z n O } }$ -PEG. Design of porous photocatalysts also can increase the surface area. Zhao et al. fabricated mesoporous $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ spheres with high light absorption and sufficient charge separation and transfer rate.117 The introduction of highly acidic ${ \bf W } { \bf O } _ { 3 }$ increased the population of acidic sites on composite photocatalysts and thus enhanced their adsorption capacity toward various contaminants. In the combination of $\mathrm { W O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ particles and MF membranes, flux decline of membranes was reduced by composite photocatalysts, and the photocatalysts could be separated from the solution by membrane filtration after photocatalytic treatment. ${ \mathrm { F e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ nanoparticles also have been widely employed as photocatalysts in membrane photocatalytic reactors because their magnetic properties are advantageous for their reclamation from solution. Moreover, it has been reported ${ \mathrm { F e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ loading can not only increase visible light response of membranes, but also facilitate the adsorption of HA, which contributes to the redox reaction between HA and ROS on ${ \mathrm { F e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ surfaces.118,119

# 3. CONFIGURATIONS OF PHOTOCATALYSIS-MEMBRANE SYSTEMS FOR FOULING CONTROL

Generally, two types of systems have been established to introduce photocatalysts into membrane system for process intensification of membrane fouling control: PMS with suspended photocatalysts and PMS with photocatalytic membranes. Because the chemical and physical properties (e.g., structure, specific surface area, light response capacity, and stability) of various photocatalysts are distinct—even for the same photocatalyst, the incorporation method and the targeted contaminants are different—it is difficult to compare various reported PMS designs. In Table 4, we provide some examples of the effects associated with different PMS for membrane fouling control. In the PMS with suspended photocatalysts, photocatalysts are dispersed in the feed solution, which increases the contact opportunity between contaminants and photocatalysts (this contact is usually restricted by mass transfer over the photocatalyst layer immobilized on membrane substrates in the PMS with photocatalytic membranes). Moreover, dispersing particles can increase the amount of suspended photocatalysts for rapid photocatalysis processing. For the PMS with photocatalytic membranes, photocatalysts are immobilized on the membranes or a free-standing photocatalytic membrane is fabricated. The distinct benefit of this design is that the photocatalysts are easily recycled and regenerated. In this section, the research progress of these two types of configurations of PMS in membrane fouling control will be discussed, with an emphasize on effect of photocatalysts immobilization on membrane separation performance and stability.

3.1. Photocatalysis-Membrane System with Suspended Photocatalysts. As mentioned above, different photocatalytic nanoparticles with superior photocatalytic performance for contaminant degradation, such as $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ ${ \mathrm { Z n O } } _ { \cdot }$ , and ${ \mathrm { F e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 } ,$ have been used in PMS. Various modification methods (e.g., elemental doping or heterojunction construction) can further increase their photocatalytic activity. On the basis of the position of membrane module, PMS with suspended photocatalysts can be classified into two groups: (1) split-type, in which a slurry photocatalytic unit is followed by a membrane module unit (usually a tubular membrane module), as shown in Figure 8a and (2) slurry type, in which the membrane is submerged in the photocatalytic solution (Figure 8b), and plate/hollow fiber membrane modules are commonly applied in this system to reduce space occupation. The split-type design with isolated photocatalysis and membrane units avoids potential membrane damage caused by UV light irradiation and photogenerated ROS. This approach also facilitates their individual maintenance, which prolongs the lifespan of the entire system.143 Szymanski et al. designed a split-type PMS using $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ as photocatalysts for HA treatment. When the concentration $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ photocatalytic particles is $1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { g } \mathrm { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } .$ , the permeance of ceramic UF membrane presents no decrease as operation proceeds. This sustained performance is because HA are adsorbed and degraded by $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ in the photocatalysis unit before entering the membrane module. Pretreatment performance is enhanced as amount of suspended photocatalysts is increased.

A membrane bioreactor coupled with a photocatalytic reactor was proposed by Oliveira et al. for wastewater dispose $6 0 \%$ of organic contaminants from the feed stream for the subsequent membrane bioreactor through adsorption as well as attack by free radicals, alleviating membrane fouling. The fouling resistance in this hybrid system was 7.3 times lower than that in independent membrane system. However, when the photocatalysts are sent to the membrane module unit along with the treated solution for recycling, they might be deposited on the membrane surface or in the transport pipeline, which would simultaneously reduce the photocatalytic and membrane performance. Moreover, photocatalysts may mechanically damage the membrane surface, which would negatively affect its long-term stability. Erosion of the membrane would depend on the shape and size of photocatalysts. In the work from Mozia et al., compared with lab-made ${ \mathrm { A } } 8 0 0 { \cdot } { \mathrm { T i O } } _ { 2 }$ with round particle shape and a larger size of $5 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ ,commercial $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ ST-01 whose shape is angular and particle size is $8 \mathrm { \ n m }$ caused more severe damage to the membrane.144

The integration of photocatalysis and membrane module in one unit (slurry type) decreases the length of pipeline, reduces the risk that the pipeline is blocked by photocatalysts, and cuts stirring and aeration are carried out to reduce membrane fouling by increasing turbulence of the solution. A large number of oxygen bubbles can also accelerate the formation of hydroxyl radicals through the reaction of oxygen and photogenerated electrons, thus promoting photodegradation of foulants. However, light scattering associated with a large number of bubbles also interferes with photocatalysis. Jiang et al. proposed a novel bubble-free backwashing method instead of aeration, and more effective control of membrane fouling was achieved by maintaining high photocatalytic performxplo l catalysis to remove bacteria for the mitigation of subsequent membrane biofouling.14 Increasing the amount of suspended photocatalysts can accelerate photocatalytic efficiency, but at the same time, catalyst particles would also enhance light scattering. Therefore, it is crucial to adjust the photocatalyst dose to the optimal value.

Although PMS with suspended photocatalysts exhibits high photocatalytic performance, which is beneficial to membrane fouling mitigation, there is still a challenge for the recovery and regeneration of those photocatalysts. Jing et al. has demonstrated $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } / \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ composite particles could be easily recovered by a magnetic bar due to the magnetic property of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } ,$ and the photocatalytic activity of them only reduced by $3 . 8 7 \%$ after 3 reusing cycles.1 146 Guo et al. prepared a poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA)/poly(acrylic acid) $\left( \mathrm { P A A } \right) / \mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ nanocomposites by electrospinning method and it used as a support for Au nanoparticles loading. The large area of the composite material helped to avoid Au aggregation and the presence of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ enables rapid separation of composite catalysts from the solution.147 Inspiringly, Li et al. adopted a submerged magnetic PMS using $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 ^ { - } } \mathrm { G O } { - } \mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ composite paricles  atalyst. ${ \mathrm { F e } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ not only enhanced Fenton degradation of contaminants, but also enabled magnetic recovery of photocatalysts from the bulk solution.

In industrial application processes, slurry-based PMS are more practical than split-type PMS, because slurries can reduce the damage of photocatalyst particles caused by high shear force, avoid the etching of membrane materials by photocatalytic nano/micro particles, as well as pump clogging. However, the aeration required in slurry-type PMS requires high energy consumption, so it is crucial to regulate aeration rate and the size and position of bubbles. When selecting membrane materials for PMS, although ceramic membranes have high chemical and physical stability, and many studies have been carried out to control biofouling on ceramic membranes using photocatalysis,149,150 the utilization of organic membranes is likely to be more economical. Therefore, the improvement of tolerance of organic polymer membranes to photogenerated free radicals is an important area for further development.

3.2. Photocatalysis—Membrane System with Photocatalytic Membrane. To more effectively strengthen the separation and regeneration of photocatalysts from the effluent and avoid secondary pollution, free-standing photocatalytic membranes manufactured with pure photocatalysts and photocatalytic membranes with immobilized photocatalysts have been developed and applied in PMS.143 The photocatalytic decomposition of foulants and membrane separation are conducted in the same unit (membrane module) within a membrane operation process. In PMS with free-standing photocatalytic membrane, materials with large specific surface area, high conductivity, and chemical stability are required to form the membrane. GO nanosheets are ideal candidates that act as the skeleton of a free-standing photocatalytic membrane. Liu et al. first deposited $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles on $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 }$ nanosheets, and then intercalated them into the adjacent GO nanolayers to form a free-standing sunlight-driven photocatalytic membrane for oil-water separation.82 This membrane exhibited high self-cleaning performance and reusability;

For PMS with photocatalysts coated on membrane surfaces, polymeric and ceramic membranes are both used as the supports, and the possibility of photocatalyst agglomeration is reduced compared to photocatalyst-blended membranes. However, a potential challenge with this type of membrane is that photocatalysts can detach from the membrane surface during filtration, which would reduce long-term photocatalytic stability. To overcome this issue, some strategies have been proposed to enhance the interaction between membrane surfaces and photocatalysts. For instance, Feng et al. constructed a polydopamine (PDA) layer on PSf membrane substrates taking the advantage of selfpolymerization and strong adhesion property of dopamine, and then $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles were fixed on the membrane through chemical bonds with PDA.157 Photocatalytic activity of this membrane was enhanced owing to the enlarged contact area between contaminants and the photocatalysts immobilized on the membrane surface, whereas in photocatalyst-blended membranes, the active sites of the photocatalysts might be covered. In real industrial feed streams to photocatalytic membrane systems, the types of foulants can be complex, including organic compounds (e.g., protein, polysaccharide, biomacromolecules, etc.), inorganic constituents (silicates, carbonates, calcium, etc.), and bacteria.8 Moreover, these foulants can work in concert to exacerbate fouling processes, and, in some cases, antifouling processes themselves can induce fouling in downstream components via creation of reaction products that are not fully mineralized. # 4 CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVE

After years of limited progress and skepticism within the community, integration of photocatalysis into membrane processes is emerging as a promising approach for membrane fouling mitigation. In this Review, advances in photocatalysts for effective foulant degradation has been discussed. To date, as shown in Figure 10, conventional photocatalysts such as $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles, as well as various emerging materials, such as $\mathrm { g - C } _ { 3 } \mathrm { N } _ { 4 } ,$ MOFs, and COFs, have been used. Efforts have been directed toward improving their photocatalytic activity via enhancing visible-light response, promoting charge separation, and facilitating charge transport. Specific strategies include loading noble metal, doping nonmetal elements, and constructing semiconductor heterostructures. Two configurations of PMS have been introduced. PMS with suspended photocatalysts is likely more feasible for large-scale application, as it has higher photocatalytic efficiency because of the larger specific surface area of photocatalysts and adjustable concentration, while PMS with photocatalytic membranes may enable facile photocatalyst recycling. Impact of photocatalyst immobilization on membrane separation performance and stability should be explored thoroughly.

Although significant progress has been achieved, photocatalysis, as an environmentally friendly method for removal of membrane fouling, still struggles to compete with traditional chemical cleaning. Technological challenges to meet the requirements in industrial applications include low quantum efficiency, poor (sun)light utilization, difficult photocatalyst loading and recovery. Future research should continue focusing on the issues outlined above. Possible solutions may include exploration of novel materials, which promote harvesting photons in a more efficient way and thus improve visible-light response of photocatalysts. Construction of a welldefined structure has the potential to extend the lifetime of active charges. Particular heterostructures, for example, give rise to unique charge transport pathways between VB and CB of different semiconductors, successfully avoiding recombination of electron—hole pairs.

New strategies for integration of light sources and filtration apparatus are also needed. At present, membrane modules commonly applied in industry are composed of metal or opaque plastics. Therefore, it is crucial to design new types of membrane modules that are either optically transparent (for external illumination) or outfitted with internal light sources while still maintaining mechanical robustness for pressuredriven operation. A possible strategy is to adopt a tubular membrane module in the photocatalytic reactor and arrange the light source on the inner wall of its shell, or to apply a spiral wound module with light sources on the mesh paper (i.e., feed channel spacers).

For photocatalytic membranes, a key challenge lies in the fact that the photocatalytic functionality that is desirable for fouling control can also lead to deleterious degradation of the membrane itself (mainly for organic membranes). Fabrication methods need to be improved to maximize the photocatalytic ability without compromising the membrane separation performance. Although the short-term processing capacity of photocatalytic membranes may decrease because of the loss of membrane flux caused by photocatalyst immobilization, membrane fouling could be mitigated in a long-term process, thereby maximizing the economic benefit.

UV light can damage membrane substrates, and photogenerated ROS can not only oxidize contaminants attached on the membrane but also attack polymer chains, leading to changes in the microstructure and material properties of polymeric membranes. Therefore, UV-resistant membranes, shifting to more visible-light photocatalysts, and strategies to protect organic membranes from free-radicals are all promising areas for exploration.
