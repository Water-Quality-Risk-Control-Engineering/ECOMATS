# . Introduction

Atrazine (ATZ, 2-chloro-4-ethylamineisopropylamine-s-triazine) is one of the most globally applied triazine herbicides in agriculture. It has moderate soil sorption and water solubility $( 3 3 \mathrm { m g } \bullet \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ , which makes it easy to flow into both surface and ground water, and then it is frequent to be detected with the concentration from 0.07 to ${ } _ { 3 0 0 } \ \mu \mathrm { g } \bullet \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ Besides, it is stable in the natural environment and can transport and accumulate by food chain. Owing to above accounts, it has received extensive concerns on its potential hazards to the ecological environment and human health [1,2]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop an effective method to remove ATZ pollution, enabling sustainable development in agriculture and environmental protection. Due to the structural stability and high solubility of ATZ, as well as its benzene ring structure formed by three nitrogen atoms, it exhibits resistance to many conventional removal methods, such as adsorption, filtration, biodegradation, and chemical degradation [3,4]. It is worth mentioning that advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are hailed as a more effective technique in degrading organic pollutants compared to traditional water treatment methods [57]. With super strong oxidizing ability, a series of highly oxidative species, for example, hydroxyl radicals $( { } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { O H } )$ , sulfate radicals $( S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet } ~ ^ { - } )$ and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ are generated in the process of AOPs and would attack the chemical bonds in organic compounds, breaking them down into smaller and harmless molecules like carbon dioxide and water [8,9]. The highly reactive oxygen species (ROSs) are commonly produced by activation of substances like peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and peroxydisulfate (PDS) etc. Interestingly, due to its asymmetric structure, PMS can be activated more easily than PDS, and it works with a high efficacy, adaptability and versatility in AOPs [10]. It has reported by substantial studies that PMS can be activated by UV [11], heat [12], ultrasound [13], and transition metals [14]. Among the alternatives, transition metals are featured for easy control, highly selective, and widely applicable in a variety of environmental remediation processes. In particular, Co and Ru based catalysts always exhibit high activity in PMS activation under mild reaction conditions. Among which, Ru-based catalysts have attracted tremendous attention recently due to their corrosion resistance, stable properties and excellent catalytic activities [15,16]. In the field of AOPs, based on a carbon nitride platform, Yan et al. developed Ru-based catalysts of CNRu, which showed a better performance in PMS activation and diclofenac removal in comparison to catalysts stemming from other transition metals (Co, Fe, Ni, and Cu) [17]. Despite Ru-based catalysts exhibit preferable activities in activating PMS for pollutant degradation, the high surface energy of Ru always leads to the issue of metal particle aggregation, which greatly restricts the further promotion of catalytic activities. To overcome this issue to enhance the feasibility of Ru-based catalysts in AOPs, it is crucial to minimize the Ru particle aggregation.

An effective strategy was reported to leverage strong metal-support interactions for dispersing and anchoring metals [18,19]. The interactions established between the support and the guest metals can boost the formation of the overlayer, which encapsulates the metals and serves to restrain the aggregation of metal particles. Layered double hydroxide (LDH) is a category of materials similar to hydrotalcite clay and anionic clay, and has been widely applied in various fields for the advantages of low cost, simple synthesis, good thermal stability and versatile chemical functionality [2022]. For instance, LDH containing active metals is deemed as a promising candidate for PMS stimulation and pollutant removal [23]. LDH is also considered as an ideal support material owing to its precise structure and tunable functional properties, allowing for precise modification of the local structure. In addition, LDH features abundant alkaline coordination sites on the surface, making it highly suitable for immobilizing transition metal atoms, and then forming highly active catalytic centers. Wang et al. managed to support Ru subnanoclusters on NiFe-LDH by the strong metal-support interactions of O-Ru bonds stemming from the electronic transfer between support and metal sites, which greatly enhanced the water splitting performances [24]. Sun et al. achieved atomic dispersion of Ru on $\mathrm { N i } _ { 3 } \mathrm { V }$ -LDH through the strong metal-support interactions of Ru-O-Ni bonds provided by the support, which played a pivotal role in the superior oxygen evolution reaction (OER) performances [25]. By all accounts, the strong metal-support interactions favor the high distribution of guest metals and then the outstanding performances of catalysts. Furthermore, a strong synergistic electron coupling effect can always be provided by LDH, for establishing the desired metal-support interactions. Combined with the above-mentioned information that Ru atoms exhibit higher activities than other transition metals, it therefore highly anticipates an exploration of loading Ru on LDH for efficient PMS activation and pollutant degradation. Among the alternatives of diversified metals within the LDH structures, NiFe-LDH was employed because it not only serves as the support, but also provides PMS based degradation activity.

Herein, Ru-doped nickel-iron LDH catalysts of Ru/NiFe-LDH were fabricated (Scheme 1) to trigger PMS and then produce highly reactive oxygen species for degradation of ATZ. The NiFe-LDH substrate exhibited a floral morphology assembling by substantial nanosheets. The guest Ru was coordinated with the alkaline sites on its surface, forming the strong metal-support interactions of Ru-O bonds. The strong interactions between Ru and NiFe-LDH endow the catalytic centers of Ru sites with a homogeneous dispersion state without any particle aggregation. Benefiting from the key role of well dispersed Ru and minor effect of NiFe-LDH substrate, the resultant catalysts of Ru/NiFe-LDH demonstrated admirable PMS-based AOP performances. The $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } /$ NiFe-LDH with optimum Ru loading could completely eliminate ATZ within $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ with the rate constant $( k )$ of $0 . 1 2 6 \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , which was a tenfold increase in comparison to that of the undoped NiFe-LDH substrate $( k = 0 . 0 1 2 \mathrm { { m i n } ^ { - 1 } } .$ Furthermore, the catalytic activity of $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } /$ NiFe-LDH in PMS stimulation and ATZ degradation outstripped multifarious ever reported catalysts. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) probe indicated that various reactive oxygen species, including $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 } ,$ O2 -, OH, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \mathrm { ~ - ~ } }$ and surface-bond radicals were generated during the degradation. Quenching experiments unveiled that $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ OH and surface-bond radicals were the dominant contributions for the removal of ATZ by 5.8Ru/NiFe-LDH/PMS system. That was to say the ATZ oxidation in this work was driven by synergistic radical and non-radical reactions. Furthermore, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LCMS) analysis was employed to identify the intermediates formed during the degradation of ATZ, providing additional insights into the degradation pathways. This contribution is for the first time to incorporate highly dispersed Ru on LDH for strengthening PMS based pollutant elimination, which provides an extricable avenue for the development of AOPs.

# 2.2. Catalyst fabrication

# 2.2.1. Synthesis of NiFe-LDH

The substrate of NiFe-LDH was prepared by a hydrothermal approach. In a typical procedure, $\mathrm { N i C l } _ { 2 }$ $\left( 0 . 1 7 2 \ g \right)$ and $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 }$ $\left( 0 . 0 2 4 ~ \ g \right)$ were jointly added into $7 3 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of deionized water for dissolution. Afterwards, $0 . 1 6 8 \ g$ of urea and $2 \mathrm { m L }$ of sodium citrate $( 0 . 0 1 \mathrm { { M } ) }$ were added under magnetic stirring until completely dissolved. Then, the mixture was transferred into a $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ of Teflon reactor kettle and kept at $1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ . The pH value before and after hydrothermal processing was 3.0 and 9.1, respectively. After that, the homogeneous suspension was formed, and the mixture was restored down to room temperature. The products were obtained via centrifuge separation at $8 0 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ for $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ and washing with deionized water and ethanol, finally dried at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight in vacuum.

# 2.2.2. Synthesis of Ru/NiFe-LDH catalysts

Ru/NiFe-LDH catalysts were fabricated by the well-known impregnating method. Firstly, the obtained NiFe-LDH was dispersed into deionized water $( 4 5 \mathrm { m L }$ . Then, $\mathrm { R u C l } _ { 3 } { \bullet } \mathrm { x H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ was mixed into the suspension with further stirring at room temperature for $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ Subsequently, $_ { 0 . 1 g }$ of NaOH was added into the above mixture. Finally, the products were dried at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight in vacuum. Different loadings of Ru (Ru content determined by ICP: $2 . 7 { - } 6 . 9 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ on NiFe-LDH were achieved by simply adjusting the amount of $\mathrm { R u C l } _ { 3 } { \bullet } \mathrm { x H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ Specifically, the resultant 2.7, 3.9, 4.7, 5.8 and $6 . 9 \mathrm { \ : w t \% }$ of Ru in the samples were obtained by adding 0.0107, 0.0134, 0.0199, 0.0225 and $0 . 0 2 8 6 g$ of $\mathrm { R u C l } _ { 3 } { \bullet } \mathrm { x H } _ { 2 } 0$ , respectively. The properties of the samples were analyzed as detailed in Text S2 (Supplementary material).

# 2.3. Degradation procedures

The catalytic activities of prepared samples were assessed by stimulating PMS to degrade ATZ. All the experiments were performed in $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of glass beakers injected with $2 5 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of ATZ solution $( 1 0 \mathrm { m g } \bullet \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ with a water bath controlled at $2 5 \pm 1 { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ The pH value of the reaction solution was not regulated unless it was expressly stated. To start, a set quantity of catalyst was added into above ATZ solution, and then the suspension was thoroughly churned at a constant speed of $3 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ for $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ to achieve an adsorption-desorption equilibrium. After that, a certain dosage of PMS was added into the solution to trigger ATZ degradation. Within predetermined time intervals, $1 . 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of reaction liquid was taken out, filtered and mixed with $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of methanol for further analysis of the residual ATZ concentration. Degradation of other pollutants, such as sulfamethoxazole (SMX), carbamazepine (CBZ)

and 4-chlorophenol (4-CP) were under the identical procedures as ATZ. Quenching experiments were conducted with the similar processes of degradation above described but additionally adding p-benzoquinone (p-BQ), tert-butanol (TBA), ethyl alcohol (EtOH), L-histidine (L-his), potash iodide (KI) or phenol (PN) to investigate the roles of reactive oxygen species. All the experiments were reduplicated three times, and the average values of the data with error bars were given. The samples were analyzed using the methods as described in Text S2. EPR analysis was conducted as shown in Text S3 (Supplementary material).

According to the above-mentioned characterization results, it could be summarized as follow three opinions. (i) First and foremost, as evidenced by XRD, FESEM, HRTEM and HAADF-STEM, NiFe-LDH substrate with the morphology of blossom nanosheets was successfully prepared by the hydrothermal method. (ii) Furthermore, despite the absence of any Ru-related peaks observed on XRD, Ru was truly loaded onto NiFeLDH. On one hand, Ru content in the Ru/NiFe-LDH samples was exactly determined by ICP-OES. In other words, the successful fixation of Ru atoms on the surface of NiFe-LDH nanosheets were established through coupling with O atoms.

# 3.2. ATZ degradation by Ru/NiFe-LDH via PMS activation

# 3.2.1. Removal performances of various catalysts

The catalytic activity of the Ru/NiFe-LDH materials were evaluated by removing ATZ from aqueous solution via PMS activation. Based on the results shown in Fig. 3a, the samples exhibited an extremely low removal rate of ATZ in the first $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of adsorption, indicating that the adsorption capacity for ATZ could be almost negligible. While only PMS was added into the reaction solution, merely $1 2 . 5 \%$ of ATZ could be eliminated within $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ Once not only PMS, but also the NiFe-LDH material was added, the activity of ATZ removal increased to $5 6 . 2 \%$ in $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ , Although the degradation performance was improved, the complete removal efficiency was still unrealized. To our surprise, it was found that the catalytic activities of Ru/NiFe-LDH/PMS systems were strikingly higher than that of NiFe-LDH/PMS. The degradation efficiency to ATZ gradually improved from $9 7 . 4 \%$ within $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ up to $1 0 0 \%$ within 90 min along with increasing Ru loading from 2.7 to 5.8 wt $\%$ . The optimal removal efficiency was reached when Ru loading was $5 . 8 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ , and further increasing the Ru loading to $6 . 9 \mathrm { w t } \%$ led to a little decrease in its performance, with 6.9Ru/NiFe-LDH taking $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ to completely remove ATZ. After the ATZ degradation by 5.8Ru/NiFeLDH/PMS system continued for a period of time, the leaching concentration of metal ions at 90 and $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ was detected by ICP-OES to be 0.54 and $1 . 3 0 \mathrm { m g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for Ru, 0.44 and $0 . 8 9 \mathrm { m g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for Fe, and 1.03 and $1 . 6 6 \mathrm { m g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for Ni respectively, which met the European Union requirement of less than $2 . 0 \mathrm { \ m g { \bullet } } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ [36]. The leaching should be due to the physical detachment of Ru sites from the NiFe-LDH and the dissolution of NiFe-LDH substrate over time. In order to differentiate whether the homogeneous leaching metal ions or heterogeneous $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } / \mathrm { N i } .$ . Fe-LDH played the catalytic role, the equal concentration of metal ions as leaching amount was separately added into the reaction system instead of 5.8Ru/NiFe-LDH. As appeared in Fig. 3b, the removal rate of ATZ within $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ was $2 2 . 0 \%$ and $2 8 . 7 \%$ with addition of 0.54 and $1 . 3 0 \mathrm { m g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ of Ru ions, less than $^ { 1 3 \% }$ for adding Ni and Fe ions, and the reaction efficiency was dramatically lower than that of 5.8Ru/NiFe-LDH $. 1 0 0 \%$ $9 0 \mathrm { m i n } \dot { }$ . Thus, it was reasonably to conclude that the heterogeneous $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } $ NiFe-LDH rather than the leaching Ru ions took responsibility for the oxidation of ATZ by activating PMS. To this end, it was rational to elucidate that as for Ru/NiFe-LDH materials the modified Ru sites played an important role in accelerating PMS activation. According to the aforementioned characteristic results, the guest Ru sites were highly dispersed on NiFe-LDH through forming strong Ru-O interactions, which effectively activated PMS and degraded ATZ.

# 3.2.2. Influences of temperature and co-existing ions

To better understand the environmental adaptability of the $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } /$ NiFe-LDH/PMS system, the influences of temperature and co-existing ions on the catalytic performances were further investigated. Fig. 3c provides a clear depiction of the degradation profiles of the $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } ,$ /NiFeLDH/PMS system at different temperatures. With the reaction temperature raised from 15 to $3 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the complete degradation of ATZ took declining time from 90 to $4 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and the rate constant $( k )$ elevated from 0.077 to $0 . 1 7 0 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , correspondingly. According to the Arrhenius formula [38], an activation energy (Ea) of $2 9 . 2 7 \mathrm { k J } \bullet \mathrm { m o l } ^ { - 1 }$ was yielded. The activation energy of $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u }$ /NiFe-LDH was lower than those of CoFe $@$ -600NC/CCM, $\mathrm { F e C o _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } } ,$ diatomite, comparative to that of $\mathrm { F e / C o } @ { \mathrm { C } }$ , and just higher than that of $\mathrm { C o _ { 3 } O _ { 4 } } @ \mathrm { T i O _ { 2 } } / \mathrm { C }$ (Table S1). This indicated the moderate energy barrier of ATZ degradation by

$5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } ,$ /NiFe-LDH/PMS system. # 3.4. ATZ degradation pathways

The degradation pathways of ATZ in the 5.8Ru/NiFe-LDH/PMS system was studied, and the intermediate products were analyzed using LC-MS. By detecting the intermediate products at reaction time of 0, 60, 90, and $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , a total of 10 degradation intermediates were obtained (Table S2 and Fig. S12). Through a combination of reported literature and fragment analysis in the mass spectrometry, possible degradation pathways were inferred and shown in Fig. 6. Three main degradation pathways of ATZ were identified as follows: (i) alkylhydroxylation, (ii) de-alkylation and (iii) dechloro-hydroxylation [57]. Firstly, considering $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \mathrm { ~ - ~ } }$ as an electrophilic oxidant, it tended to target electron-rich regions within the ATZ molecule, causing the removal of alkyl groups and subsequent formation of dealkylation products [55]. Thus, a series of ATZ derivatives could be decomposed through dealkylation reactions, including 2-chloro-4-ethylamino-6-amino-1,3, 5-triazine (CEAT, $m / z \ = \ 1 7 4 )$ and 2-chloro-4-amino-6-isopropylamino-1,3,5-triazine (CAIT, $m / z = 1 8 8 $ . Secondly, in the alkylation hydroxylation process, reactive oxygen species attacked the ethyl or isopropyl groups adjacent to the nitrogen (N) atom of ATZ through the •OH-induced H-abstraction process [57]. Carbon-centered radicals could further react with -OH to form alkyl hydroxylation intermediates, 1-((4-chloro-6-(isopropylamino)-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)amino) ethan-1-ol (CNET, $m / z = 2 3 2$ and CNIT, $m / z = 2 3 2$ ) were generated through alkylation hydroxylation [58]. These products then underwent the next steps of decomposition, including the cleavage of C-N bonds in the side chain to achieve dealkylation, resulting in CAIT and CEAT [39]. Subsequently, -Cl combined with Cl to form $\scriptstyle \mathrm { N = C - C }$ , and CEAT was converted into $( E )$ -6-chloro-N2-ethylidene-1,3,5-triazine-2,4-diamine (CVAT, $m / z = 1 7 2$ ). Due to the relatively low number of C13-O27 bond (0.55) in CNIT, it was prone to cleavage and attack by free radicals, thus 2-chloro-4-acetamido-6-isopropylamino-1,3,5-triazine (CDIT, $m / z =$ 230) could be obtained through alkyl oxidation [41]. Moreover, CDIT served as a precursor to 2-hydroxy-4-amino-6-isopropylamino-s-triazine (OAIT, $m / z = 1 7 0$ ), was formed through alkyl oxidation to obtain ODIT, and then through dealkylation reaction, OAIT was produced, followed by further deisopropylation to obtain 2-hydroxy-4,6-diamino-1,3, 5-triazine (OAAT, $m / z = 1 2 8$ [59]. Similarly, OAAT could also be obtained through CAIT alkylation and deisopropylation. It underwent heterolytic cleavage of the C-Cl bond and dechloro-hydroxylation reaction to form the adduct OAAT. The Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (T.E.S.T.) was employed for assessing the toxicity of ATZ and its degradation intermediates, containing the $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 ^ { - 9 6 } }$ h for fathead minnow, $\operatorname { L C } _ { 5 0 ^ { - 4 8 } } \mathrm { h }$ for Daphnia magna and bioaccumulation factor. Results depicted in Fig. S13a-b revealed that the acute toxicity $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ value for ATZ was $1 9 . 0 3 \mathrm { m g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for fathead minnow and $1 7 . 4 9 \mathrm { m g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for Daphnia magna respectively, which were referred to "harmful". Notably, the $\mathrm { L C } _ { 5 0 }$ values for the degradation intermediates exceed those of ATz. It could further be seen that the bioaccumulation factors of all intermediates were lower than that of ATZ (Fig. S13c). Additionally, colony-forming unit (CFU) count studies, which employed PBS as a control and Image J software for data analysis as stated in Text S5 (Supplementary material), indicated a significant increase in E. coli survival rates from $2 2 . 5 \%$ to $7 8 . 3 \%$ as the ATZ degradation continued for $0 { \mathrm { - } } 1 8 0 { \mathrm { ~ m i n } }$ in ${ 5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } } ,$ /NiFe-LDH/PMS system (Fig. S13d). This increase evidenced a decrease in the inhibitory effect on bacterial growth due to the degradation of ATZ. Therefore, these results jointly demonstrated the reduction in ATZ toxicity after its degradation in the $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } ,$ NiFe-LDH/PMS system. In summary, by initiating a cascade of reactions, the $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } ,$ /NiFe-LDH/PMS system orchestrated the replacement of specific molecular components within ATZ, effectively neutralizing its potential harm to aquatic ecosystems and human health.

# 4. Conclusions

In summary, NiFe-LDH as the substrate with a morphology of blossom nanosheets was successfully prepared by a hydrothermal method, and Ru was decorated on NiFe-LDH for PMS activation and ATZ removal. Results further revealed that Ru species were successfully immobilized in NiFe-LDH through strong metal-support interactions, and were uniformly dispersed within the layers of blossoms, which greatly enhanced the ATZ degradation performances. Kinetic results revealed that the rate constant $( k )$ for ATZ removal in the $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } / \mathrm { N i F e } .$ LDH/PMS system was 10 times larger than that in the NiFe-LDH/PMS system. Under the optimal experimental conditions with a catalyst dosage of $0 . 2 \ \mathrm { g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ and PMS content of $2 . 0 \ \mathrm { g } { \cdot } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , complete removal of ATZ was achieved within $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Furthermore, the $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u }$ /NiFe-LDH/ PMS system exhibited favorable catalytic activity even with the addition of different inorganic ions and good resistance to $\mathsf { p H }$ interference. This indicated the excellent environmental tolerance of 5.8Ru/NiFeLDH/PMS system. Mechanism insights disclosed that non-radical of $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 } ,$ free radical of 'OH along with surface-bond radicals were the main contributors to ATZ removal in the $5 . 8 \mathrm { R u } ,$ /NiFe-LDH/PMS system. Additionally, free radicals of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \mathrm { ~ - ~ } }$ and $0 \mathbf { \hat { 2 } } ^ { - }$ also participated in the degradation reaction. It was identified by LC-MS analysis that the degradation generated 10 intermediate products during ATZ degradation. Besides, the degradation pathways including alkyl-hydroxylation, de-alkylation and dechloro-hydroxylation demonstrated the mineralization and decomposition of ATZ and its derivatives.
