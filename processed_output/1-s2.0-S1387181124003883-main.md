# A B S T R A C T

Keywords:   
Magnetic porous carbon

A calcination strategy based on the use of mixed MOF HKUST-1/MIL-100 as precursor was used for the obtention of a porous carbon composite (C-HKUST-1/MIL-100) containing iron-copper bimetallic particles within it. The prepared carbon was characterized by XRD, SEM, EDS spectroscopy and $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption-desorption, confirming the obtention of a micro-mesoporous carbon with Fe-Cu particles homogenously distributed within the structure. For comparison, Cu and Fe-carbons (C-HKUST-1 and C-MIL-100, respectively) were also prepared from the corresponding HKUST-1 and MIL-100 MOFs, respectively. The catalytic performance of the developed carbons as heterogeneous catalysts for persulfate-based advanced oxidation degradation of bisphenol A was evaluated. The Fe-Cu@carbon showed the best catalytic performance, leading to a total BPA degradation after just $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction, which was closely related to the synergistic effect of iron and copper. The effect of some key parameters including initial pH value, PS concentration and catalyst dosage was investigated using the Fe-Cu@carbon. In addition, the developed carbon showed good reusability, with no apparent loss in BPA degradation, after five cycles and the ability to treat real water samples, with the advantage that the recovery process after degradation is facilitated thanks to its magnetic properties.

# 1. Introduction

The economic growth, accelerated industrialization and the exponential increase of global population have led to environmental deterioration, compromising a resource as important as water [1]. Over the last few years, many types of organic pollutants such as pesticides, dyes, pharmaceutical products and plastic derivatives have been found in various aqueous matrices [2–4]. Among these organic contaminants, bisphenol A (BPA), an important industrial raw-material extensively used for making polycarbonates and epoxy plastics, has attracted special attention due to its nonbiodegradability and high toxicity to humans and animal life, even at low levels of exposure [5]. BPA has been identified as an endocrine disruptor, and can cause dysfunction of reproductive organs, metabolic disorders and carcinogenesis [6]. Therefore, it is necessary to stablish efficient treatment methodologies to remove BPA from water.

To date, several methods are available to treat BPA contaminated water such as adsorption [7], photocatalysis [8], biodegradation [9], membrane filtration [10] and advanced oxidation processes $\left( \mathsf { A O P s } \right)$ [11]. Among them, AOPs, based on the generation of strong-oxidative reactive oxygen species, have been considered as a promising technology for degradation of organic pollutants due to their high efficiency, wide application range and good reproducibility [12,13]. In these processes, highly active radicals such as superoxide $( \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { * } )$ , hydroxyl (⋅OH) and sulfate $\left( { \bf S } { \bf O } _ { 4 } ^ { - } \right)$ are generated by using different oxidants like persulfate (PS), peroxymonosulfate (PMS), ${ \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ . Sulfate radicals, due to their high redox potential (2.5–3.1 V), long half-life $( 3 0 - 4 0 ~ \mu s )$ and good applicability at broad pH range (2.0–8.0), have provided excellent results for the degradation of a wide variety of organic pollutants including tetracycline and sulfonamide antibiotics [14,15], methylene blue and rhodamine B dyes [16,17], fenitrothion and atrazine pesticides [18,19] and bisphenol A [20–22]. The generation of these radicals from chemical oxidants can be carried out by light, microwave, heat and heterogeneous catalysis, the latter being one of the most effective and energy saving strategies [23].

Among the many catalysts employed in AOPs processes, iron-based solids, such as zero-valent iron [24], iron oxide [25], iron sulfide [26] and iron hydroxide [27], have been widely used because of their lower cost and toxicity. Particularly, magnetite $\left( \mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } \right)$ , an inverse spinel structure with Fe(II) and Fe(III) in the octahedral sites, has attracted considerable research attention owing to the reversible oxidation-reduction of the iron species and magnetic properties, which allow its easy recovery from wastewater by an external magnetic field [28,29]. However, two limiting factors for the application of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ in this field are its low activity under some operational conditions and its easy agglomeration, causing a reduction in catalytic performance. In order to improve the catalytic activity, $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ has been combined with different metals, metal oxides and metal sulfides. For instance, ${ \mathrm { C o - F e } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ catalyst, prepared by hydrothermal chemical coprecipitation method, has showed a histidine removal efficiency of $6 2 ~ \%$ after $3 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of reaction by PMS activation [30]. Shi et al. demonstrated the efficiency of $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 } O _ { 4 } } / \mathrm { C o _ { 3 } } S _ { 4 }$ nanosheets for the degradation of a high concentration of rhodamine B dye through sulfate radical generation [16]. Other dyes, such as malachite green, methyl green, methylene blue or methyl violet, have been satisfactorily eliminated by silver [31] and $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ [32] coated $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ nanoparticles. The degradation of chloroxylenol has also been studied using a $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 } O _ { 4 } } / \mathrm { S } – \mathrm { W O _ { 3 } }$ composite obtaining a significant improvement in the removal capacity, which was 16 times higher than that of the bare $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ [33]. In this context, it has also been reported the potential of Fe-Cu composites as catalysts for the effective degradation of multiple organic pollutants by AOPs [34–36].

On the other hand, to avoid problems related to the agglomeration of the metallic particles, recent investigations focused on the incorporation of these particles into porous carbon materials, since its large surface area, excellent electrical conductivity and high stability can drastically increase the adsorption and access of contaminants to the active centers and also enhances electronic transfer during degradation process [37, 38]. In this regard, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), a class of porous materials built from metal ions/clusters and organic ligands, have emerged as potential precursors of metal-doped porous carbons due to their high porosity, defined structure and outstanding tunability [39–41]. By an easy and one-step calcination process under inert atmosphere, where the precursor MOF acts as template and no additional carbon precursor is required, stable porous carbon structures can be obtained, in which metal or metal oxide nanoparticles coming from the metal ions or clusters are homogenously distributed within the framework. Following this strategy, different carbons derived from MIL-101 [42], ZIF-67 [43,44] and MOF-74 [45] MOFs have been prepared and efficiently applied as catalyst in advanced oxidation processes of organic compounds.

Bearing this in mind, the aim of this work is to explore the obtention of Fe/Cu-doped magnetic carbons, by a facile single-step pyrolysis of a mixed-metal-organic framework, and their use as catalyst for the activation of PS to degrade bisphenol A. Copper/iron mixed MOF was prepared by the partial conversion of HKUST-1 into MIL-100 at room temperature. Factors that influence the catalytic activity of the prepared carbon during BPA degradation, such as the catalyst dosage, the amount of PS and the pH of the medium were studied. In order to study the synergistic effect produced by combination of the two metals, the catalytic activity of the developed Cu/Fe-carbon (C-HKUST-1/MIL-100) was compared with Cu and Fe-carbons (C-HKUST-1 and C-MIL-100, respectively). Additionally, the reusability and stability of the bimetallic catalyst was evaluated.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Chemicals

1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid $\mathrm { ( H _ { 3 } B T C }$ , $5 9 8 ~ \%$ ), ethanol $( > 9 9 . 8 $ $\%$ , copper(II) nitrate trihydrate $\mathrm { ( C u ( N O _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 3 H _ { 2 } O }$ , $9 9 . 5 ~ \%$ ) and methanol $( \geq 9 9 . 8 ~ \% )$ were obtained from Sharlau. Iron(III) chloride hexahydrate $\mathrm { ( F e C l _ { 3 } . 6 H _ { 2 } O }$ , $5 9 7 \ \%$ ) was purchased from Panreac. Bisphenol A (BPA, $9 7 \%$ and potassium persulfate $( \mathrm { K } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } , > 9 9 . 0 \% )$ were supplied by Merck. All of them were used without further purification.

# 2.2. Characterization

A Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with $\mathrm { C u } \mathrm { K } \alpha$ radiation was used to record the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. Scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) experiments were performed by using a Hitachi S-3400N scanning electron microscope equipped with a Bruker AXS Xflash 4010 EDS system. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were collected at $7 7 ~ \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ by using a TriStar II (Micromeritics) gas adsorption analyzer. Before measurement, the samples were degassed in a vacuum at ${ 1 7 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } }$ overnight. BrunauerEmmett-Teller (BET) and two-dimensional non-local density functional theory (2D-NLDFT) models were applied to determine the specific surface area and pore size, respectively.

# 2.3. Synthesis of HKUST-1

HKUST-1 framework was synthesized by room temperature reaction according to a procedure reported in the literature [46]. $3 . 3 6 ~ g$ of trimesic acid dissolved in $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of ethanol were added dropwise under constant stirring to $3 . 8 6 \ 8 $ of $\mathrm { C u } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 3 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ dissolved in $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m l }$ of ethanol. After $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ , the resulting blue solid was washed with ethanol and dried at $7 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight.

# 2.4. Synthesis of Cu/Fe mixed MOF (HKUST-1/MIL-100)

Cu/Fe mixed MOF was prepared by the partial conversion of HKUST1 into MIL-100 at room temperature following an adaptation of the experimental procedure reported by Li et al. [46]. A portion of as-synthesized HKUST-1 $( 0 . 2 \ g )$ was stirred with $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } 0$ solution in methanol $( 1 1 . 1 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . After this time, the solid was separated by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with ethanol and dried at $7 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ .

For comparison, MIL-100 sample was prepared following identical procedure as that used for the preparation of HKUST-1/MIL-100 MOF using $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ as reaction time to achieve the full conversion.

# 2.5. Preparation of MOF-derived porous carbons

The porous carbons C-HKUST-1, C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 and C-MIL100 were obtained through direct carbonization of the HKUST-1, HKUST-1/MIL-100 and MIL-100 crystals, respectively, in a tubular furnace under $\mathrm { N } _ { 2 }$ atmosphere at $7 7 3 \mathrm { ~ K ~ }$ for $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The obtained samples were washed thoroughly with methanol and vacuum dried.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Synthesis and characterization of MOFs precursors

The copper/iron mixed MOF was prepared by a two-step process (Fig. 1) based on the partial transformation of a HKUST-1 MOF, previously synthesized at room temperature, into MIL-100 MOF by reaction of HKUST-1 with iron(III) chloride for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . For comparison purposes, a MIL-100 sample was also synthesized extending the transformation time to $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . Fig. 2a shows the X-ray diffraction patterns of HKUST-1 before and after 2 and $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ of transformation. The XRD pattern of HKUST-1 showed good crystallinity and matched well with that previously reported [49]. After $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ of conversion, the HKUST-1 peaks become less intense, and new peaks at $2 \theta = 3 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ , $4 . 1 ^ { \circ }$ , $1 0 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ and $1 1 . 1 ^ { \circ }$ are observed, which according to the literature, correspond to the MIL-100 structure [50], indicating the obtention of a mixed HKUST-1/MIL-100 material. As can be observed, extending the conversion time to $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ causes the total disappearance of the diffraction lines assigned to HKUST-1 and only the MIL-100 peaks can be observed, confirming the total conversion of HKUST-1 into MIL-100.

As shown in Fig. 3a, after the carbonization process, the XRD characteristic peaks of MOFs disappear in all cases, and new diffraction lines emerge at $2 \theta = 4 3 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ and $5 0 . 6 ^ { \circ }$ in the case of C-HKUST-1 sample, which are assigned to Cu (JCPDS No. 04–0836) [54]. It should be noted that the presence of $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ in C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 and C-MIL-100 samples results in a pronounced magnetic response (Fig. S4).

# 3.3. Degradation of bisphenol A

The catalytic activity of the developed materials was evaluated for the persulfate-based advanced degradation of bisphenol A, an endocrine disruptive chemical of public concern [58]. Fig. 4 compares the degradation ratios of BPA by the different prepared carbon materials. It could be seen that only $1 9 \ \%$ BPA removal efficiency was achieved via PS oxidation without catalyst after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , indicating the stability of BPA in aqueous solution. On the other hand, total BPA degradation was achieved after only $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ when using C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 carbon as catalyst, compared to only $3 8 \%$ and $4 4 ~ \%$ degradation values obtained when using C-HKUST-1 and C-MIL-100, respectively. These results indicate that the C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 catalyst is an excellent PS activator to degrade BPA, which can be attributed to the synergetic effect of Cu and Fe on the carbon. Based on Eq. (1), the synergistic factor of the C-HKUST-1/MIL-100/PS system was determined to be 7.7, confirming the synergistic effect of the system instead of a simple additive effect. In addition, using the C-HKUST-1/MIL-100/PS, after $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction, the TOC removal efficiency was $7 6 \%$ , indicating high mineralization of BPA.

To identify the active radicals involved in the degradation of BPA in the C-HKUST-1/MIL-100/PS process, free radical quenching tests were performed. Methanol (MeOH) was used as scavenger for $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ and •OH radicals, and tert-butanol (TBA) was chosen as selective scavenger of •OH due to its higher reaction rate than that of methanol [59,60]. As exhibited in Fig. S7, the BPA degradation efficiency decreased from 100 $\%$ to 67 and $4 6 ~ \%$ with addition of TBA and MeOH, respectively, indicating that both radicals played a significant role for BPA degradation. $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ ions, generated in-situ (Eq. (2)), and $\mathrm { F e ^ { 2 + } }$ species could react with PS to generate $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ radicals (Eqs. (3) and (4)), which would react with $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ to form •OH radicals (Eq. (5)). The formation of copper oxides after degradation was confirmed by X-ray diffraction (Fig. S8). Meanwhile, according to the standard redox potentials of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C u } ^ { + }$ , the reduction of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ by $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { \mathrm { ~ + ~ } } }$ via Eq. (6) is thermodynamically favorable, which greatly enhances the interfacial electron transfer in C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 material. Similar results have been obtained in various previous studies [33,60,61]. Furthermore, it is worth highlighting that the magnetic properties of the C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 carbon allows its easy recovery from the aqueous solution after the degradation process with an external magnet. Thus, according to these results, C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 material was selected to find the optimal reaction conditions to carry out the degradation process.

Effect of different conditions on the degradation of BPA using CHKUST-1/MIL-100 catalyst was investigated. The influence of the catalyst dosage is shown in Fig. 5a. As can be seen, the degradation efficiency of BPA increased from 38 to $1 0 0 \%$ with increasing C-HKUST-1/ MIL-100 dosage from 0.5 to $_ { 1 } g / \mathrm { L }$ , which is due to the higher amount of active sites. However, the degradation efficiency was almost constant as a higher dose of catalyst $( 2 g / \mathrm { L } )$ , thus $_ { 1 } g / \mathrm { L }$ was selected as optimum CHKUST-1/MIL-100 dosage. Fig. 5b shows the effect of the PS concentration on the BPA removal efficiency. The degradation efficiency significantly increased as PS amount increased from 0.014 to 0.028 mmol, indicating that at lower PS concentration, a low amount of sulfate radicals was generated and not enough for the degradation of BPA. A further increase in the PS dosage from 0.028 to $0 . 0 5 5 \ \mathrm { m m o l }$ did not result in changes in degradation efficiency, thus PS amount was set at $0 . 0 2 8 \mathrm { m m o l }$ . The effect of the initial pH on the degradation of BPA with C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 was investigated as shown in Fig. 5c. It can be seen that about 100, 64 and $6 7 \%$ of BPA is removed by C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 when degradation is conducted at pH 5, 7 and 9, respectively, indicating that the best oxidation performance was obtained at slightly acidic condition, which is in line with previous studies and can be attributed to the accelerated generation of sulfate radicals from persulfate activation under these conditions [62,63]. Thus, $\mathrm { p H } = 5$ was selected as optimum pH.

The reusability and stability are key parameters to evaluate the degradation performance of the catalysts. Therefore, cycling experiments were carried out for the degradation of BPA using C-HKUST-1/ MIL-100 material under the optimum reaction conditions. Between consecutive reactions, the catalyst was washed with methanol and vacuum dried for the subsequent experiment. As shown in Fig. 6a, the degradation efficiency within $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ was over $9 5 ~ \%$ after five cycles, revealing the excellent reusability of the developed C-HKUST-1/MIL100 catalyst. In addition, in order to study the stability of the material, a leaching test was performed (Fig. S9). After catalyst removal, no further degradation was observed, demonstrating that the contribution of the homogeneous catalytic reaction is negligible. Finally, to gain a deeper insight into the applicability of the developed catalyst for the degradation of BPA, the performance of C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 in groundwater real sample was evaluated. The sample was collected from a private well located in Mallorca Islands (Spain) and, as BPA was not detected, it was spiked with a standard solution of BPA to a final concentration of $5 0 \mathrm { m g } /$ L. As shown in Fig. 6b, the prepared catalyst showed good degradation of BPA, achieving $9 5 ~ \%$ degradation of the pollutant in $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction, indicating that although the time to completely degrade BPA was longer than with distilled water, the C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 catalyst is a promising material for the treatment of polluted water.

# 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, porous Fe-Cu@carbon catalyst (C-HKUST-1/MIL-100) was prepared by simple calcination of a mixed HKUST-1/MIL-100 MOF. The developed carbon was evaluated for the persulfate-based advanced degradation of bisphenol A, obtaining a total degradation efficiency of the pollutant after just $1 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , which can be attributed to the synergetic effect of Cu and Fe on the carbon. The optimum reaction conditions were established as $\mathrm { p H } = 5$ , amount of catalyst $= 1 \ g / \mathrm { L }$ and amount of persulfate $= 0 . 0 2 8$ mmol. The developed catalyst could be reused efficiently over five degradation cycles with the advantage of the easy retrieval of the material from the medium by simply applying an external magnetic field due to its magnetic properties. Finally, C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 was applied to treat real water samples, reaching a $9 5 \%$ of BPA degradation in only $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , confirming its excellent features to treat real water samples. These results demonstrate that C-HKUST-1/MIL-100 carbon is a novel catalyst with great potential for pollutants degradation in aqueous medium.
