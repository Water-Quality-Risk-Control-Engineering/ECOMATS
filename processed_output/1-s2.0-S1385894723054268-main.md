# . Introduction

The co-existence of hydrophobic and hydrophilic pollutants presents a unique challenge in traditional wastewater treatments. When stacking different treatments for removing hydrophobic/hydrophilic pollutants, these methods must be compatible with each other usually resulting in high operational costs. For example, the increasing discharge of produced water (PW) containing oil residuals and additive chemicals from oil and gas (O&G) production facilities and transportation systems has imposed irrecoverable damage to the marine ecosystem [1]. The commonly used hydrocyclone [2] and air flotation [3] techniques can effectively separate oil suspensions, but more thorough treatment technologies are required to remove the dissolved oils and hazardous chemical components, thus achieving the goal of zero-harmful discharge and possible water reuse.

A variety of alternative methods such as micro-filtration [4], bioremediation [5], photo-chemistry [6], electrochemistry [7], and adsorption [8] have been considered for more complete cleaning of the PW.

Among them, the sorption technique is one of the most popular methods due to its high removal efficiency, low-cost operation, and recoverability of adsorbent materials [9]. In recent years, some natural and synthetic sorbents such as bentonite [10], vegetable fibers [11], wool fibers [12], vermiculite [13], activated carbon [14,15], high-silica zeolites [16], and hydrophobic aerogels [17] have attracted much attention in terms of their high sorption capacity, low price, and/or environment-friendliness [18]. However, the application of sorption methods remains challenging in view of low sorption selectivity to certain pollutants and recycle of the sorbent materials suspended in treated water. Notably, polyurethane (PU) sponge with high porosity, considerable sorption capacity, and good elasticity provides a more outstanding substrate for oily water treatment [1921]. Especially, the oleophilic and hydrophilic character of PU is very favorable for simultaneous removal of dissolved chemicals in the process of wateroil separation [22].

To further enhance and broaden the sorption capacity of PU sponge for various pollutants, activated carbon (AC) is considered to couple with the PU. However, regeneration of an AC loaded sponge normally demands a heavy load of chemicals and potentially cause secondary contamination and disposal issues. In our previous work, a selfregenerable adsorptive photocatalyst, i.e., titanate nanotubes (TNTs) loaded AC (TNTs@AC), has exhibited high photocatalytic ability to degrade dissolved pollutants while preserving a high sorption capacity attributed to its high specific surface area and tubular structure [23,24]. Meanwhile, the TNTs were synthesized from P25 due to their capability to enhance the material's surface area and photocatalytic reactivity by adopting a nanotube configuration. The TNTs $@ \mathrm { A C }$ material is thus regenerated once the concentrated pollutants are fully degraded under UV irradiation [25]. Herein, we propose to coat TNTs@AC onto the PU sponge to realize the enrichment and degradation of oil/chemical with no or low chemical consumption for sponge regeneration. Such a regenerable filter will offer multiple unique advantages: 1) exhibits synergistic sorptive and photocatalytic properties; 2) allows continuous operations for simultaneous removal of multiple pollutants; and 3) imbues it with remarkable potential for application and efficient regeneration. Furthermore, most of the current catalyst research is in powder form, limiting the catalyst reusability and technology upscaling. The regenerable sponge filter could support the powdered photocatalyst and thus overcome the fundamental challenge of the catalyst application, ultimately reducing the operational cost of water treatment processes.

In the present work, simultaneous removal of oil and chemical additives (i.e., phenol as an example compound) from synthetic PW were evaluated with the regenerable TNTs@AC modified sponge filter. First, TNTs@AC composite was synthesized via an alkaline hydrothermal process and subsequently coated on PU sponge (TNTs@AC@PU). Next, batch and filter experiments were performed to gauge the sorption capacity of the novel sponge filter. Furthermore, the regenerability under photo-treatments were demonstrated. Finally, the potential pathways of sorption and degradation of the pollutants were explored. These results derived from this project will provide fundamental knowledge needed to develop a new prototype solution for simultaneous removal of oil and chemicals from wastewater.

# 2.1. Materials

# 2.2. TNTs@AC synthesis

The composite TNTs@AC was prepared with a one-step hydrothermal method [23,24]. First, a solution via the dispersion of $1 . 2 \ g \ \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ (P25), $2 . 4 { \mathrm g }$ granular activated carbon (GAC), and $2 8 . 9 \ : \mathrm { g } \mathrm { N a O H }$ in $6 7 \mathrm { m L }$ Milli-Q water was prepared. After stirring for $1 4 . 5 \mathrm { h }$ , the gray mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined reactor and heated in a muffle oven with a rate of $2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ to $1 3 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and held at this temperature for $7 2 \mathrm { h }$ . The bluish-gray precipitates were collected and transferred to a beaker and acidified with HCl solution to reach $\mathsf { p H } = 7 . 0 0 \pm 0 . 5 0$ After pH adjustment, the particles were centrifuged at $5 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ for $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ and rinsed with both ethanol and Milli-Q water twice. In the last step, precipitates were dried at $1 0 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight. The bluish-gray TNTs@AC was collected and stored for further use.

# 2.3. TNTs@AC coating on polyurethane sponge

Prior to coating, polyurethane (PU) sponge blocks $( 2 { \times } 2 { \times } 2 . 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } )$ were cleaned with ethanol in ultrasonic bath for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . These blocks were then rinsed with Milli-Q water twice and dried at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in an oven for $^ { 5 \mathrm { h } }$ Next, a $3 0 0 \mathrm { m L }$ suspension of TNTs@AC in methanol $( 1 . 6 7  g \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ was prepared. The sponge blocks were immerged into the TNTs@AC suspension for $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 1 h }$ , then taken out and washed with distilled water to remove free $\mathrm { T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C }$ particles. The treated sponge blocks were dried at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . This step was repeated three times to maximize the amount of $\mathrm { T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C }$ loading on the PU sponge $\mathrm { \ T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C } @ \mathrm { P U } )$ . Importantly, it is noteworthy that the chosen drying method at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ does not induce any changes to the structure of the PU sponge. The amount of immobilized TNTs@AC was measured by mass differences of the sponge before and after coating processes.

# 2.5. Batch sorption experiments

Phenol sorption using pristine PU sponge and $\mathrm { T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C }$ powder as control were studied at the same operating conditions. Phenol sorption isotherms were conducted in a similar fashion by performing the experiment with an initial phenol concentration of $0 . 1 \mathrm { m g L } ^ { - 1 }$ to $1 2 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g }$ $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , followed by $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ shaking at $2 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ . After the phenol sorption on TNTs@AC powder, upon centrifuging at $6 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ for $5 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , the supernatant was sampled and analyzed for phenol concentration.

To test the oil sorption behavior onto TNTs@AC@PU, the immersion method was performed. Specifically, $_ \mathrm { ~ 1 0 . 0 ~ g ~ }$ pump/motor oil was transferred into a $2 5 0 \mathrm { m L }$ glass beaker containing $1 0 0 \mathrm { m L }$ Milli-Q water. The weighted TNTs@AC@PU blocks $( 2 \times 2 \times 2 . 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 } )$ were immersed in the oil and water mixture for different time periods (101800 s), then taken out and drained for $3 0 s ,$ , and dried in an oven at $6 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight. After drying, the sponge blocks were weighted and the difference in mass of the sponge before and after sorption presented the amount of adsorbed oil. The bare PU sponge as a control was tested for oil sorption at the same operating conditions. It's worth noting that the absorbed oil exhibited minimal mass reduction attributable to self-volatilization of the oil samples at $6 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight. This loss was approximately $0 . 1 4 \%$ and $0 . 1 5 ~ \%$ for motor oil and pump oil, respectively (Table S1). The mentioned oil sorption procedure was repeated with different concentrations of oil.

# 2.6. Filter tests for binary sorption of oil and phenol

The sorption behavior of phenol on $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ filter in presence of oil was investigated. To avoid pore blocking by oil droplet, phenol sorption was first carried out in $4 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ phenol solution at $\mathrm { p H } = 7$ with initial concentrations of 0.5 to $8 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ for $^ { 5 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ and $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ shaking. Then, the sponge blocks were mixed with both phenol solution and pump/motor oil $( 1 0 ~ \mathrm { g } )$ for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , then drained for $3 0 \ s$ and taken out. The water phase was sampled and analyzed by HPLC. As a comparison, phenol sorption only on $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ block and only by pump/motor oil was also tested under the same condition.

# 2.7.Simulation of pollutant sorption on PU

The chemisorption process of phenol and oil on PU was simulated using computational chemistry techniques. First, the structures of phenol (including phenolate anion), oil (specifically icosane, $\mathrm { C _ { 2 0 } H _ { 4 2 } } ,$ . and 1-eicosene, $\mathrm { C _ { 2 0 } H _ { 4 0 } } ,$ as representative objects) and PU (monomer, $\mathrm { C _ { 1 7 } H _ { 1 8 } N _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } } )$ were optimized to obtain their global minimum conformation at the b3lyp/ $\mathsf { \Delta } ^ { \prime } 6 \mathsf { - } 3 1 + \mathsf { g }$ (d, p) level by Gaussian 09. Subsequently, multiple potential chemisorption states between PU and the pollutants were calculated at same level as the structure optimization. The electrostatic potential (ESP) of PU and the pollutants was analyzed to identify possible sites on their molecular surfaces. The sorption energies of all the species were then calculated using the b3lyp, $^ { \prime } 6 { - } 3 1 1 { + } { + } g$ (d, p) level of theory, employing the solvation model of density (SMD) continuum solvation model. The chemisorption energy (∆G) was determined as the difference between the sum of electronic $\left( \operatorname { E } _ { 0 } \right)$ and the correction thermal free energies $\mathbf { \Pi } ( \mathbf { G } _ { \mathrm { c o r r } } )$ for the adsorbed states and the initial PU and pollutants.

# 2.8. Regeneration and reusability test

The regeneration of $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ filter was carried out in two steps: first, the adsorbed oils were physically "squeezed out"; next, the entrapped oil residual and phenol were degraded by electrochemical, UV, or combined treatments. For electro-regeneration, the spent sponge block was placed into a beaker between stainless steel mesh electrodes with ${ \bf N a } _ { 2 } { \bf S O } _ { 4 }$ electrolyte $\left( 0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { M } \right)$ . A $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m A } }$ current was applied in the electro-regeneration process for up to $^ { 5 \mathrm { h } }$ .For photo-regeneration, the spent sponge block was transferred into a quartz beaker with a quartz cover, added $1 5 0 \mathrm { m L }$ Milli-Q water, then placed into a photoreactor (UV wavelength $= 2 5 4 \mathrm { n m }$ and stirred at $2 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ for up to $^ { 5 \mathrm { h } }$ To improve the photo-degradation speed, $\textsf { 5 m M }$ PMS solution was added into the quartz beaker instead of Milli-Q water to enable degradation by reactive species formed through activated PMS. The photoelectrochemical treatment is the combination of photo- and electro-degradation, i. e., the photo-regeneration was conducted on the spent sponge and followed by electro-regeneration.

After regeneration, the residual phenol in regenerated sponge block was subjected to be extracted in $8 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ methanol at $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ .The phenol concentration in methanol was analyzed by HPLC for calculating the regeneration efficiency. The regeneration kinetics of T $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { N T S } } @ \mathbf { A C } @ \mathbf { P U }$ filter was performed from 0.25 to $^ { 5 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ , while reusability was tested by performing 5 cycles for phenol sorption sponge and 10 cycles for oil

# 3.2. Batch phenol sorption

The phenol sorption experiments were first conducted under $0 . 1 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ $\mathrm { N a C l }$ and a $\mathsf { p H }$ value of 3 at which materials exhibit higher sorption capacity. Herein, $\mathsf { p H }$ is a key factor for phenol sorption, because the surface chemistry of sorbent is changed by the protonation and deprotonation of surface functional groups. The point of zero charge $\mathrm { ( p H _ { P Z C } ) }$ of AC, TNTs, and $\mathrm { T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C }$ are 7, 2.9, and 3.1, respectively [25]. When $\mathrm { p H } > 3 . 1$ , the surface of TNTs@AC becomes negatively charged, which will result in electrostatic repulsion between sorbent and adsorbate, as phenol displays anionic status at pH beyond its $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathsf { a } }$ value of 9.99 at $2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ [38]. When $\mathsf { p H }$ decreases below 3.1 the surface of TNTs@AC becomes positively charged while phenol remains in neutral form, avoiding electrostatic repulsion interactions. However, an extremely acidic environment is also not favorable, as acid can partially damage the TNTs tubular structure resulting in the loss of sorption capacity [39]. The results of phenol sorption under different pH are shown in Fig. 3a, and $\mathtt { p H } 3$ was chosen based on the result. In addition, the salinity of the aqueous phase can similarly affect the sorption behavior. NaCl in phenol solution can affect the orientation of water molecules by its solvation and dissociation, where the electrostatic field of $\mathrm { N a ^ { + } }$ and $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ can attract the water molecules thus the adsorbates become more hydrophobic, also called salting-out. The result of salting-out phenomena is that more phenol "precipitate" on the sorbent surface and thus improves the sorption capacity [40]. Based on the experimental result shown in Fig. 3b, $0 . 1 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ and $1 \mathrm { ~ M ~ N a C l }$ solution yielded similar phenol uptake, thus $0 . 1 \mathrm { ~ M ~ N a C l }$ was chosen for the experiments.

Phenol sorption isotherms were studied to investigate the interaction of the adsorbate phenol and different sorbent materials surface, using the initial phenol concentration ranging from 0.1 to $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ . As shown in Fig. 4b, TNTs@AC@PU filter has the highest phenol uptake of $4 6 . 4 1 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ g ^ { - 1 } }$ . The sorption ability of bare PU can be interpreted by the formation of hydrogen bonding between the amino groups and the ether or ester groups of PU with the hydroxyl group of phenol [38,39]. $\textstyle \pi - \pi$ interaction could be another reason, resulting in the attachment of the benzene ring of the adsorbed phenol to the benzene ring of PU [39]. The significantly improved sorption ability of $\mathrm { T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C } @ \mathrm { P U }$ can be attributed to the synergistic effect of TNTs@AC. AC has a good sorption property of phenol due to its high surface area $( 5 9 9 . 5 \mathrm { m } ^ { \bar { 2 } } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ and porous structure [40], while the small tubular structure of TNTs resulted in "tube-filling" or capillary condensation of phenol [43].

The value of $\mathrm { K _ { F } }$ and n determine the steepness and curvature of the sorption isotherm, respectively. When the value of $1 / \mathfrak { n }$ is less than unity, the significant sorption takes places at low concentration; however, enhancement of the sorption amount become less significant by increasing the concentration. The values of $\mathrm { K _ { F } }$ (10.41, 1.44 and 23.09 for TNTs@AC, pristine PU and TNTs@AC@PU) and n (3.22, 2.49 and 2.63 for $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C }$ pristine PU and $\mathrm { T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C } @ \mathrm { P U } )$ show easy separation of phenol from wastewater and high uptake capacity at low concentrations. The linear isotherm doesn't fit any materials because the phenol sorption is chemical interaction controlled, not concentration dependent only. The Langmuir isotherm, an indicator of monolayer sorption [45], also does not fit the phenol sorption behavior. The dual-mode is considered to be the combination of linear and Langmuir isotherm, and the sorption behavior will be linear at low concentration and fit the Langmuir model at high concentration. The result shows that it fits better than linear and Langmuir isotherms but worse than Freundlich isotherm. # 3.4. Filter tests for binary sorption of phenol and oil

The phenol sorption isotherm on TNTs@AC@PU in the presence of oil was evaluated for use as a filter under realistic conditions. For comparison, the sorption of phenol on either $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ or pump/ motor oil was tested as controls. As shown in Fig. 4e, TNTs@AC@PU filter displayed a high phenol sorption capacity in the presence of oil, indicating that the modified PU material can not only satisfy the need for oil removal, but also simultaneously adsorb the dissolved pollutant. It should be noted that the phenol sorption capacity of $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ in presence of oil was higher than in pure water solution, which could be explained by the transfer of phenol from water to oil phase. The noctanolwater partition coefficient Log $K _ { \mathrm { o w } }$ of phenol is 1.47 [57], indicating that phenol will have a slightly higher concentration in organic solution than in water. Thus, oil can enrich the phenol from water onto the surface of TNTs@AC@PU, thus promoting the sorption of phenol.

# 3.5. Computational simulations of phenol and oil sorption on PU

The sorption mechanisms of phenol and pump oils on PU were further determined using computational chemical simulations. Electrostatic potential (ESP) is a crucial indicator for identifying potential chemisorption sites. In this study, we selected icosane $\mathrm { ( C _ { 2 0 } H _ { 4 2 } ) }$ and 1- eicosene $\mathrm { ( C _ { 2 0 } H _ { 4 0 } ) }$ as the representative motor and pump oil structures, respectively. As indicated in Fig. 5, the negatively charged groups ${ \mathrm { > C } }$ $= { \mathsf { C } } { \mathsf { < } } .$ OH, -O-) of pollutants readily adsorb onto the positively charged sites of PU, i.e., -NH- groups. Conversely, the positively charged sites $\left( - \mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } - \right)$ of icosane are more likely to bind to negatively charged $> \mathbf { C } = \mathbf { O }$ groups.

The Gibbs free energy (∆G) value needs to be calculated to decide if those chemisorption processes will proceed. However, the $\Delta \mathbf { G }$ calculation results suggest that icosane $\mathrm { ( C _ { 2 0 } H _ { 4 2 } ) }$ and 1-eicosene $\mathrm { ( C _ { 2 0 } H _ { 4 0 } ) }$ are unlikely to undergo chemisorption processes. This conclusion is based on the absence of sorption states between PU and the oil structures. In the case of phenol and phenolate ion, the calculation results indicate that all possible chemisorption pathways are thermodynamically unfavorable $\Delta G > 0$ • Table S6). This suggests that the capture of pollutants by PU primarily occurs through physical sorption rather than chemisorption. Therefore, it is necessary to coat the PU with TNTs@AC to improve the chemisorption of oils/phenols. However, it is challenging to implement computational chemistry to study the chemical sorption process of TNTs@AC due to its complex structures and various functional groups.

# 3.6. Regeneration and reuse

The possibility of regeneration is a very important aspect in the implementation of the novel filter. Regeneration of the $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ filter for oil sorption was easily achieved by physically squeezing to remove the oil. Different approaches, including UV photocatalysis, electro-degradation, photoelectro-degradation, and UV/PMS activation, were explored to degrade phenol and possible oil residuals for complete filter regeneration.

In Fig. 6a, the phenol degradation efficiency by photocatalysis and electro-degradation were $4 8 . 4 6 \mathrm { ~  ~ { ~ \% ~ } ~ }$ and $6 6 . 8 1 \quad \%$ respectively. Photoelectro-degradation was then used to improve the regeneration. Fig. 6b has shown that photoelectro-degradation can increase the degradation efficiency up to $9 2 . 3 9 ~ \%$ , but an extended treatment time $( 1 2 \mathrm { h } )$ was applied which might lead to damage of the $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ filter. The anode was fouled, and ferric hydroxide complexes had been formed in the solution, leading to the PU sponge adopting a dark yellow color. To overcome these disadvantages, UV/PMS was considered, which quickly reaches the efficiency $9 0 ~ \%$ in $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . Therefore, UV/PMS seems to be a more practical regeneration method for the reusability of the TNTs@AC@PU filter.

In Fig. 6c, the pump oil sorption capacity on TNTs@AC@PU showed no obvious change even after 10 cycles. However, the motor oil sorption capacity on $\Gamma \mathrm { N T s } @ \mathrm { A C } @ \mathrm { P U }$ decreased to $7 0 . 7 1 \ \%$ in the second cycle and then gradually decreased in subsequent cycles, probably due to the high viscosity of motor oil. The adsorbed motor oil cannot be squeezed out completely and therefore stayed on the surface of ${ \mathrm { T N T s @ A C } } ,$ . blocking the pores of the filter, and thus affecting the subsequent motor oil uptake. Manually squeezing the oil out might also be a limiting factor, as the force produced by fingers may not be enough to squeeze all adsorbed oil out. Hence, more and more motor oil was left in the PU sponge and the sorption efficiency gradually decreased. A strong mechanical force is preferred to improve the regeneration of oil-soaked sponge filters.

The reusability of the sponge filter was evaluated in five sorptionphoto-regeneration cycles. As observed in Fig. 6d, the phenol sorption capacity decreased gradually and reached $7 0 \%$ after 5 cycles, which can be explained by two main factors. Firstly, the incomplete regeneration of TNTs@AC@PU is evident, as 1 h of UV irradiation achieves only a $9 0 \%$ phenol degradation efficiency. Secondly, the potential leakage of TNTs@AC attached on the PU sponge during the repeated sorption and regeneration experiments contributes, with a measured loss of TNTs@AC from the PU sponge recorded at $1 . 8 4 \ \% \pm 0 . 8 7 \ \%$ during these processes.

To elucidate the degradation pathways of phenol/oils, the production of active species in the UV/PMS treatment was analyzed using EPR in combination with the spin-trapping reagent DMPO. In Fig. 7, we observed the signals of DMPO- $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ and DMPO-·OH in the PMS-water system, indicating the generation of active species $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ and ·OH by PMS under UV light. Interestingly, we detected the formation of 5,5- dimethyl-2-oxo-pyrroline-1-oxyl (DMPO-x) in both the water and methanol systems under UV light. This observation suggests that the presence of $\mathrm { T N T s } @ \mathrm { A C }$ enhances the production of these two active species, as the signal of DMPO-X originated from the rapid oxidation of DMPO $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ and DMPO-•OH [5860].

A potential degradation pathway of phenol by free radicals in UV/ PMS system was proposed based on the literature [6163]. As shown in Fig. 8, $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ and ·OH can attack phenol to form intermediate substances including quinol, catechol, and benzoquinone, which are then further oxidized into chain-like small molecules of carboxylic acids via ringopening reactions. Finally, these small molecules can be mineralized into $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ .Similarly, radical oxidation can shorten the carbon chains of pump/motor oils and eventually convert them into inorganic carbon species.

# 4. Conclusions

A novel regenerable PU sponge filter, modified by adsorptive photocatalyst TNTs@AC, was successfully synthesized, evidenced by SEM imaging, EDS mapping, and XRD analysis. $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ filter displays synergistic effect for sorption of oil and phenol, e.g., yielding 12 times increase in phenol uptake capacity compared to that of pristine PU. The sorption capacities of $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ for pump oil and motor oil were approximately 92 and 69 times the weight of the sponge filter itself, respectively. The incorporation of TNTs@AC on the PU filter enables the photodegradation and electrolysis of the absorbed materials, and thus realizes the regeneration and reuse of the TNTs@AC@PU filter. While UV/PMS delivered the best regeneration efficiency of $9 3 . 8 8 ~ \%$ in the reusability experiment, the $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ filter particularly showed no apparent change to the uptake capacity of pump oil after 10 cycles. In summary, the innovative $\mathrm { T N T s @ A C @ P U }$ filter realizes the simultaneous enrichment and degradation of oil and dissolved chemicals, which provides an alternative approach to remove both hydrophobic and hydrophilic pollutants in a fast, cost-effective, and recyclable manner. This design also offers a new strategy to implement catalysts in the practical water and wastewater treatment processes for reuse.
