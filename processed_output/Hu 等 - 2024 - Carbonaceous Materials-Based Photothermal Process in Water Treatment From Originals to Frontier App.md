# Carbonaceous Materials-Based Photothermal Process in Water Treatment: From Originals to Frontier Applications

Shuyuan Hu, Lei Qin,\* Huan Yi,\* Cui Lai, Yang Yang, Bisheng Li, Yukui Fu, Mingming Zhang, and Xuerong Zhou

The photothermal process has attracted considerable attention in water treatment due to its advantages of low energy consumption and high efficiency. In this respect, photothermal materials play a crucial role in the photothermal process. Particularly, carbonaceous materials have emerged as promising candidates for this process because of exceptional photothermal performance. While previous research on carbonaceous materials has primarily focused on photothermal evaporation and sterilization, there is now a growing interest in exploring the potential of photothermal effect-assisted advanced oxidation processes (AOPs). However, the underlying mechanism of the photothermal effect assisted by carbonaceous materials remains unclear. This review aims to provide a comprehensive review of the photothermal process of carbonaceous materials in water treatment. It begins by introducing the photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials, followed by a discussion on strategies for enhancing these properties. Then, the application of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal process for water treatment is summarized. This includes both direct photothermal processes such as photothermal evaporation and sterilization, as well as indirect photothermal processes that assisted AOPs. Meanwhile, various mechanisms assisted by the photothermal effect are summarized. Finally, the challenges and opportunities of using carbonaceous materials-based photothermal processes for water treatment are proposed.

# 1. Introduction

Water quality and water resource scarcity are major challenges that humanity is facing continuously.[1,2] According to the Sustainable Development Goal 6 launched by the United Nations, over 3 billion people are exposed to significant risks due to poor water quality, and 2.3 billion live in areas with water resource scarcity.[3] In response, various strategies have been implemented to ensure access to clean and sufficient water for human consumption.[4-6] However, traditional water treatment technologies have always proved to be expensive and energy-intensive, and in some cases, they may even contribute to secondary pollution.[712] Therefore, a water treatment technology that is cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and sustainable should be developed, serving as a viable alternative for efficient water treatment.[13]

In recent years, advancements in efficient photothermal materials have made it possible to utilize solar energy for water treatment purposes.[1417] The photothermal process involves the absorption of solar energy by these materials, which is then converted into thermal energy. This thermal energy can cause a photothermal effect on the surroundings or other processes. Various types of photothermal materials are commonly used, including plasmonic nanomaterials,[18] semiconductors,[19] organic polymer materials,[20,21] and carbonaceous materials.[22] Among them, plasmonic nanomaterials have limited practical applications due to their high cost and potential metal leaching, as well as they only exhibit high absorption ability near specific wavelengths.[2325}Semiconductors generate less thermal energy compared to other photothermal materials. By contrast, carbonaceous materials have the potential to be one of the most promising materials for the photothermal process. This is due to their advantages of broad-spectrum absorption, superior photothermal conversion properties, high cost-effectiveness, abundant sources, and low potential for secondary pollution.[2628] An excellent photothermal process depends on the photothermal properties of photothermal materials, such as the optical absorption range and the photothermal conversion ability.[29] Therefore, the development of photothermal materials with superior photothermal properties is of utmost importance.

Research on the carbonaceous materials-based photothermal process in water treatment has garnered significant interest. Specifically, there has been a focus on the use of photothermal evaporation to remove environmental pollutants, which involves harnessing the photothermal effect to obtain clean water.[28,3033] For instance, Arunkumar et al.[34] conducted a review of various carbonized botanical species that could be used for photothermal evaporation. They highlighted the potential of describing plantbased photothermal conversion materials for solar desalination and suggested that the carbonization technique could enhance the optical absorption of these materials by increasing the carbon concentration. Another direct photothermal application in water treatment is photothermal sterilization, which involves the use of high temperatures to inactivate bacteria.[3538] Wang et al. [37] reviewed photothermal sterilization, discussing the fundamental principles and engineering methodologies of photothermal sterilization. They reported that the photothermal sterilization mechanism included protein denaturation, bubble formation, and collapse. However, they did not pay attention to the sterilized fundamental principle of carbonaceous materials. It should be noted that the physical/mechanical destruction and oxidative stress caused by carbonaceous materials, can also result in the destruction of cellular structures.[3941]

Furthermore, the photothermal effect of carbonaceous materials not only can heat water for evaporation or sterilization but also can provide energy for other processes that assist in water treatment.[4244] In particular, the photothermal effect can assist advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) for water treatment, such as photocatalysis, persulfates oxidation, Fenton oxidation, and electrochemical processes.[45,46] Photothermal effect-assisted AOPs have shown bright prospects in water treatment. However, there is a lack of systematic summaries on the assisted mechanism of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effects on AOPs, and published works present varying perspectives. Besides, the application of photothermal effect-assisted AOPs using carbonaceous materials is a much less explored discipline. Therefore, it is necessary to review the comprehensive application of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal processes in water treatment, with a particular focus on photothermal processassisted AOPs and their assisted mechanism.

Previous reviews discussed photothermal evaporation, photothermal sterilization, and photocatalysis (Table 1). However, there is still a lack of established design strategies for carbonaceous materials and the process of photothermal effect-assisted AOPs. This review aims to fill in these gaps and also discusses other areas such as different forms of photothermal evaporation, the fundamental principle of carbonaceous materials for sterilization, and the assisted mechanism of photothermal processassisted AOPs. In this work, the photothermal properties and their optimized space of carbonaceous materials are introduced first. It then summarizes the design strategies that enhance the efficiency of photothermal processes and compares their enhanced mechanisms. Next, the comprehensive application of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal process in water treatment is reviewed. In order to distinguish the difference between photothermal applications in water treatment, the concepts of direct photothermal process and indirect photothermal process are proposed according to the participated form of thermal energy in water treatment. The direct photothermal process uses photothermal effects directly to obtain clean water, while the indirect photothermal process uses the photothermal effect to assist other reactions for water treatment. The assisted mechanism and effect in different AOPs are discussed. Finally, potential future directions and challenges are mentioned to promote the development of efficient and sustainable water treatment technology.

# 2. Photothermal Properties of Carbonaceous Materials

Carbonaceous materials are a big category, including carbon dots (CDs), carbon black (CB), graphene, graphene oxide (GO), reduced graphene oxide (RGO), carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and carbon aerogels, and so on. The photothermal effect depends on the photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials. In this section, the properties of carbonaceous materials that are employed in the field of photothermal applications are introduced concisely, including optical absorption, photothermal conversion property, and thermal conductivity.

# 2.1. Optical Absorption

Optical absorption is the pillar of the excellent photothermal effect, the optical absorption ability of materials depends on the range and intensity of absorption. When carbonaceous materials serve as photothermal materials, broad-spectrum absorption is considered to be the most common and biggest advantage. However, the absorption intensity and optimum/typical absorption range are different in carbonaceous materials with different microstructures. For example, the absorption wavelength of CB materials crosses $2 5 0 { - } 2 5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , and that of CNTs is related to the tube diameter.[47] The absorption intensity of graphene can be modified by its layer. The different optical absorption properties are influenced by their structures, and the detailed influences are presented as follows.

i. CDs can be categorized into two groups: graphene quantum dots and amorphous quantum dots. The optical absorption range of CDs can be influenced by the different synthetic methods and carbon sources. Therefore, the typical optical absorption range of some CDs exhibits in the utralviolet (UV) region $( 2 0 0 - 4 0 0 \mathrm { n m } )$ and then extends into the visible range. In addition, some CDs absorb the optical energy in the range $5 0 0 { - } 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ .The different absorption ranges of different CDs can be attributed to their content of surface groups and $\pi$ -conjugated structure in carbon cores.[48]

ii. The CNTs are also promising optical absorption materials, which can be divided into single-wall carbonaceous nanotubes (SWCNTs) and multi-wall carbonaceous nanotubes (MWCNTs).[49] The structure and properties of SWCNTs are determined by the folded form of single-layer graphene, and it can be divided into metallic-CNT and semiconductingCNT . The SWCNTs with specific chirality can determine the wavelength-selective properties, and their absorption wavelength can also be modified by the tube diameter. For example, the tube diameter is $0 . 7 \mathrm { - } 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ corresponding to the peak absorption wavelength of $1 0 0 0 { - } 2 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ [50] Because the MWCNTs have various chirality, they possess a wide range of optical absorption from UV to far infrared (IR).

iii. Graphene has always been a popular material in photothermal applications. The single-layer graphene sheet can absorb $2 . 3 \%$ of light in a wide range.[51] In fact, the absorption of graphene is independent of wavelength, and the absorption can constantly rise following the layer (Figure1 a,b). It is attributed to the unique electronic structure of graphene, which is a zero-gap linear band structure called "Dirac cones" [50] The unique electronic structure results in the semi-metallic and zero-gap semiconducting nature of graphene. Thus graphene can absorb a considerable fraction of light from UV to IR.[52] The unique electronic structure makes the graphene able to assist the photo-generated electrons transporting and enhance the degradation efficiency. As the graphene derivatives, GO and RGO have excellent optical absorption, but it is not completely the same as graphene. They are a 2D network structure consisting of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ and $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 }$ hybridization, which is different from the ideal graphene sheet $( 1 0 0 \% \ \mathrm { s p } ^ { 2 }$ hybridization).[53] The energy gap of GO and RGO depends on the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 } / \mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ ratio and can be modulated by controlling the degree of redox,[54] thus changing the ability of optical absorption.

iv. Carbon aerogels are a kind of emerging material in the photothermal process for water treatment. It can be prepared by various carbon materials, such as CNTs, GO, and RGO. The optical absorption of carbon aerogel spans the entire spectrum and the absorption intensity of it is always very high. The excellent performance of carbon aerogels is influenced by the precursor and porous structure. The excellent and unique optical absorption of carbon aerogels makes them considered promising materials in the photothermal process for water treatment.

Although the light absorption range of most carbonaceous materials is satisfactory, the intensity of light absorption is relatively low. Various strategies should be adopted to enhance the absorption intensity, such as modifying the morphological structure, optimizing the chemical structure, and fabricating composites.

# Photothermal Conversion Property

Photothermal conversion property is influenced by optical adsorption. When the photothermal conversion materials are irradiated by the light, the materials can absorb light energy and convert it into thermal energy. Based on different photothermal materials, the fundamental mechanisms of photothermal conversion are classified into plasmonic localized heating (plasmonicbased nanomaterials),[18] non-radiative relaxation (semiconductor materials),[36] and thermal vibration in molecules (carbonaceous and organic polymer materials).[55] This review focuses on the thermal vibration in molecules of carbonaceous materials. According to molecular orbital theory, the molecular orbital with a lower energy than the original atomic orbital is called the bonding orbital, such as $\pi$ -bonded molecular orbitals. On the contrary, the molecular orbitals with a higher energy than the original atomic orbital are called the antibonding orbitals, such as $\pi ^ { * }$ -bonded molecular orbitals. The $\pi$ -conjugated structure $( \pi -$ $\pi ^ { * }$ structure) of carbonaceous materials is the key to the thermal vibration in molecules. After the carbonaceous materials are exposed to sunlight, the electrons on the $\pi$ -bonded molecular orbitals can be excited to the $\pi ^ { * }$ -bonded molecular orbitals. Subsequently, the photo-generated electrons relax to the $\pi$ -bonded molecular orbitals via electron-phonon coupling.[55] This process produces thermal energy.

Carbonaceous materials have been recognized as efficient photothermal materials because of their excellent photothermal conversion efficiency.[56,57] Although the fundamental mechanisms of photothermal conversion are identical, carbonaceous materials exhibit varying photothermal conversion efficiency due to differences in microstructure and band gap energy. For example, Balou et al. conducted a study on the photothermal conversion efficiency of different CDs and found that factors such as synthesis method, choice of precursors, and functionalization process could significantly affect the band gap energy, thereby influencing the photothermal conversion efficiency[58] In the case of CB nanofluids, the temperature can be raised from ${ \approx } 2 0$ to ${ \approx } 1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ after 7 min of exposure, and the heating rate is almost independent of the CB concentration.[59] Similarly, the CNTs have been demonstrated to have an efficient photothermal conversion property, which can be further improved through functionalization.

This improvement is attributed to the ability to change the band gap energy, which then affects the fundamental mechanisms of photothermal conversion. Besides, although GO is a common material in the photothermal process, it should be noted that bare GO shows weak photothermal conversion.[36] To enhance its photothermal conversion property, modifying the morphology/chemical structure or composites with other materials is necessary.

In short, the photothermal conversion property depends on the optical absorption capacity and chemical structure of the carbonaceous materials. The design of carbonaceous materials is necessary because it can enhance optical absorption and optimize the chemical structure to realize the significant increase in photothermal conversion property. However, the photothermal conversion efficiency between different experiments cannot be compared owing to the different calculated methods for assessing the photothermal conversion efficiency, such as using evaporated efficiency or temperature variation. Therefore, formulating a uniform and scientifically calculated method is necessary.

# .. Thermal Conductivity

The thermal conductivity of carbonaceous materials serves a significant role in water treatment using the photothermal effect. In this photothermal application, we not only hanker after promoting the transfer of heat but also need to reduce unnecessary heat loss. Fortunately, carbonaceous materials can meet the demand because their thermal conductivity spans an extraordinarily large range (almost over five orders of magnitude from the lowest to the highest, shown in Figure 1c).[60] The differences in functions of thermal conductivity and applications can be attributed to different microstructures of the carbonaceous materials. The applications are divided into two cases: Carbonaceous materials with high thermal conductivity used to promote heat transfer and carbonaceous materials with low thermal conductivity used to heat insulation.

The first case is present in the nanofluid water treatment process. Carbonaceous materials with ultra-high thermal conductivity promote the transfer of heat and then enhance the treatment efficiency. The common carbonaceous materials with high thermal conductivity have single-layer graphene $( \approx 5 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { \ W }$ $\mathrm { m ^ { - 1 } ~ K ^ { - 1 } }$ ,[61]MWCNTs $( \approx 3 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { \ W \ m ^ { - 1 } \ K ^ { - 1 } } )$ , and SWCNTs.[62] In contrast, carbonaceous materials with low thermal conductivity are widely used in photothermal interface applications for water treatment due to their ability to avoid heat loss to solution. Carbonaceous aerogels are the most popular carbonaceous materials with low thermal conductivity. For example, Qin et al. prepared the aerogels with $0 . 0 3 8 7 ~ \mathrm { { W } ~ m ^ { - 1 } ~ K ^ { - 1 } }$ low thermal conductivity, which is lower than the polystyrene foam $( 3 0 { - } 4 0 ~ \mathrm { m W }$ $\mathrm { m ^ { - 1 } ~ K ^ { - 1 } }$ [63] CB materials also have a low thermal conductivity and their thermal conductivity is correlated to volume fraction.[47]

The carbonaceous materials with different thermal conductivities have broad prospects in photothermal applications, which reason behind the extraordinarily large range is rooted in their atomic structure. The constant of thermal conductivity (K) for a solid material is carried by acoustic phonons $( K _ { \mathfrak { p } } )$ and electrons $( K _ { \mathrm { e } } )$ , namely $K = K _ { \mathrm { p } } + K _ { \mathrm { e } }$ [60] The dominant factor of the carbonaceous materials and metal materials is $K _ { \mathrm { { p } } }$ and $K _ { \mathrm { e } }$ respectively. These carbonaceous materials are dominated by $K _ { \mathrm { { p } } }$ , which can be explained by the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ bonding structure, and $K _ { \mathrm { e } }$ may become important after doped materials. In addition, in terms of graphite, its thermal conductivity is mostly governed by crystallite structure, grain size, and phonon scattering in different conditions.[60,64,65] For amorphous carbon materials, their thermal conductivity can be influenced by the number and structural disorder of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 3 }$ [66]

According to the above contents, various carbonaceous materials with high/low thermal conductivity are important members of the photothermal process for water treatment. In addition, the atomic structure, microstructure, and prepared method are the key parameters determining the thermal conductivity of carbonaceous materials. To meet the requirement of the photothermal process in water treatment, carbonaceous materials with different thermal conductivities can be prepared by designing their structure and optimizing the synthetic routes.

# 3. Design of Carbonaceous Materials for Efficient Photothermal Process

Based on the above discussion, there is a tight connection between the photothermal properties and the structure or constituent of carbonaceous materials. A rational design for the morphological or chemical structure of carbonaceous materials can significantly affect the whole photothermal properties. In addition, the photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials are influenced by their synthetic routes, such as the process of precarbonization and the temperature and duration of carbonization, which are the key parameters determining the photothermal conversion property for carbonized materials. For example, Long et al.[67] carbonized the carrots at different temperatures (300, 550, and $6 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ . The result indicated that a higher carbonization temperature resulted in increased optical absorption (Table 2). This could be attributed to the temperature change in the roughness of the surface structure, leading to a reduction in reflection. However, it should be noted that a higher carbonization temperature does not always guarantee a higher photothermal conversion efficiency. Thus, it is necessary to explore a designed method or synthetic route that can enhance the optical absorption and photothermal conversion properties and change the thermal conductivity of carbonaceous materials.

# 3.1. Modification of Morphology Structure

The modification of morphological structure is a common method in the design of carbonaceous material due to its ease of implementation. Various carbonaceous materials with different morphologies have been designed, wherein the presence of a porous structure and a rough surface structure has been found to significantly enhance optical absorption. The advantages and methods employed in the preparation of porous and rough surface structures have been summarized as follows.

i) Porous structure: There are many reasons for the improved photothermal process of porous carbonaceous materials in water treatment. A higher optical absorption ability of porous carbonaceous materials is the first one. It can be attributed to the light being reflected several times in the pores, leading to a decrease in scattering and reflection and an increase in the absorption of light (Figure 2).[68] Besides, the sufficient active sites of the porous structure would be to the benefit of water treatment.[6971] In addition, the pore properties of carbonaceous materials have impacts on the photothermal efficiency. For example, the rich microporous structure can cause spatial confinement, which in turn promotes the generation of thermal energy from electron vibrations due to the small space restricting electrons from moving freely[72] On the contrary, the large pores would limit the heated area.[73] Hence, the synthetic route that can prepare the carbonaceous materials with rich microporous should be explored and developed to improve the photothermal performance.

Nowadays, various porous carbonaceous materials have been prepared for the photothermal process, including carbonaceous aerogels, carbon-loaded porous materials, and carbonized biomass.[74,75] The most popular one is the carbonaceous aerogels due to their abundant porosity and high optical absorption.[7476] For example, Jiang et al. prepared a bilayered aerogel composed of RGO and bacterial nanocellulose (BNC), and the porosity was as high as $9 8 \%$ [77] The bilayered aerogel showed ${ \approx } 1 . 5 \%$ optical transmittance and ${ \approx } 2 . 5 \%$ reflectance, as well as showed an excellent photothermal effect, in which its temperature could be rapidly increased to $7 8 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ after $_ { 1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ } }$ light exposure. And the pore properties of carbonized biomass can be varied by adjusting the carbonization temperature. Carbonized biomass is identified as a promising material for the photothermal process due to the decreased production processes and materials cost.

ii) Rough surface: The rough surface morphology is another very important point to optimize the photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials. Consistent with porous structures, rough-surfaced materials facilitate the scattering of light multiple times, thereby promoting the absorption of a greater amount of incident light (Figure 2). Besides, the rough surface morphology can enlarge the larger specific surface area, resulting in enhanced optical absorption.[86]

The obtaining way of rough surfaces can be divided into the mechanical method and the deposition method. The mechanical method involves making the surface rough by physical and mechanical means. For example, Yang et al. prepared polyvinyl alcohol-nanocellulose aerogels and then sanded the prepared material to make it become a rougher surface.[86] The results demonstrated that the prepared biomass aerogels with rough surfaces had a higher evaporation rate $( 1 . 4 0 \mathrm { ~ k g ~ m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { ~ h } ^ { - 1 } )$ and evaporation efficiency $( 8 8 \% )$ than the ones with smooth surfaces. The deposition method is depositing materials on a smooth surface to form rough surfaces. Liu et al. fabricated polyacrylonitrile powder (PAN) and CNTs composite nanofibrous membrane (PC NFM) by the free surface electrospinning.[21] The PC NFM with a smooth surface was deposited polydopamine (PDA) and then a PC@PDA NFM with a slightly rough surface was obtained. The results could be imagined that PC $@$ PDA NFM with a rough surface had a higher optical absorption.

Generally, a carbonaceous material with high porosity, rough surface, and large specific surface area would typically exhibit great capacity for optical absorption. For example, Kou et al. had effectively fabricated CNT-coupled cotton, resulting in it exhibiting a significant optical absorption capacity across the entire spectrum, with a sustained light absorption rate of $9 5 \%$ due to the porous structure, rough surface morphology, and large specific surface area. Notably, this modified material addressed the previously observed limited absorption of CNTs within the wavelength range of $7 6 0 { - } 2 5 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ [81] However, as shown in Table 2, the $\alpha { \mathrm { - M o C } } _ { 1 - x } @ { \mathrm { C B } }$ material demonstrated a notably high specific surface area of $3 2 7 . 3 \ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ [8] yet its optical absorption was comparatively lower than that of the hydroxyapatite (HAP)/CNT bilayer aerogel, which had a specific surface area of $4 1 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ .[63] It is important to note that optical absorption is influenced by many other factors, such as the precursor and synthesis routes (Table 2). Hence, the preparation of carbonaceous material needs an overall consideration.

# 3.2. Design of Chemical Structure

The chemical structure can radically affect the photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials, such as $\pi$ -conjugated structure and defect structure. The most significant influence is exerted by the $\pi$ -conjugated structure of carbonaceous materials because their fundamental photothermal conversion mechanism is thermal vibration in molecules. [55] More $\pi$ -conjugated bonds and tighter arrangement can lead to better photothermal performance. According to molecular orbital theory, the inclusion of new atoms leads to forming new molecular orbitals due to the principle of energy proximity, which can cause a more closely aligned $\pi$ -conjugated structure (Figure 2). Therefore, the essence of designing chemical structure is introducing other atoms into the carbon structure in order to alter the $\pi$ -conjugated structure of carbonaceous materials.

Two common methods can achieve the change of the $\pi$ . conjugated structure for carbonaceous materials, which are doping atoms and forming amorphous carbon. Doping ions cause a redistribution of the electronistructure,[8890 thu changing its properties such as catalytic properties and photothermal properties. For example, The CDs with heteroatom doping had higher photothermal conversion efficiency owing to the band-gap energy being decreased,[91,92] and N-doped graphene aerogel had higher optical absorption owing to the rough and disorganized laminar structure.[13] Similarly, N-doped graphene sheets were demonstrated to have a lower total reflectance $( 2 - 1 0 \% )$ than pure graphene sheets $( 5 - 1 0 \% )$ [22] In addition, the formation of amorphous carbon can also achieve a tight arrangement of energy levels outside the nucleus, because the amorphous materials are no long-range crystalline order and usually contain other atoms such as hydrogen and nitrogen.[93,94] The amorphous carbon can easily induce the photothermal conversion process even under a low frequency of light, which demonstrates that the amorphous carbon can produce thermal energy at most wavelengths of sunlight.[95]

The $\pi$ -conjugate structure can also negatively affect the effectiveness of water treatment by carbonaceous materials in the photothermal process. For example, graphene sheets tend to form agglomerates by undergoing restacking due to their $\pi$ . conjugate structure and van der Waals forces.[96] The restacking phenomenon results in the surface area and the charge transfer performance declining, and then the contact area between the pollutants and carbonaceous materials is reduced.[97] Given the circumstances, a precise balance between these positive and negative effects should be considered when improving the photothermal performance by modifying the $\pi$ -conjugate structure.

In addition, the defect structure of carbonaceous materials can also affect the photothermal performance by influencing the optical absorption and thermal conductivity. Hu et al. partially reduced GO to RGO to improve the optical absorption and they found that the defect structure of RGO aerogels had a low thermal conductivity and an excellent thermal insulating property, which could be attributed to decreasing the intensity of epoxy groups and then introducing defects on the perfect $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ framework.[98] Similarly, Zhang et al. prepared a defect-abundant graphene aerogel by laser irradiation reduced small-size graphene oxide sheets, and the defect-abundant graphene aerogel had excellent optical absorption $( \approx 9 9 \% )$ and low thermal conductivity $( 0 . 0 0 7 5 \mathrm { ~ W } \mathrm { m K } ^ { - 1 } ,$ [76] Hence, the defect-abundant graphene aerogel could heat the high surface temperature from room temperature to $9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under only 1 sun. They adopted different methods to reduce GO to RGO and then indicated that the thermal annealing method could fully remove oxygen-containing groups and restore the defects, but the laser reduction method could generate sufficient functionalized groups and defects.

Although the above-mentioned methods can obtain closely aligned extra-nuclear $\pi$ -electron energy levels, most of these researches focus on the $\pi$ -conjugate structure changing the catalytic and electrochemical properties, and less on the photothermal properties. Similarly, the influence of the defects structure in the photothermal property of carbonaceous materials is lacking attention. The influenced mechanism of the chemical structure on the photothermal conversion property needs to be explored further.

# 3.3. Fabrication of Composites

Fabricating composites is one of the most common and effective methods for preparing carbonaceous materials with excellent photothermal performance because it can simultaneously change the optical absorption, photothermal conversion property, and thermal conductivity. The modification of the morphology structure and chemical structure is confined to a single-carbon material. Fortunately, the composites are prepared by two or more materials with different optical absorption, photothermal conversion properties, and thermal conductivity, which works synergistically to maximize the photothermal effect. Besides, the composites can reduce the cost of materials. For instance, given the expensive cost of graphene-based materials derivatives, researchers usually add a few amounts of graphene-based materials into other materials and then a composite with outstanding photothermal performance can be obtained. Compared to graphitic carbon nitride (CN) only $1 5 . 2 \%$ of absorption in the entire spectrum, the CNGO that CN doped with $0 . 2 \ \mathrm { g }$ of GO absorbs $6 6 . 4 \%$ of light.[99]

Carbonaceous composites can be divided into two forms, carbon-carbon materials (CCMs) and carbon-other materials (COMs). CCMs refer to the composites prepared by multiple carbonaceous materials. For example, Hu et al. significantly enhanced the optical absorption and photothermal conversion efficiency of RGO aerogels by introducing MWCNTs.[98] They found that the average solar absorption increased from $8 2 \%$ of neat RGO aerogels to $9 2 \%$ of RGO-sodium alginate (SA)-CNT aerogels because MWCNTs were outstanding light absorbers. On the contrary, COMs refer to the composites which are prepared by carbonaceous materials and other materials. For example, the semi-metallic tellurium loaded the treated rape pollen (a biomass-derived material),[100] or an aerogel consisting of PDA and CNTs nanofiber,[21] which all showed excellent photothermal properties than original materials. Xing et al. reported a hybrid aerogel consisting of GO and black phosphorus nanoflakes.[101] They demonstrated that the composite aerogels exhibited more outstanding photothermal properties than GO aerogels because the black phosphorus could absorb more light throughout the visible light region. The fabrication of composites is widely recognized as the predominant approach for producing carbonaceous materials that exhibit exceptional photothermal properties. The advantages and disadvantages of CCMs and COMs are different, and the selection of materials is important. CCMs are the most likely composite material with best optical absorption because the carbonaceous materials all have broad-spectrum absorption. And CCMs avoid the secondary pollution produced by leaching metal from plasmonic nanomaterials. For COMs, the introduction of other materials (like semiconductors or plasmonic nanomaterials) can produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) under solar illumination and then make for pollutant degradation. In this regard, the performance of COMs is superior to CCMs. However, the introduction of plasmonic nanomaterials may cause the leaching metal. Hence, a rational selection of the form of carbonaceous composite is important according to the requirement of water treatment.

# 3.4. Comparison of the Design Method

The photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials can be tuned effectively by the above three design methods. However, it is important to note that these methods differ in their underlying mechanisms for enhancing photothermal performance. Modification of morphological structure (porous structure and rough surface structure) belongs to extrinsic modification, which improves the optical absorption via larger specific surface area and multiple light scattering. It cannot affect the photothermal conversion property. Design of chemical structure $\scriptstyle { \overline { { \pi } } }$ -conjugate structure and defects structure) is a fundamental method because the atomic composition and electronic structure are adjusted, which can improve the optical absorption, photothermal conversion property, and thermal conductivity. However, the two methods are confined to improving the photothermal performance of single-carbon materials. Fabricating composites is a forceful method to address the limitation. A carbonaceous material with excellent photothermal performance can be achieved through the amalgamation of diverse materials that possess varying absorption ranges, photothermal conversion properties, or thermal conductivity. Besides, the composite can improve the photothermal performance of materials via a simple process, as well as reduce the cost of materials.

These methods are used to improve the photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials from different aspects. Not only they do not conflict with each other, but they can complement each other to achieve the best photothermal effect. The photothermal properties are optimized in multiple pathways. However, a breakthrough is still needed for the practical application of thermal energy generated by solar energy, which remains something we need to continue to explore in the future.

# 4. Carbonaceous Materials-Based Photothermal Process in Water Treatment

Carbonaceous materials can be modified to enhance their photothermal performance, resulting in photothermal materials that can efficiently absorb solar energy and convert it into thermal energy. This thermal energy can then be utilized in water treatment.

Comprehensive photothermal applications of carbonaceous materials in water treatment are discussed in this section. The applications are divided into two types: direct photothermal process and indirect photothermal process. The direct photothermal process refers to the direct utilization of the thermal energy generated by the photothermal process to achieve water treatment objectives. The indirect photothermal process refers to the use of thermal energy to assist other reactions involved in water treatment.

# 4.1. Direct Photothermal Process for Water Treatment

# 4.1.1. Photothermal Evaporation

Solar-driven photothermal evaporation technology has gained significant popularity in the fields of seawater desalination and water purification.[104] Photothermal evaporation utilizes thermal energy to generate steam from a solution, effectively removing a majority of contaminants, including inorganic salts, metal ions, and high boiling point organics, from the condensate water.[105108] The development of photothermal evaporation for water treatment can be categorized into three stages: i) photothermal nanofluid; ii) photothermal films; and iii) photothermal evaporation systems (Figure 3). Various applications of photothermal evaporation and evaporation effect are summarized in Table 3.

i) Photothermal nanofluid: In the original study, various carbonaceous materials in the form of nanoparticles were dispersed into the aqueous,109] resulting in localized vapor production without heating the entire solution (Figure 3a).[110,11] For example, Wang et al. prepared RGO. $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ magnetic nanoparticles for treating cadmium pollution through photothermal evaporation.[112] Although the concentration of $\mathrm { C d ^ { 2 + } }$ meets the standards for reclaimed water quality, the evaporation efficiency was only $7 0 \%$ under 1 sun. To enhance the efficiency, many researchers increase the light intensity from 1 sun to N (2, 3, ….,10) sun. In this respect, Ni et al. and Ghasemi et al. studied the graphitized CNT nanofluids and CB nanofluids in solar steam generation, respectively, achieving efficiencies of up to $69 \%$ and $8 5 \%$ at a solar intensity of 10 suns.[26,113] Nevertheless, the requirement for high light intensity necessitates additional energy input, which contradicts the principles of low energy consumption and sustainability. Furthermore, even under 10 suns, the efficiency remains unsatisfactory. The low evaporation efficiency can be attributed to the dissipation of a significant amount of thermal energy through radiation, convection, and conduction, and then shows an increase in temperature throughout the aqueous solution. The problems of high light intensity and low evaporation efficiency limit the practical utilization of photothermal evaporation. Because the extra energy to provide a high light intensity is cost-efficient, the low evaporation efficiency cannot meet the demand for practical water treatment. Therefore, researchers introduce the concept of photothermal films aiming to minimize dissipated heat.

ii) Photothermal films: Photothermal films are a type of photothermal evaporation that is concentrated at the water interface, achieving heat localization and reducing the light intenyiu,11,1Fop   ev ${ \tt A g } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { P O } } _ { 4 }$ -RGO-coated textiles for water evaporation and achieved a high evaporation efficiency of $8 6 . 8 \%$ under 1 sun.[70] This is a significant improvement compared to photothermal nanofluids, as it achieves higher evaporation efficiency at a lower solar intensity by concentrating thermal energy at the water interface. In addition, using photothermal materials that float at the water interface for water treatment eliminates the need for subsequent separation and recovery of nanoparticles. However, it has remained unavoidable that some thermal energy is lost to the bulk water.[116] In order to further reduce the thermal energy loss, the photothermal evaporation system is proposed.

iii) Photothermal evaporation system: The photothermal evaporation system is a commonly researched type of efficient water treatment,[107,11122] which consists of photothermal material, support substrate, and water supply material.[82] Obviously, the effectiveness of water treatment using photothermal evaporation depends on the photothermal performance of the carbonaceous materials, and the strategies to obtain excellent photothermal performance have been described in Section 3. The support substrate plays a critical role in allowing the photothermal material to float at the water interface and reducing heat loss. Polystyrene (PS) foam is commonly used as the support substrate due to its low density with $1 0 { - } 4 5 \ \mathrm { k g \ m } ^ { - 3 }$ and thermal conductivity of $0 . 0 4 ~ \mathrm { { W } ~ m ^ { - 1 } ~ K ^ { - 1 } }$ [21,81,123] The water supply material is responsible for providing a constant water source to the evaporation layer. The evaporation rate will be reduced if the water transport rate does not match it.[82] Therefore, the water supply material needs to have hydrophilic properties and high porosity to ensure timely water supply. The different combinations of these three elements form various photothermal evaporation systems, which can be classified into three categories (Figure 4).

The first category is the independent type: The above three elements are independent and self-existent. The carbonaceous material is separated from the solution by the support substrate, while the water supply material acts as a connecting bridge between them. In this instance, commonly used materials for water supply include cotton bar,[76,96,124,125]cotton fabric,[80] filter paper,[21] blotting paper,[119] and hydrophilic polymer membrane.[126] For example, Liu et al. assembled a photothermal evaporation system by placing the prepared-carbonaceous aerogel in a PS foam, where the PS foam was covered by filter paper as water supply material.[21] This system achieved an evaporation efficiency of $9 4 . 5 \%$ under 1 sun, surpassing that of photothermal film. The advantage of this independent-type evaporator is that it minimizes thermal energy losses in the solution as the photothermal material remains completely separated from the water. However, the drawback of this evaporator type is the complex combination process.

The second category is contact type: In this photothermal evaporation system, the photothermal material and the water supply material are designed as a single element. The photothermal material is designed with a porous structure or channel to obtain water directly. This evaporator can be further classified as the complete contact type and the partial contact type, based on the extent of contact between the carbonaceous material and the solution. The complete contact type means that the bottom of the photothermal material is completely in contact with the solution, allowing for water supply.[107,121,127,128] For example, Zhou et al. coated the carbon dots on the vertically aligned acetate fibers and then fixed it to the PS foam with a pore in the center, and the acetate fibers could supply water.[106] On the other hand, the partial contact type refers to only the edge of the photothermal material being in contact with the solution. Photothermal materials used in this type are typically flexible materials like fabric or airlaid paper[80,84,129,130] The two contact type evaporators offer different advantages. The complete contact type can supply water quickly because of the large contact area, but it also loses more heat. Conversely, the partial contact type operates in the opposite manner.

The third category is monolithic-type: In this photothermal evaporation system, the three elements are designed as single, integrated units. This evaporator is light enough to be self-floating. It consists of a top layer with excellent photothermal properties achieved through [117,118,131] or loading,[63,120,132] and a bottom layer with hydrophilic properties, multiple channels, and low thermal conductivity. The most commonly used material for the monolithic-type system is natural wood with a directly carbonized surface. Natural wood has the characteristics of high porosity, low density, low thermal conductivity, and suitable hydrophilia. Chen et al. prepared an asymmetric Janus wood evaporator by carbonizing the surface of natural wood, resulting in a structure that exhibited impressive water supply capabilities and the ability to float on its own.[117] The asymmetric Janus wood evaporator achieved the highest evaporation efficiency of $8 2 . 0 \%$ when tested with a highly concentrated solution $2 0 \mathrm { w t \% }$ NaCl). The monolithic-type photothermal evaporation system offers several advantages, including simple construction and minimal heat loss.

The aforementioned evaporators have been proven to significantly reduce thermal energy. However, it still dissipates thermal energy into the environment due to the carbonaceous material being completely exposed to air. To figure out the issue, convective flow has been introduced into the evaporation system to keep the evaporating surface temperature below the ambient temperature, allowing the evaporator surface to draw heat from the environment instead of dissipating it. For instance, Liu et al. prepared a 3D double-sided material by folding the CNTs-coated air-laid paper into a wavy structure and introduced the convective flow to draw heat from the environment (Figure 3d).[84] The evaporation rate had a leap from 1.67 to $5 . 5 5 \mathrm { k g } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 2 }$ when the introduction of the convective flow. Similarly, Li et al. and Wang et al. designed a 3D artificial transpiration evaporator and a 3D spiral structure evaporator respectively for taking advantage of the convective flow to enhance the evaporation rate and efficiency.[124,105]

The efficiency of photothermal evaporation has a huge leap from photothermal nanofluid to photothermal evaporation systems. Especially, the introduction of convective flow has been a significant milestone in this progress. However, some challenges remain an impediment to the practical application of photothermal evaporation systems, such as salt accumulation and lower evaporation efficiency in highly concentrated wastewater. In addition, the issue of solo-evaporation results in the concentration of pollutants in water, necessitating further treatment. To step over these barriers, various evaporation systems need to be designed further. For example, the development of the self-cleaning system can address salt accumulation, and the improvement from soloevaporation to synergy-evaporation (such as introducing other AOPs) can solve the concentrated pollutants.

# 4.1.2. Photothermal Sterilization

Photothermal sterilization is another direct photothermal process in water treatment. Human health is exposed to threats from the presence of pathogenic microorganisms in the water.[133] Thus, providing safe water to human beings by killing parasites and bacteria is essential. Research has shown that the most pathogenic microorganisms would be inactivated in high temperatures $( { \approx } 6 0 { - } 8 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ [ Therefore, the thermal energy produced by the photothermal process has been widely used for sterilization.[137] A summary of the carbonaceous materialsbased photothermal effect in photothermal sterilization is shown in Table 4.

The involvement forms of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal sterilization can be divided into photothermal nanoparticle suspensions and photothermal films.

i) Photothermal nanoparticle suspensions: The sterilization pathways of photothermal nanoparticle suspensions can be divided into three types: high-temperature destroying, thermal-mechanical destroying, and physical contact destroying (Figure 5a).[37] The mechanism of high-temperature destroying involves heating the surroundings through the photothermal effect to reach the inactivation temperature, which ultimately destroys enzymes, ribonucleic acids, and proteins.[138141] Thermalmechanical destroying occurs when the rapid rise in temperature around the nanoparticles leads to the instantaneous and explosive formation of steam (bubble), generating strong pressure waves that damage cell membranes or viruses.[142,143] Physical contact destruction, on the other hand, is attributed to carbonaceous materials, where the unique sharp structures of these materials cause damage to the membrane of bacteria.[144] For example, SWCNTs aggregation will cause damage to the membrane of Escherichia coli bacteria and then the intracellular content can be released.[145] Thus, the excellent sterilization effect of the carbonaceous materials suspension is achieved through the synergistic effects of multiple sterilization mechanisms. Nevertheless, when introducing sunlight into the water sterilization system, the high-temperature sterilization caused by the photothermal effect is dominant.

Despite the excellent sterilization effect of suspended photothermal particle-based water treatment, there are two common drawbacks associated with this form. Firstly, parts of the thermal energy are used to heat the aqueous solution rather than for sterilization purposes. Secondly, the separation of the photothermal materials from the water is troublesome. To solve the problem of poor utilization of thermal energy in sterilization, the key lies in the pathogenic microorganisms present within the range heated by the photothermal material. Therefore, many researchers have modified photothermal materials to empower materials with the property of localizing bacteria or viruses. ii) Photothermal film: Photothermal film can confine the thermal energy into a region, which results in it being more efficient than suspensions (Figure 5b).[154157] According to the state of the treated solution, the sterilization form of the photothermal film can be further divided into fixed-bed reactor and fluid-bed reactor. The principle of the fixed bed reactor is identical to that of the photothermal evaporation system, that is., the photothermal effect allows the solution to evaporate and the pathogenic microorganisms can also enrich in the interface. The high temperature can inactivate bacteria and then the obtained condensate is harmless. For example, Xue et al. prepared $\mathrm { C u } _ { 2 \cdot x } \mathrm { S } / \mathrm { S } / \mathrm { R G O }$ powder and then vacuum filtrated it onto the melamine sponge, which had a significant inactivating effect on Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus.[158] Similarly, Zhang et al. designed a composite membrane consisting of RGO/polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), which could produce a temperature higher than $1 2 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ steam for water sterilization (Figure 5c).[9] With regards tothe fluid-bed rector, a tube coated with the photothermal film is designed, and the solution containing pathogenic microorganisms flows through the tube. Furthermore, the fluid-bed reactor can avoid the problem of blockage owing to the photothermal membrane being hermetically sealed.

Consistent with photothermal evaporation, photothermal sterilization has a developed process from nanoparticle suspensions to photothermal film. This advancement has addressed the issues of thermal energy loss and nanoparticle separation. Besides, the fluid-bed reactor avoided the blockage problem of a fixed-bed reactor. Despite the photothermal efficiency has been increased in the fluid-bed reactor, it is not necessarily guaranteed that the sterilization efficiency will also improve in practical applications.

AOPs are widely used in water treatment.[160] However, the low utilization rate of ROS and poor mass transfer process limit the efficiency of AOPs.[161] The photothermal process can breathe new life into AOPs for water treatment. Photothermal effect assisted-AOPs shows high performance in water treatment, including photocatalysis, Fenton oxidation, and persulfates oxidation. # 4.2.1. Photothermal Effect Assisted Photocatalysis

Photocatalysis is an extensively researched technology that utilizes solar energy for organic pollutant degradation, heavy metal ions reduction, and pathogenic microorganism inactivation.[162166] Exposure of photocatalysts to light can produce electron-hole pairs, and then the photo-generated electrons and holes can react with dissolved oxygen and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ to produce ROS for pollutant treatment.[167169] The introduction of the photothermal effect into photocatalysis is favorable for the latter, and the efficiency of photocatalysis can reach new heights when carbonaceous materials are chosen as the photothermal conversion materials.[170173]

The assisted pathways of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect on photocatalysis are multiple, which can improve the photo-generated electron transfer and then inhibit the recombinationf electron-holepairs.[,Morimornly, the carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect can continuously provide thermal energy for photo-generated electron transfer. The introduction of photothermal effect can prolong the recombination of electron-hole pairs further. For instance, Gan et al. introduced the photothermal process into photocatalysis to degrade methylene blue in water, and then they demonstrated that the photothermal effect of RGO could significantly enhance the photocatalytic process.[42] The RGO could provide a transport channel for photo-generated electrons from $\mathrm { P } 2 5 ( \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } )$ . The photothermal effect of RGO assisted the photo-generated electrons transporting and then hampered the recombination of electronhole pairs further. The contribution of the RGO-based photothermal effect was as high as ${ \approx } 3 8 \%$ Fang et al. declared that the synergistic effect of photocatalysis and photothermal reaction was more efficient than the photocatalysis or thermal-driven reaction at the same temperature.[175] They explored the conversion rate of photo-driven, thermal-driven, and photothermal synergisticdriven $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ cycloaddition with epoxides. The result demonstrated that the photothermal synergistic-driven ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ cycloaddition conversion rate $( 8 3 . 5 \% )$ outperformed the thermal-driven conversion rate $( 1 7 . 2 \% )$ at the same temperature. The synergistic effect of the photothermal effect and photo-generated electrons and holes resulted in a more excellent conversion rate. Lu et al. also incorporated active carbon as photothermal materials into the CN matrix for degrading sulfamerazine inspired by the Arrhenius equation.[170] The degradation efficiency of photothermal effect-assisted photocatalysis was $40 \%$ higher than that of pure photocatalysis. The significant increase in degradation efficiency could be ascribed to the increased temperature of water and the effective collision chance of active substances.[170,176] The involvement of carbonaceous materials could reduce activation energy and improve light absorption, as well as improve the electron transfer performance caused by the change of the band structure.

In general, the carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect effectively assisted photocatalysis can result as follows: i) Carbonaceous composite can optimize the band structure and then improve the light absorption to generate more electron-hole pairs; ii) Carbonaceous composite can provide transport channel for photo-generated electrons and then inhibit the recombination of electron-hole pairs; iii) Photothermal effect can further accelerate the separation of the electron-hole pairs and prolong their lifetime; iv) Photothermal effect can encourage the production of ROS; and v) Photothermal effect can promote fast enrichment of pollutants around the photocatalyst and then make for the degradation. Although these mechanisms are proven can significantly assist photocatalysis, the distinction between the contribution of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect and of carbonaceous materials is a major bottleneck, which needs to be explored further.

# 4.2.2. Photothermal Effect Assisted Fenton Oxidation

Fenton oxidation is recognized as an effective method for removing organic pollutants from water. The organic pollutants can be degraded by the active intermediate with highly oxidizing (such as $\mathrm { F e O } ^ { 2 + }$ , ·OH, and ·OOH) produced by the reaction of the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ [ I   ha energy of the reduction reaction $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } {  } \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } )$ is higher than the oxidation reaction $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } {  } \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + } )$ , which resulted in the accumulation of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ and the declined rate of degradation. Besides, the limited range of pH and the ultrashort time of ·OH are challenges for Fenton oxidation.[187] The carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect can resolve these problems for effective Fenton oxidation.

It has been tested that introducing carbonaceous materialsbased photothermal effect can accelerate the electrons transfer and the ·OH production, and then contribute to promoting the Fenton reaction for pollutants degradation.[18890] For example, Xie et al. prepared the polymetallic oxide nanoparticle-loaded wood-derived carbon (POC) as photothermal and catalytic material for degrading ultra-high-concentration mixed organic wastewater, which contained Rhodamine (RhB), phenol, and tetracycline.[189] The degradation of RhB, phenol, and tetracycline could respectively reach $100 \%$ $100 \%$ , and $9 6 . 2 \%$ after $1 2 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . The photothermal effect of POC led to the temperature increase and it was beneficial to photo-Fenton reaction kinetics. Besides, the introduction of carbonaceous materials can also enhance the optical utilization efficiency of original photo-Fenton catalysis. In turn, photo-Fenton oxidation can also improve the photothermal process because photo-Fenton oxidation is an exothermal reaction. The synergistic effect provides abundant free radicals and then contributes to the excellent degradation of high-concentration organic pollutants. Jin et al. also demonstrated that the decolorization rate of dyes could be enhanced along with the temperature increase from 20 to $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ [191] This could be ascribed to the redox reaction being improved and more ·OH was produced via the photothermal effect on Fenton oxidation. Besides, the photothermal effect can also improve the effective collision chance between the dye and ·OH.

In addition, the carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect can improve the mass transfer of ·OH to target pollutants.[27] Liu et al. demonstrated the rate constants of the photothermal effect assisted Fenton oxidation higher than the photo-Fenton oxidation for MB degradation.[192] They possessed the photothermal effect not only improved the transfer of photogenerated electrons to produce more ·OH but also heated the solution and then promoted the mass transfer of organic pollutants and ·OH. Besides, they considered the photothermal effect also reduced the activation energy of organic pollutants degradation.

The utilization of carbonaceous materials in the photothermal effect has been found to enhance the electro-Fenton degradation of pollutants. Lu et al. prepared mesoporous carbon hollow spheres (MCHS) and utilized them for the electrochemical production of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , which was subsequently employed for in situ pollutant degradation.[45] The role played by MCHS in this system was the catalytic synthesis of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and provided the photothermal effect to assist the Fenton oxidation. The degradation efficiency could be high to $9 8 . 1 \%$ . It was ascribed to the MCHS could provide many electrochemical active sites and then enhanced $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ production selectivity. Besides, when solar irradiation, the photothermal effect of MCHS could promote the generation of ·OH and accelerated Fenton oxidation for degradation of methyl orange. The related reaction kinetics was 3.5 times higher than one without light irradiation.

To further distinguish the assisted effect of the photothermal effect from other factors for Fenton oxidation, a common method is making the Fenton oxidation in a low-temperature water bath. For example, Cai et al. prepared a material by amorphous $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ and C-decorated Fe-based geopolymer sphere (A$\mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } / C { \cdot } F e G S }$ for degrading tetracycline hydrochloride.[193] The photothermal effect significantly influenced the degradation of tetracycline hydrochloride because the strong photothermal effect of ${ \mathrm { A } } { \cdot } { \mathrm { M n O } } _ { 2 } / { \mathrm { C } } { \cdot } { \mathrm { F e G S } }$ could enhance the electron transfer and utilization rate, and then produce more radicals. The degradation of tetracycline hydrochloride was increased from $6 6 . 1 \%$ (temperature is $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $9 6 . 1 \%$ (temperature is $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ after $8 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ .The strong optical absorption and electron transfer of C contributed to improving photo-Fenton oxidation. To distinguish the assisted effect, they kept the reaction at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and found the degradation of tetracycline hydrochloride decreased, which demonstrated that the activation of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ was dependent on the solution temperature. Besides, the photothermal effect also changes the contribution ability of radicals, which will produce more $\cdot 0 ^ { - 2 }$ than other radicals at a higher temperature owing to the photothermal effect can enhance the adsorption of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ on the surface. Sun et al. also put forward that the photothermal effect had a different influence on different ROS.[194] But, the different influences and reasons are much less explored. It appears to be an emerging field of research.

In general, the pathways of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect assisted Fenton oxidation can be concluded: i) Carbonaceous composite can improve the optical absorption of the original Fenton catalyst; ii) Carbonaceous materials are considered as the promising catalysts for $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ production; iii) Carbonaceous materials and the photothermal effect all accelerate the electrons transfer and ·OH production; iv) Photothermal effect can enhance the adsorption of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ in surface and then produce more $\cdot { \mathsf { O } } ^ { 2 - }$ , and v) Photothermal effect can promote the transference and enrichment of organic pollutants and ROS. The assisted effect of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect on Fenton oxidation is undoubted, but the correlational research is less. That will be a promising direction to research.

# 4.2.3. Photothermal Effect-Assisted Persulfate Oxidation

Persulfate activation is also identified as a promising strategy for degrading organic pollutants or sterilization.[195,196] Activated persulfate produce ROS such as ·OH and $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - } }$ , which have strong oxidizing property and stability.[19] There are many ways toactivate persulfate, includinthermal activation,198,199] hotoactivation,20,201] and catalyst activation.[202206] Among these methods, the advantages of thermal activation are high effectiveness and the method without the potential issue of chemicalinduced secondary pollution, and the production rate of ${ \mathrm { S O } } _ { 4 }$ . in the thermal activation is higher than the other activation ways.[207] But the temperature of thermal activation needs to be above ${ \approx } 4 5$ to $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , the sustainable input of extra thermal energy enables increased cost.[208] The photothermal process can convert solar energy into thermal energy for activating persulfate, which is cost-efficient.[209] In addition, carbonaceous materials can activate persulfate via catalysis activation. Hence, the system of solar illuminating carbonaceous materials can achieve efficient activation of persulfate due to the multiple activated pathways of simultaneous participation (Figure 6e).[210]

Many researches demonstrated the carbonaceous materialsbased photothermal effect could assist the persulfate activation. Zhang et al. chose CB as a photothermal material to activate persulfate, they proposed that solar-driven thermal energy generated by carbonaceous materials could be used to thermally activate persulfate.[43] The results showed the photothermal activation of the peroxydisulfate (PDS) process could degrade roughly $9 5 \%$ of sulfamethoxazole (SMX) within $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ . It is obvious that when activating persulfate by photothermal activation, not only photothermal effect can activate the persulfate, but also UV light and carbonaceous material can facilitate the persulfate activation. However, the activation effect of the latter two is almost negligible compared to the former. Moreover, Zhang and workers found that the degradation rate of SMX decreased significantly at lower temperatures, demonstrating that only the temperature was high enough to break OO bonds and generate ·OH and $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 }$ · for effective pollutant degradation. The same conclusion was reached in the experiments of Qiu et al., in which the BPA was degraded in a system with light and peroxymonosulfate (PMS).[99] In this system, photo-generated electrons and holes were the main active substances, and free radicals generated by the light and catalyst activation of PMS assisted the degradation of BPA. The photothermal effect of CNGO should activate PMS theoretically. And yet, the system temperature only increased to $2 8 . 9 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ which could not achieve the temperature of PMS activation. Once again, it has been demonstrated that photothermal materials can activate persulfate to degrade pollutants if they generate a high enough temperature, otherwise, they only have a facilitating effect on the reaction. Another thing to note is that the efficiency of photothermal activation PDS for degrading SMX in actual river water was notably reduced, it can be attributed to the inhibition of Natural Organic Matter in the water. CB is poorly stabilized in terms of it being gradually oxidized by free radicals during the multiple PDS activation process, resulting in structural changes and the reduction of photothermal effect. Therefore, more carbonaceous materials with high stability and outstanding PS activation efficiency are needed to develop.

On the other hand, researchers have proposed introducing persulfate to the process of photothermal evaporation to avoid the overflow of the VOCs with clean water. Xiao et al. deposited CB on air-laid cloth as a photothermal evaporation film and prevented phenol from entering the clean water by using activated PDS and PMS, which took advantage of interfacial photothermal evaporation.[211] And they certified that the CB and the photothermal effect were the main catalysts for activating PDS. Despite the carbonaceous materials-based photothermal process can provide efficient persulfate activation via multiple activated pathways, there is less research focus on the carbonaceous materialsbased photothermal effect to activate persulfate.

As an emerging application, the indirect photothermal process (photothermal effect assisting the other reaction) shows great potential for development. The carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect has demonstrated that it can significantly improve the efficiency of AOPs and pollutant degradation. The photothermal process can breathe new life into AOPs for water treatment. Currently, research on the photothermal effect-assisted process in water treatment is primarily focused on photocatalysis, Fenton oxidation, and persulfate oxidation. However, there is a lack of exploration into other reactions that can be promoted or activated by the photothermal effect. Besides, the assisted mechanism also needs to be explored further. Considering the excellent assisted functions of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect, subsequent research could be directed closer in this direction.

# 5. Conclusion and Perspectives

# 5.1. Conclusion

This work provides an explanation for the photothermal process of carbonaceous materials in water treatment by discussing their photothermal properties. Carbonaceous materials can absorb a wide range of solar energy and convert it into thermal energy through the thermal vibration in molecules. The thermal conductivity of carbonaceous materials is beneficial to the maximum utilization of thermal energy in water treatment. The optimized space of carbonaceous materials is also indicated. Various strategies for improving photothermal properties and effects are reviewed, including modification of morphology structure, design of chemical structure, and fabrication of composites. After modification, these modified carbonaceous materials with excellent photothermal effects are then applied to water treatment. According to the used form of photothermal effect in water treatment, photothermal applications are classified as direct photothermal process and indirect photothermal process. Direct photothermal processes include photothermal evaporation and photothermal sterilization, with a review of the development, advantages, and disadvantages of different photothermal evaporation. The sterilized mechanism and forms of the carbonaceous materials-based photothermal process are summarized. In addition, the indirect photothermal process that carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect assisted AOPs are discussed. The carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect not only can enhance the efficiency of photocatalysis and Fenton oxidation but also can activate persulfate for efficient water treatment. The study delves into the different assisted mechanisms and effects of various AOPs. The efficient assisted effect is attributed to the synergistic effect between carbonaceous materials and their photothermal effect.

# 5.2. Perspectives

Numerous instances of carbonaceous material-based photothermal processes in water treatment have showcased their significant potential. However, for these applications to be effectively implemented in practical water treatment, they must overcome a series of challenges. This discussion provides an overview of the progress made in the development of photothermal applications and highlights the current challenges that need to be addressed.

i. Photothermal evaporation: Photothermal evaporation obtains the most attention in water treatment. The evaporation efficiency has gradually increased from suspension evaporation of $4 6 . 8 \%$ under 10 suns improved up to a 3D structured evaporation system of $1 8 6 \%$ underr 1 sun.[105,111] This improvement can be attributed not only to the optimization of the evaporated form but also to the introduction of convective flow. The introduction of convective flow has effectively addressed the issue of heat loss, which was previously difficult to overcome. This innovation allows the evaporation system to draw thermal energy from the environment and utilize it for evaporation. The breakthrough of the photothermal evaporation system with convective flow leads to stronger competitiveness in water treatment. Even so, there are still important challenges that continue to be tackled, such as the reduction in evaporation efficiency observed in highly concentrated solutions. To overcome these challenges, the development of various evaporators is necessary.

i. Photothermal sterilization: Photothermal nanoparticle suspensions are a promising form of photothermal sterilization due to them offering multiple sterilization pathways, as discussed in Section 4.1.2. However, the application of this form is limited due to the repulsive force between carbonaceous materials and most bacteria, which is caused by the negative surface charge properties of the carbonaceous materials. To address this issue, researchers have focused on altering the surface charge properties of carbonaceous materials to eliminate the repulsive force. However, they have overlooked the underlying cause of these surface charge characteristics, which is the influence of solution pH. When the solution satisfies the $\mathrm { p l } < \mathrm { p H } < \mathrm { p H } _ { \mathrm { p z c } }$ condition, the carbonaceous material can be positively charged and the pathogenic microorganisms to be negatively charged. It is an alternative strategy that can eliminate the repulsive force by adjusting the solution $\mathrm { p H }$ .In addition, the fluid-bed reactor of photothermal film is expected to be a practical application because it significantly reduces heat loss and avoids material recovery and blockage issues. However, relying solely on the high temperature generated by photothermal is unrealistic for completely eliminating pathogenic microorganisms. Therefore, further development in photothermal sterilization is indispensable. i. Indirect photothermal process for water treatment. Research has demonstrated the introduction of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect can significantly assist AOPs. However, it is difficult to determine the specific contribution of the photothermal effect and other factors in assisting AOPs. The dominating pathway of assisted effect influence for the AOPs is also ambiguous. Therefore, it is important to focus on fundamental studies to understand the mechanism and then establish uniform and scientific principles for the development of the photothermal effect. In addition, there is limited research on the photothermal effect that assisted other AOPs, such as ozone oxidation, sonocatalytic oxidation, and photoelectrocatalysis. Given the excellent assisted effect of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal effect on photocatalysis, Fenton oxidation, and persulfate oxidation, it is likely that it can also significantly enhance other AOPs for water treatment. Besides, published works have paid more attention to the degradation of dye pollutants, and the other persistent organic pollutants are ignored. For these reasons, more research is needed to address these gaps and facilitate widespread implementation.

Moreover, the emerging applications combined with the photothermal process also need more attention. For example, Zhang et al.[212] reported an innovative photo-thermoelectric structure. This structure effectively repurposed food waste by transforming it into versatile carbonaceous materials, which could then be used for sustainable photothermal evaporation and electricity generation from the ocean. Similarly, spatial confinement is an emerging application in water treatment, which enhances the degradation efficiency in AOPs by the nanostructure. Zhang et al.[213] incorporated the concept of spatial confinement into the photothermal process and demonstrated that the temperature within the hollow carbon nanospheres was significantly higher than the solution temperature under solar irradiation. As a result, the degradation efficiency of pollutants in the photothermal spatial confinement system was 7.1 times higher compared to the photothermal system without nanoconfinement. It is crucial to further develop these emerging applications combined with the photothermal process, as they can breathe new life into the photothermal process for water treatment and can hold immeasurable benefits for the world.

In addition to the challenges of each application listed separately above, there remain some common obstacles that must be addressed. These include the complexity of preparing highquality carbonaceous or composite materials, as well as the difficulty in degrading and cyclically utilizing carbonaceous materials in water treatment. Furthermore, all of these applications are staying at the experimental stage. Although many studies have shown promising results in terms of stability and minimal loss in evaporation systems, longer test studies are required to develop them into practical applications. Moreover, most of the research into enhancing the photothermal properties of carbonaceous materials is confined to the design of external structures and morphologies. However, the limited machinability also restricts the delicate design of the structure to improve photothermal efficiency. Therefore, further exploration is needed to improve photothermal properties at a deeper level. It is also worth noting that while the $\pi$ -conjugated structure of carbonaceous materials plays a crucial role in determining their properties, most research has focused on catalytic and electrochemical properties rather than photothermal conversion properties. Therefore, more efforts in this direction are necessary. Obviously, there are great gaps between experimental applications and real-world applications. However, once these challenges are overcome, environmentally friendly and constantly solar-powered water treatment technologies will have the widest applications in the future.

# 5.3. Environmental Implication

Water resources are closely linked to human activity, but clean water resources are scarce. Most water treatment technologies currently consume large amounts of energy. Signally, the photothermal process is driven by inexhaustible solar energy, which addresses the balance between environmental remediation and energy consumption. In addition, carbonaceous materials are cheap, as well as have excellent photothermal performance and low potential for secondary pollution. The water treatment using a carbonaceous materials-based photothermal process is efficient and environmentally friendly. Therefore, the development and challenges of carbonaceous materials-based photothermal processes in water treatment attract a lot of interest.
