# . Introduction

The wide occurrence of antibiotics in wastewater, caused by the increasing consumption of pharmaceuticals, has become one of the major environmental concerns [1]. To date, antibiotics including their transformation products have been detected in aquatic environments of 71 countries across the world [2]. Even in trace levels, antibiotics are reported to cause adverse chronic effects on human health as well as ecological systems [3]. As an emerging pharmaceutical pollutant, sulfamethoxazole (SMx) has been frequently found in wastewater, surface water, and even groundwater [4]. The concentrations of SMX in landfill leachate was reported to be $0 . 2 9 \mu \mathrm { g / L }$ in China, which was comparable to those from other countries, e.g. USA and Japan [4]. However, it is difficult to degrade SMx in conventional wastewater treatment and membrane treatment processes because of its low biodegradability and perennial resistance.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) based on the activation of peroxydisulfate (PDS) for the generation of sulfate radicals $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ is one of the most expedient technologies for degradation of refractory compounds. With a higher redox potential (2.53.1V) and longer halflife $( 3 - 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 } \ : s )$ than hydroxyl radical $( ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { O H } )$ , $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - }$ has the potential of becoming a viable alternative to 'OH [5]. Besides, $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - }$ is more selective than $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ that it mainly attacks emerging contaminants (e.g., antipyrine, sulfamethazine, ciprofloxacin, methyl paraben and so on) via electron transfer [1,6]. Alternative approaches to generate $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - }$ include the activation of PDS at a wide pH by ultraviolet radiation (UV) [7], heat [8], carbon catalysts [9] and transition metals [1012]. Of all the activation methods evaluated, transition metal minerals have attracted most attention, probably due to their abundant existence in natural fields where in situ PDS oxidation is expected to be applied in environmental remediation. Manganese dioxide $\left( \mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 } \right)$ is one of the most ubiquitous mineral, and is regarded as a feasible material due to its high stability, low cost, natural abundance and unique $\mathrm { M n } ^ { - 2 + } / \mathrm { M n } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { M n } ^ { 4 + }$ redox cycles [13,14]. However, according to literatures, the catalytic reactivity of $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ alone was relatively low for activation of PDS for the degradation of refractory organic contaminants, and this impeded its potential for wasterwater treatment applications [15]. Ma et al. observed that $\mathbf { M n O _ { 2 } }$ have shown reactivity in activating peroxymonosulfate (PMS) rather than PDS, presenting $9 6 \%$ of sulfachloropyridazine removal in $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ while $3 1 \%$ in the $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ -PDS system [16]. Zhu et al. found that $\mathbf { M n O _ { 2 } }$ could act as an PDS activator to generate non-radical singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ from decomposition of metastable manganese intermediate [17]. However, this $\mathbf { M n O _ { 2 } } / \mathbf { P D S }$ system with $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ is far from effective toward a wide range of recaltricant pollutants because of the substrate-selectivity and low oxidation potential of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } ( \mathrm { E } ^ { 0 } ( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } / 0 _ { 2 } ^ { - } ) = + 0 . 8 1 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { N H E } } )$ [18]. In addition, $\mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } }$ can also be used along with electrolysis for PDS activation, in which free radicals were reported to be responsible for contaminants degradation [19]. The production of reactive oxidizing species in the $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { P D S }$ system is likely to be modulated by applying external forces. Thus, it is of great significance to further explore the potential of $\mathbf { M n O _ { 2 } }$ for PDS activation in Mn-based field remediation.

In the past decade, thermal-activated persulfate (TAP) has been recognized as a green chemical process and exhibits great promise in generating $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ without the use of any chemicals via breaking the peroxide bonds of PDS anions (Eq. (1)). Previous studies have evaluated the feasibility of TAP for SMX degradation. Ji et al. employed heat to activate persulfate to remove sulfamethoxazole and other sulfonamides [20]. They observed that the $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ of SMX removal increased remarkably with increasing temperature. Also, Milh et al. reported that SMX was completely degraded at $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ xisen  perl []. ee, et al. significantly enhanced sMx degradation both in conventional and microwave heating by increasing the temperature to ${ \bf 1 3 0 ^ { \circ } C }$ This phenomenon is typical for many TAP oxidation reactions where more reactive species are produced at higher temperatures resulting in the faster organic pollutions removal [21]. Nevertheless, TAP usually requires high temperatures and costs for equipment and operation to eliminate contaminations. Therefore, many resechers have applied heat in combination with catalytic to increase pollutant removal efficiency. For instance, Duan et al. found that the heat started to take effects from $4 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and dramatically boosted the nitrobenzene removal rate to $1 0 0 \%$ at $7 5 \mathrm { { } ^ { \circ } C }$ in the carbon catalyst/PDS system [22]. In a study conducted by Nachiappan and Gopinath, the maximum Chemical Oxygen Demand reduction (about $9 4 . 2 5 \%$ was achieved at $7 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ during the treatment of pharmaceutical wastewater with heterogeneous fly ash-activated persulfate and heat [23]. It is hypothesized that thermal energy can excite the catalyst and produce the electron-hole pairs on its surface [24]. The free electrons react with the PDS anions to produce more $\mathrm { { \le 0 } } _ { 4 } \mathrm { { ^ \bullet - } }$ .Heat can also accelerate the thermal mobility of the reactant so that the molecular collision becomes enhanced to increase the the probabilities of effective contacts [25]. Qi et al. applied the modified-drinking water treatment residuals as an effective PDS activator for SMX degradation. They observed that the removal rate of SMX increased from 80 to $9 9 \%$ with the increasing reaction temperature from 25 to $4 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ [26]. Therefore, thermal activation could be considered as a promotor for the catalytic activation of PDS for pollutants removal. However, rare studies were conducted to investigate the synergy between metallic catalysts and heat for PDS activation. Integration of $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ and heat can be a promising hybrid activator for PDS to evolve $S O _ { 4 } { } ^ { \bullet - }$ and •OH. The $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ /heat/PDS system can surmount several barriers which are manifested at other processes such as acidic condition in Fenton oxidation, long reaction time in biological processes, desorption in adsorption process and sludge production in coagulation treatment.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Chemicals

Sulfamethoxazole (SMX), sulfamethazine (SMZ), ciprofloxacin (CIP), acetonitrile (ACN, $9 9 . 9 \% )$ , rhodamine B (RhB), acid Orange 7 (AO7), benzoquinone (BQ), and sodium azide $\mathrm { ( N a N _ { 3 } ) }$ were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Corporation. # 2.2. Preparation and characterization of $M n O _ { 2 }$

$\mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } }$ was synthesized using a hydrothermal method according to Yang et al. with some modification [27]. The detailed preparation information was provided in Supporting Material (SI). The material mineralogical identity and structure were determined by D/Max-IIIA X-ray diffractometer (XRD) (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) equipped with $\mathrm { C u \mathrm { - K } \alpha }$ radiation at an accelerating voltage of $4 0 \mathrm { k V }$ and current of $4 0 \mathrm { m A }$ The morphology of the $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ was investigated by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Quanta450 FEG, FEI, USA). The surface elemental composition of $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ was determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha with AlKα radiation).

# 2.3. Experimental procedures

Batch experiments were conducted in $2 5 0 \mathrm { m l }$ beakers containing 100 mL of SMX solution at $5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (except for experiments studying the temperature effect) in a water bath kettle. The initial pH was adjusted to 7.0 by diluted NaOH or $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } S O _ { 4 } }$ except for experiments investigating the pH effect. The reaction was initiated by subsequent addition of predetermined amount of PDS and $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ under a mechanically stirring. At certain time intervals, $1 \mathrm { m L }$ of samples was withdrawn and immediately mixed with $1 \ \mathrm { m L }$ of sodium thiosulfate to quench the reaction. The mixed solution was then filtered through $0 . 2 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ membrane filters for further quantitative analysis. Furthermore, comparative studies of SMX removal by $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ alone ${ ( 1 ~ g / \mathrm { L } ) }$ , PDS alone $( 4 ~ \mathrm { m m o l / L }$ , $0 . 5 \mathrm { m L }$ , $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 } /$ PDS, $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ /heat, PDS/heat, and $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } } ,$ /heat/PDS were carried out under the same conditions. All experiments were conducted in duplicate.

To probe the generated free radicals and singlet oxygen, the radical scavengers of methanol (MeOH, 5 and $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ ), tert-butyl alcohol (TBA, 5 and $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ , sodium azide $\mathrm { ( N a N _ { 3 } }$ ,2 and $5 \mathrm { m M }$ ) and rhodamine B (RhB, $1 0 \ \mathrm { m g / L } )$ were added into the $\scriptstyle \mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } } ,$ /heat/PDS system. Benzoquinone (BQ, $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ )was employed to detect the existence of the superoxide intermediates. In addition, an electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer (JES-FA200, Japan) was applied to detect the reactive oxygen species (ROS) by using DMPO and TEMP as the respective ROS capturers.

# 2.5. Analytical methods

The concentration of residual SMx was measured by highperformance liquid chromatography (HPLC, RIGOL3000, China) with a C18 column ${ \mathrm { ~ 7 ~ } } \mu \mathrm { m } .$ $4 . 6 \times 2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m m } )$ . # 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Synergy between $M n O _ { 2 }$ and heat for persulfate activation

Persulfate activation in various systems were investigated in terms of SMX degradation under the same reaction conditions. As shown in Fig. 1 (a), only a slight degradation of SMX was noticed by individual $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ . In presence of both $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ and PDS, no obvious improvement of SMX removal was observed, theoretically implying that neither $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ adsorption nor $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ catalysis were fruitful for SMX elimination (the leached Mn ion can be ruled out because $\mathrm { M n ^ { 2 + } }$ ions are not reactive for PDS activation [17]). The low removal efficiency of SMX in this system might be attributed to the aggregation of $\mathrm { { M n O } } _ { 2 } ,$ which dramatically decreased the surface area and accessible active sites to inhibit catalytic activity [28]. Thermal activation of PDS at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ showed a higher efficiency than $\mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } }$ , but the overall rate of SMX degradation still stayed at a low level of $2 5 . 2 \%$ . This observation was consistent the previous report, in which SMX could not be removed effectively by heat (50C) in the process of interacting with PDS [1]. In contrast, a complete SMX degradation was observed within $1 5 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ in the $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ /heat/PDS system where PDS was activated by $\mathbf { M n O _ { 2 } }$ and heat simultaneously, and the loss of SMX was accompanied by a decrease a of PDS (Fig. S2). Besides, the SMx removal in these oxidation systems followed the pseudo-first-order kinetic, and the $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ values for heat/PDS and $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ /heat/PDS were 0.0018 and $0 . 0 2 0 2 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. The high removal rate of SMX in the $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ /heat/PDS system was probably owing to the potential synergistic effect between $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ and heat. According to previous research conducted to explore the ability of the TAP/ZnO-GAC oxidation system for AB113 degradation, the synergistic effect of heat and ZnO-GAC was measured to be only $1 8 . 3 6 \%$ but the removal efficiency of AB113 reached the maximum in the TAP/ZnO-GAC system [29]. Furthermore, in the treatment process of carbamazepine by the US/UV-C/Fe2+/persulfate $/ \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ oxidation system, their synergistic effect between $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { U V / U S }$ was determined to be $5 8 . 6 \%$ [30]. In our study, we calculated the synergy percentage between $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ and heat by Eq. (2) during the activation of PDS [31].

# 3.6. Implication

We report herein the $\mathbf { M n O _ { 2 } }$ /heat/PDS system for hazardous organic compounds degradation. The $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 } ,$ /heat-activated PDS-based AOPs presented several remarkable superiorities that were ideally fitted to wastewater treatment applications. First, the system exhibits a promising oxidation technology for treatment of various contaminants, containing heterocyclic groups, aniline, carboxyphenyl and azo benzene. Intriguingly, the reactivity of $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } } ,$ /heat/PDS system is unselective to the organic substrate. As depicted in Fig. S7, complete degradations of five organic pollutants (ciprofloxacin, sulfamethazine, bisphenol A, acid orange 7, rhodamine B) and $9 8 \%$ bisphenol S were rapidly achieved in $1 8 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ , respectively. The $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ /heat/PDS oxidation system was based on a radical regime at ambient temperature that the PDS molecule was first activated and released free radicals. The resulting strong oxidants, both $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and 'OH can continue to react with pollutions of concern. These prossess may result in the generation of secondary radicals $\left( \mathbb { R } \bullet \right)$ which can propagate further oxidations. the turn-over of these active substances is also high, while the concentrations of these oxidation intermediates are very low [8]. Thus, different contaminants could be efficiently removed. Second, the effectiveness of the $\mathrm { { M n O } } _ { 2 } ,$ /heat/PDS oxidation system makes a presumption for the application in specific high-temperature wastewater for pollutions degradation. Some industries such as palm oil extraction, petroleum refining, textile, fertilizer, cardboard, paper and furfural manufacturing industries [29,64] have hot wastewater including organic matter. The thermal potential of these sewage can be combined with the $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ to activate PDS without the need for an extra heat input. Thirdly, Milh et al. illustrated that SMX was completely degraded at $7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with the existent of persulfate [1], while $1 0 0 \%$ SMX could be removed at a lower temperature condition $( 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ with $\mathrm { { M n O } _ { 2 } }$ in our work. Because the activation energy of the reaction is responsible for the SMX elimination reduced in the presence of $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ Thus, the $\mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } / h e a t / P D S }$ system acquires less heat for wasterwater treatment to reduce energy consumption and cost. However, the performance of the optimized process should be further evaluated for the treatment of real effluents released from landfill leachate and pharmaceutical factory. The reaction kinetics and utilization rate of PDS in the existent of various ions conditions could be further understood by experimental researches.

# 4. Conclusion

Our study presents that $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ and heat can activate PDS through chain reactions to generate $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ for effective degradation of SMX. Approximately $1 0 0 \%$ of SMX was degraded by the $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ /heat/ PDS in $1 5 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ with a pseudo-first-order $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ of $0 . 0 2 0 2 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . The concentration of PDS showed a positive correlation with oxidative performance. The $\scriptstyle \mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } } ,$ /heat/PDS system exhibited significant superiority, especially in neutral conditions. The removal of SMX in this system depends largely on the synergistic effect $( 8 8 . 6 2 \% )$ . Scavenging experiments, chemical probes, EPR and XPS were used to investigate the mechanism of SMX degradation. SMX was mainly oxidized by $S O _ { 4 } { } ^ { \bullet - }$ and •OH. In addition, three possible degradation pathways of SMX including cleavage of S-N bond, electron transfer, hydroxylation for benzene ring and dehydrogenation of the amino group were proposed. After four consecutive oxidation cycles, the $\mathrm { M n O _ { 2 } }$ catalyst maintained its favorable performance. In a word, thermal-catalyst synergistic activation of PDS is a promising oxidation method for degrading contaminants effectively.
