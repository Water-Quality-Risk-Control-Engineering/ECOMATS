# Abstract

One-pot design of heteroatoms doped carbocatalysts for remediation of wastewater via persulfate (PS) activation is strongly desirable. Herein, a novel nitrogen and sulfur doped and co-doped carbons with controllable N, S doping is rationally prepared using pyrolysis strategy from polypyrrole-polythiophene polymers and copolymer. The N, S co-doped carbon (NS-C) show highly catalytic oxidation compared by N doped carbon (N-C) and S doped carbon (SC) due to the synergistic effect between $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S dopants. It’s supposed that N and S doping can effectively create highly and main active sites to the electron transfer via PS activation, which could generate reactive oxygen species (ROS). Structure-dependent activity during activation of PS was confirmed by quenching experiments and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) analysis, prove that the non-radical species $( ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } )$ is the main ROS generated. To improve the carbocatalyst separation and stability, the hydrogel beads-based alginate NS${ \mathbf { } } \mathbf { C } @ $ Alginate were easily prepared. The catalytic efficiency rate by using the real water matrix shows that the NS-C/PS synergic system non-radical-led pathway exhibited a high ability to remediation of surrounding inorganic or organic compounds. This approach provides an innovative method for production and proves an efficient and environmentally benign carbocatalyst to PS activation for practical applications.

Keywords: Conjugated polymers; N, S doped carbon; Persulfate activation; Synergistic effect, hydrogel beads.

# 1. Introduction

In the past few years, environmental pollution, resulting from excessive discharges of toxic organic molecules from different types of industries, has become a global world issue [1]. In fact, the traditional treatment methods used remain helpless to remove these organic pollutants from water, due mainly to their low biodegradability [2]. Thus, new efficient wastewater treatment processes are strongly desirable for risk mitigation and to maintain the industrial development [2]. Thus, the advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), based on persulfate (PS) and peroxymonosulfate (PMS) (SR-AOPs), were found to be the most appropriate in the decomposition reaction of the organic pollutants present in wastewater. Hence, upon the PS or the PMS activation, sulfate radicals $\mathrm { ( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } ) }$ which have great potential in eliminating harmful pollutants in wastewater, are generated [3–9]. In fact, SO4•-species have high oxidative potentials $\mathrm { E _ { 0 } } = + 2 . 5$ to $+ 3 . 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs NHE), longer life spans $( 1 _ { 1 / 2 } = 3 0 \mathrm { - } 4 0$ μs of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ vs $\mathbf { t } _ { 1 / 2 } = \mathbf { \tau } < 1$ μs of HO•), and could be used in a wide pH range, so they are considered as promising alternative in comparison to the classic Fenton-type reaction [10,11].

Today, many studies have focused on the efficient catalysts development dealing with the PS and PMS activation for the production of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals [2, 12, 13]. Recall that conventional heterogeneous catalysts, such as metallic materials, have been shown to be effective also in the PS and PMS activation processes [7, 14, 15]. However, possible secondary pollution, by the metal catalysts and toxic metals leaching, has limited their practical applications. Therefore, the development of stable, non-toxic and efficient metal-free catalysts in the SR-AOPs, is becoming more recommendable and strongly desirable. Based on these considerations, several researchers have focused their attention on the development of carbon materials due to their excellent catalytic performance, their large surface areas, their environmental friendly, their good thermal stability and their porous structures [6, 16–19]. In addition, carbon materials, such as graphene oxide (GO), carbon nanodiamond (CND), carbon nanotubes (CNT), have been used, as metal-free carbocatalysts, for PS and PMS activation, to remove organic contaminants [20–22]. Despite their porous structures and large surface areas, it was reported that the virgin carbon materials have poor catalytic performance, limiting, hence, their applications in the PS and PMS activation [23]. A strategy to improve the catalytic activity of the carbocatalysts, consists in doping heteroatoms such as nitrogen (N) and/or sulfur (S), into the carbon structure. Such strategy is considered as a feasible way to create more active sites on the catalyst surface [23–27]. The doping heteroatoms can change the chemical inertia by disrupting the electron structure of the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ hybrid carbon. Further, the difference in electronegativity between the carbon atoms and the doping heteroatoms generates a difference in the electron density and creates the active sites in adjacent carbons. Therefore the heteroatoms doping process in carbon materials can be considered as an effective strategy to increase the presence of active sites onto the material surface, leading to non-radical pathway degradation caused by the generated singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } )$ [2, 11, 27–31]. Moreover, the N, S co-doping onto the carbon surface, as compared to the simple $_ \mathrm { N }$ or S doping, enhances further the catalytic activity of the carbocatalysts thanks to the synergistic effects of the N and S dopants [1, 23, 28, 29, 32,3 3]. Recently, most N, S co-doped carbons have been prepared by adding external sources of nitrogen and sulfur [18, 24, 27]. Such preparation method was found to be complicated, and it usually suffered from the instability of the $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S doping atoms [24–26]. However, these synthesis problems could be solved by the preparation of the N, S co-doped carbon by direct carbonization of the precursors containing both $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S atoms. In this way, based on reported works [9, 19–21], doped carbon, having stable heteroatoms, N or S, in the conjugated structures were obtained after direct carbonization of certain conjugated polymers. Thus, it was shown, in that reported works, that the polypyrrole (PPy) derived N doped carbon could be used in heterogeneous degradation of organic pollutants [24]. Likewise, polythiophene (PTh), which could be used as a sulfur source, could be considered as a qualified candidate to prepare the S doped carbon for the PS and the PMS activation [11, 17, 26]. In addition, the use of one-pot synthesis of the conjugated polymers rich in heteroatoms, rich in the $\pi$ -conjugated robust skeleton, and their carbon-rich nature, gives promising precursors candidates, in comparison to others precursors for the preparation of heteroatoms doped-carbon having unique features: (1) it generates a significant amount of heteroatoms doping in the carbon structure which yield more active sites for the PS activation; (2) the intrinsic robust conjugated structure confers good framework carbonizability and nanostructural inheritability; (3) the heteroatoms can be incorporated in situ into the carbon framework with uniform distributions using conjugated polymers; (4) the aromatic conjugated nature of the polymers is prone to promote the formation of a graphitic structure having high graphitization degree; (5) the conjugated networks used in preparing carbons can themselves be readily manipulated by variation of the monomers and regulation of polymerization protocols, thus allowing the control over both chemical compositions and nanostructures of the resulting carbons [34].

In the present work, to highlight the synergistic effect between the dopants, we have designed a new N, S co-doped carbon (NS-C) catalyst, obtained after the direct carbonization of PPy-co-PTh copolymer. To assess the catalytic activity enhancement resulting from the codoping, the activity of the N, S co-doped carbon sample was compared to the activities of the N doped carbon (N-C) and the S doped carbon (S-C) samples [35]. The N-C and the S-C samples were obtained after direct carbonization of, PPy and PTh polymers, respectively. Note that prior their uses in the pollutant degradation reactions, all carbocatalysts were characterized by various methods. In addition, the orange G (OG), atrazine (ATZ) and bisphenol A (BPA), were selected as the target pollutants, and the persulfate (PS) was used as oxidant, under various experimental conditions. Further, based on the experimental identification of ROS, we also propose mechanisms describing the PS activation and the organic pollutants degradation in the $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ -based non-radical oxidative pathway. Furthermore, it was also evidenced that NS-C had a high efficiency activating PS for degradation of organic pollutants in practical applicability for real water treatment. This study aimed to develop a new carbocatalysts for the persulfate activation in practical applications.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Preparation of NS-C, N-C and S-C carbocatalysts

The detailed used chemicals are displayed in Text S1.Firstly, the polypyrrole (PPy) and the polythiophene (PTh) polymers were prepared following simple polymerization method, as shown in our previous works [2,35]. Regarding the copolymer polypyrrole-copolythiophene (PPy-co-PTh), it was prepared using easy polymerization process based on the oxidation of a mixture of two monomers (thiophene, $0 . 5 \mathrm { \textrm { m L } }$ ) and (pyrrole, $0 . 5 \mathrm { \textrm { m L } }$ ). Thereafter, the obtained polymers PPy, PTh, and the copolymer PPy-co-PTh, were pyrolyzed at $8 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in a tubular furnace with the heating rate of $5 ~ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ under nitrogen argon atmosphere for $^ { 3 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The final products were denoted as, N-C (from PPy), S-C (from PTh, the preparation, characterization and application of this carbocatalyst were shown in our previous work, and they are used here for comparison [35]) and NS-C (from PPy-co-PTh) (Fig. 1).

# 2.2. Design of the alginate hydrogel beads (NS-C@Alg)

The hydrogel NS-C $@$ Alg beads, used in this work, were prepared as follows: $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g }$ of alginate powder was first dissolved in $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of distilled water during 5 hours of stirring, then $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g }$ of NS-C carbocatalyst was added, and the resulting mixture was stirred for 5 hours. On another side, a $\mathrm { C a C l } _ { 2 }$ solution $\mathbf { ( 0 . 1 \ M ) }$ was separately prepared and kept under low stirring in a crystallizer. The hydrogels beads were then obtained by mixing both solutions above, dropwise, and by using a pump. It should be noted that when each droplet of the mixture NS-C/Alginate was brought into contact with the $\mathrm { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ ions, core-shell particles were then formed, with a core consisting of immobilized NS-C, and a shell resulting from the complexation occurring between the $\mathrm { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ ions and the alginate carboxylic groups. Finally, the hydrogel beads, labeled as NS-C@Alg, were washed several times with distilled water and then stored in an aqueous medium before their use.

# 2.3. Characterization

The composition and chemical states of the NS-C, N-C and S-C surfaces were obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) VG SCIENTA, Model SES-200. Raman spectra measurements were acquired on a Raman spectroscopy apparatus $\mathrm { \mathrm { D X R } } ^ { \mathrm { T M } } 2$ , Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the range of $3 0 0 ^ { - 1 }$ to $8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ , with a $5 3 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ wavelength incident laser light. The prepared carbocatalysts $\mathrm { X }$ -ray diffraction spectra were collected on X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical MPD X'Pert Pro diffractometer) operating with Cu Kα radiation $\Lambda { = } 0 . 1 5 4 1 8 \mathrm { n m }$ ). The morphologies of the samples were observed by field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI model Quanta 400) and transmission electron microscopy (Philips CM 200). The specific surface area (SBET) and the average pore volume $\mathrm { ( V _ { p o r e } ) }$ were measured according to the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) adsorption method by using Micrometrics Analyzer, ASAP 2020 instrument.

# 2.4. Catalytic Activity Evaluation

The catalyst activity was assessed based on the removal rate of OG by activating the PS in the carbocatalysts/PS systems. Thus, in a typical degradation experiment, a given amount of the catalyst $( 0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { g / L } )$ was added to a $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of OG $( 1 5 \mathrm { ~ m g ~ L ^ { - 1 } } ) $ ) aqueous solution, constantly stirred and kept at room temperature. Then, $4 \ \mathrm { m M }$ of the PS was added to the solution to start the catalytic reaction. At specific time intervals, $2 \ \mathrm { \ m L }$ samples were withdrawn, and filtered through filter membranes $\mathrm { 0 . 4 5 ~ \mu m } )$ . It should be noted that other pollutants were also tested such as ATZ and BPA, under the same experimental conditions as the OG removal from water. In order to identify the reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated upon the PS activation, amounts of radical scavengers such as ethanol (EtOH) and tert-butylalcohol (TBA), were added in the reaction medium to trap $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and HO•, respectively. In addition, sodium azide $\left( \mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 } \right)$ was used as quencher for $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . Note that the experimental tests were performed in duplicate or triplicate yielding measured parameters mean values and their corresponding error bars which were represented in the form of a graph. The residual concentrations of the various pollutants such OG, ATZ and BPA were assessed by UV-Vis spectrophotometer (6705 UV/Vis JENWAY) at wavelengths of $\lambda { = } 4 7 8$ , 554 and $2 3 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively. Further, in order to identify the presence of the ROS in the degradation process, we conducted in situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) analyzes. Thus, for each analyzed solution, a volume of $5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ of the reaction medium was transferred in a glass capillary sealed with Crit-O-seal TM and then placed in an ESR-tube with an outside diameter of $5 \mathrm { ~ m m }$ . N-tert-butyl- $\mathfrak { a }$ -phenylnitrone (PBN) and 2,2,6,6-teramethypiperidine (TEMP) were used as spin trap agents. Continuous-wave EPR spectra were recorded at room temperature and in aerated medium using an EMX-plus X-band spectrometer (Bruker). For the stability carbocatalysts tests, the catalyst was separated after each run, thereafter it was washed with a solvent mixture of ethanol /distilled water, three times, and finally, it was dried at $3 3 3 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { K }$ for its reuse.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Physicochemical Structures

The morphologies of the NS-C, N-C and S-C carbocatalysts were studied by SEM and TEM image analysis. The SEM images of N-C and NS-C (Figs. S1 a-c) show irregularly shaped spherical particles that are uniformly dispersed, with slight agglomeration for the particles of the S-C material. TEM images of NS-C, N-C and S-C (Figs. S1d-f) also show the same pattern of nanoparticles for N-C and NS-C carbocatalysts. For the SC sample, the TEM image shows a disordered and irregular structure of the sulfur-doped carbon matrix, which is mainly attributed to the loss of heteroatoms and graphitization of carbon occurring during the thermal decomposition process of the polymers.

High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) images were obtained and used to evaluate the interlayer spacings in the NS-C, N-C and S-C materials. The HR-TEM images (Fig. 2a-f) confirm that S-C has the highest interlayer spacing distance $\left( 0 . 3 7 \ \mathrm { n m } \right)$ , compared to NSC $\left( 0 . 3 6 { \mathrm { n m } } \right)$ ) and N-C $\mathrm { 0 . 3 4 \ n m } ,$ ) samples. In fact, in the case of S-C, the S atoms influence the degree of disorder and create many defects in the carbon matrix structure [12,29]. Therefore, the incorporation of $_ \mathrm { N }$ or S heteroatoms could lead to carbon matrix deformation. In addition, XRD analyses were used to confirm the amorphous structure and the interlayer space of the NS-C, N-C and S-C (Fig. 2g). The XRD confirms the characteristic peaks of the carbon structure where the (002) and (100) planes were assigned to the amorphous carbon planes[36– 38]. The (002) plane of the S-C, NS-C and N-C materials show the diffraction angle positioned at $2 4 . 3 ^ { \circ }$ , $2 4 . 7 ^ { \circ }$ and $2 5 . 8 ^ { \circ }$ respectively (Fig. 4 d), reflecting the interlayer spacing of about 3.66 $\mathring \mathrm { A }$ for the S-C, 3.60 Å for the NS-C and 3.45 $\mathring \mathrm { A }$ for the N-C according to Bragg's law. These differences in behavior between the S and $_ \mathrm { N }$ doped carbon are due to the larger covalent radius of S compared to N. These results are in good agreement with the HR-TEM analyses (Table S2). It should be noted that these interlayer spacing values obtained from the XRD and HR-TEM analyses are higher than the value reported in the literature for graphite (3.40 Å) [29,37].In addition, the average lateral length (La) and thickness (Lc) of the graphitic segments were also calculated from the XRD analyses. The number (N) of stacked graphene sheets is obtained by $\Nu = \mathrm { L c } / \mathrm { d } _ { 0 0 2 }$ (Table S2). As expected La, Lc and $_ \mathrm { N }$ increase with the doping of the $_ \mathrm { N }$ heteroatom due to the development of long-range ordering[39,40]. Raman analysis was performed to investigate the graphitic structure of carbocatalysts (Fig. 2h). The results show two characteristic peaks, corresponding to the D $1 3 5 8 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ ) and G $( 1 5 7 1 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ ) bands. Usually, the G band is related to the graphitic order and it is associated with the $\displaystyle \operatorname { s p } ^ { 2 }$ electronic configuration of the carbon, while the D band is related to the degree of disorder or structural defects in the carbon matrix [35, 36, 40–42]. The calculated integral intensity ratio $\mathrm { ( I _ { G } / I _ { D } ) }$ is used to assess the level of graphitic order (Table S2) [12, 40]. The calculated IG/ID ratios for the carbon materials were equal to 1.100 (for S-C), 1.107 (for NS-C) and 1.114 (for N-C), these ratios express the degree of graphitization and confirm that the S-C catalyst has the highest degree of defect, compared to the NS-C and N-C samples, indicating that the S-C has the highest defect sites, lower graphitization which can be attributed to the presence of a high concentration of S in the carbon structure, in good agreement with the XRD and HRTEM data. The textural characteristics of the three materials were studied by the ${ \bf N } _ { 2 }$ desorption and adsorption isotherms of the samples are shown in Fig. 2f. Thus, as can be seen in Fig. 2i, the materials reveal hybrid type I/IV profiles, indicating hierarchical pore size distributions, as also confirmed in Fig. S3 representing the pore size distributions, and Table S2 showing the different total pore volumes of the samples. The BET specific surface areas of the S-C, NS-C and N-C materials are 306.96, 172.23 and $1 6 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ respectively. These results show that the introduction of sulfur into the carbon matrix increases the defects which lead to an increase in the surface area and favor the exposure of the active sites by promoting the rapid electrons transfer involved in the catalytic process.

# 3.2. Catalytic Performances for the PS Activation

The catalytic activities of the carbocatalysts obtained for the oxidation of the organic pollutants such as orange G (OG), atrazine (ATZ) and bisphenol A (BPA) via PS activation were carried out. Thus, the catalytic and the adsorption performances of the prepared NS-C, N-C and S-C carbocatalysts are shown in Figs. 4a, 4b. These figures show that the PS alone could hardly degrade OG in the absence of catalysts, suggesting that inert persulfate molecules can barely generate reactive radicals without any activation. The OG elimination by adsorption is very inspiring that about $45 \%$ , $40 \%$ and $30 \%$ of OG can be eliminated thanks to the powerful enrichment effects of carbon materials. The OG adsorption led to adsorbed quantities of 117, 102 and $7 5 \mathrm { m g g ^ { - 1 } }$ for S-C, NS-C and N-C respectively, as shown in Fig. 4b. These demonstrate that a significant pollutant adsorption capacity, resulting from the large surface area of the catalyst could facilitate the pollutant oxidation reaction occurring on the carbocatalyst surface. In addition, the use of the carbocatalysts/PS systems, can further enhance the OG removal efficiency. Thus, the S-C carbocatalyst reaches $87 \%$ of OG elimination in $2 8 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ with a reaction rate of $0 . 0 6 3 \ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , while the N-C achieves a total degradation in $2 8 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ with a speed constant of $0 . 1 4 1 \ \mathrm { \ m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ . The NS-C sample further improves and strengthens the catalytic activity, and OG removal can be completed in $1 6 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ with a faster reaction rate constant reached at $0 . 2 7 4 ~ \mathrm { { m i n ^ { - 1 } } }$ . Similarly, the ATZ elimination is highly important in the case of the NS-C $( 0 . 1 9 8 \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ ) compared to the N-C $( 0 . 0 9 8 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ ) and S-C $( 0 . 0 5 5 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 } $ ). BPA oxidation is also highly efficient on the NS-C/PS system $( 0 . 1 4 1 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - }$ 1) than N-C/PS (0.074) and S-C/PS (0.05). Generally, the difference in the catalytic performance may be due to the surface chemical compositions, the bonding configurations and the SBET of the carbocatalyst. The introduction of $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S can break the structure of the carbon materials inertia and create high charge densities, due to different electronegativity [45, 46]. As a result, N atoms can create a highly positive charge on adjacent carbon atoms than sulfur due to its large electronegativity, explaining hence the significant effect of N-C as compared to S-C on the catalytic process [29]. In addition, the XPS results show significant percentage doping of N and S in the carbon structures, leading to the abundance of active sites C-N and C-S, especially graphitic N and thiophenic S. In short, the best catalytic performance of NS-C is based not only on the factors mentioned above, but also on the synergistic effect occurring between the N and the S atoms during the pollutant degradation process. The results suggest that the NS-C/PS system can be efficiently applied to a wide range of organic contaminants. Furthermore, the TOC removal efficiencies, as measured upon the organic pollutant degradation reactions by the NS-C/PS, N-C/PS and S-C/PS systems are shown in

Fig. S4. Thus, around $68 \%$ of OG, $52 \%$ of ATZ and $47 \%$ of BPA were mineralized into $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and water, within $3 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , by the NS-C catalyst. Like ways, the N-C and S-C catalysts have shown also important values in TOC removals of OG, ATZ and BPA pollutants (Fig. S4), revealing that the various carbocatalyst/PS systems exhibit good mineralization ability.

# 3.3. Identification of Radicals and Activation Mechanism

The existence of the reactive oxygen species (ROS) occurring during the catalytic processes in the carbocatalysts/PS systems was confirmed by different scavenger experiments. Generally, the ethanol (EtOH) and the tert-butanol (TBA) were used as a radical deactivation for the $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and OH• in the catalytic process. EtOH can trap both OH• $( \bf k = 9 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } M ^ { - 1 } \ s ^ { - 1 } )$ and $\begin{array} { l } { { \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } { ' } { ' } ( k } } } \end{array} = \begin{array} { l } { { 2 . 5 } } \end{array} \times \begin{array} { l } { { 1 0 ^ { 7 } } } \end{array} { } { \bf M ^ { - 1 } } \mathrm {  ~ \ s ^ { - 1 } ) }$ , while TBA highly reacts with OH• $k = 3 . 8 -$ $7 . 6 { \times } 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } ,$ [12,46–49]. As shown in Figs.5a-c, the addition of EtOH and TBA with a high concentration in the reaction process, showed very limited inhibition effects in catalytic degradation, suggesting that the $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \cdot$ -and OH• radicals were not the mains ROS for OG oxidation in the NS-C/PS, NC/PS and SC/PS systems. On the other hand, the p-Benzoquinone (p-BQ) was also used to confirm the existence of the superoxide anion radical $( \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ , and react with a reaction rate constant $( 9 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ ) [49,50]. Fig.5b shows that the contribution of the anion radical $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ can be easily excluded, because the p-BQ was found to have negligible impact on the catalytic process and the reaction rate constant decreased slightly from 0.274 to $0 . 2 2 4 \ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ , in the NS-C/PS system. In addition, the sodium azide $\mathbf { ( N a N _ { 3 } ) }$ was used as a selective scavenger, to deactivate the non-radical $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ species with a reaction rate constant $( 1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { s } ^ { - 1 } ,$ )[12,25,51]. Thus, a strong inhibition effect occurred upon the NaN3addition, as depicted in Figs. 5a-c. The reaction rate constant value dropped significantly, from 0.274 to $0 . 0 4 2 9 \mathrm { \ m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ for the NS-C/PS system, from 0.141 to $0 . 0 3 1 4 \mathrm { ~ m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ for the N-C/PS system, and from 0.063 to $0 . 0 2 8 \ \mathrm { m i n ^ { - 1 } }$ for the S-C/PS system. These results indicate clearly that the non-radical $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ is the main active species to the OG degradation in different carbocatalysts systems. To confirm the ROS scavengers results, the electronic paramagnetic resonance (EPR) was used, by using the TEMP as a spin trapping agent for $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ [51, 52], and PBN as a spin trapping agent for $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and OH• in the various carbocatalysts systems [12, 34]. Then, when PS alone was involved in the various catalytic processes in the presence of PBN or TEMP agent straight lines were observed, indicating that no radicals was generated (Figs. 5 d, e). In the others hand, evident characteristic peaks were observed when the using the carbocatalysts/PS systems. Thus, the results were clearly show the characteristic signals in the presence of PBN confirmed the generation of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } { \bullet } _ { - }$ and OH• radicals in the carbocatalyst/PS systems (Fig. 5 d). In addition, the pics intensity of the PBN signals were higher in the NS-C/PS system compared to others, shows the highly liberation of radicals in the NS-C/PS system. Then, the peaks characteristic with a triplet signal was observed by using the TEMP, further confirming the generation of $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ in the various systems. Also, the TEMP- ${ } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ peaks in the NS-C/PS system with a highly intensity compared to other systems, indicating that the coexistants of $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S heteroatoms creates a synergistic effect and promotes the ROS generation, especially $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ . The EPR results with a good agreement with the radical’s scavenger tests, suggesting that, the non-radical pathway was proposed as the dominant pathway mechanism in the various carbocatalysts systems.

As was already mentioned above, the doping of N and S heteroatoms increase the spin-charge of the adjacent carbon atoms, facilitating hence the O-O cleavage in the PS activation[1, 33, 48, 53, 54]. Thus, by considering the XPS results, it has been suggested that large amount of the $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S doping atoms (Fig. 3), as resulting from using the conjugated polymers as a precursor, creates more of active sites on the surface catalyst. In general, it was shown that the graphitic N, and the thiophenic S have important effects on the catalytic process. In fact, the graphitic $_ \mathrm { N }$ and thiophenic S induce the electrons transfer from the adjacent positively charged carbon, which become more sensitive to adsorb the $\mathrm { S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 }$ -anions and generate the ROS, especially $^ 1 0 _ { 2 } [ 1 2 , 5 1 , 5 6 - 5 8 ]$ . However, the $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S co-doping increases the number of the adjacent carbons positive sites, promoting hence the PS adsorption and improving the generation of the ROS which easily degrade the organic pollutants via electron transfer [1, 12, 48]. The co-doping procedure has another advantage which creates a synergistic effect and results in high PS activation and organic pollutants degradations. In terms of reaction mechanism, the initial step of organic pollutants degradation, the molecules are first adsorbed by the NS-C catalyst thanks to its high surface area. In addition, the $\mathrm { S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 }$ -ions are also adsorbed on the carbocatalyst surface due to the abundance of the C-N and the C-S active sites which are created by the co-doping procedure to generate $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and OH• radicals (Eqs. 1, 3, 4, 5) [47]. Regarding the non-radical mechanism, the synergistic effect occurring between the $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S dopants improve the electrons transfer on the surface, leading to the O-O cleaving bond and generate the metastable intermediates which will be oxidized and transformed to the $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ (Eq. 2). On the other hand, the $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ can also be produced from the superoxide anion $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ as an intermediate (Eqs. 3, 4, 6) [48, 58]. Furthermore, the presence of the oxygen functional groups on the surface of NS-C such as ${ \mathrm { C } } { = } { \mathrm { O } }$ groups active sites, can accelerate the PS decomposition to generate $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ , as reported in previous section [60]. Finally, the ROS generated during the catalytic reaction will react and degrade the organic molecules, from a non-radical-led pathway. To promote the universality of the criteria proposed, in the present work, for the different non-radical SR-AOP systems, and to fully understand the synergistic mechanisms, more integrated theoretical and experimental studies will be carried out in the future.

# 3.4. Environmental applications

# 3.4.1. Role of hydrogels beads in the stability and the reusability test

The SEM images of hydrogel beads-based alginate biopolymer NS-C@Alg (Fig. 7 a) show that the NS-C carbocatalyst was located inside a matrix formed by complexation between alginate biopolymer and calcium ions, formed the spherical hydrogel beads. In addition, from the obtained mapping images, it can be confirmed that the $\mathrm { C a } ^ { 2 + }$ ions and alginate biopolymer were located on the surface of the bead.

The carbocatalysts stability and their separation after their uses, are among the important characteristics which determine if the catalysts could be reused several times and applied reasonably. The N and S doped carbon materials seriously suffer from the activity deterioration problem after their uses, due to alteration of their surface structure such as the loss of heteroatoms and the adsorption of intermediates, etc. In various reported works, most of the N, S co-doped carbons have been prepared by using an external source of nitrogen and sulfur during the carbonization process. However, this method generally suffered due to the heteroatom’s instability. In the present work, we proposed, several methods to obtain metalfree catalysts with a reasonable result during their reuses in the enhancement of stability and the decrease in the mass loss. Note that these factors are suitable for large-scale applications. Firstly, the preparation of $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S co-doped carbon material, by using one-step carbonization process on the copolymer rich in heteroatoms, increasing the stability during the carbocatalysts reuse. Secondly, the hydrogel beads NS-C $@$ Alginate metal-free catalysts were prepared by using alginate biopolymer, to increase their reusability and facilitate their separation. The carbocatalysts recycling tests are realized 5 cycles (Fig. 7 b), and the NS-C shows significant stability with a $30 \%$ decrease in the removal efficiency process and with a large mass loss limiting hence its reutilisation (Fig. S4). However, after five uses, the prepared hydrogel beads were found to be highly stable (Fig. 7 c). In addition, they show also low mass loss (Fig. S5). Thus, in order to have efficient catalytic performance during the carbocatalysts reuse, the key step is to transform them as hydrogel beads-based alginate biopolymer, before their uses. These hydrogel beads have effectively, reduced the mass loss, facilitated the recovery, and improved the stability, of the carbocatalyst. Here, the new prepared hydrogel beads NS-C $@$ Alg have stable structure that should be used in the future, mainly in the industrial degradation of organic pollutants.

# 3.4.2. Effect of the reaction parameters

The effects of the reaction parameters (the catalyst dosage, the PS dosage, the OG concentration and the solution $\mathrm { p H }$ ) on the OG degradation were investigated. As can be seen in Fig. 8c, when the NS-C carbocatalyst dosage increases from 0.1 to $0 . 4 \textrm { g L } ^ { - 1 }$ , a positive effect on the reaction rate was observed. Such behavior is due to the increase of the active sites and leading to reinforcement of the synergistic effect and resulting in an increase of the adsorbed PS and OG amounts. The PS dosage also showed a positive effect on the reaction rate constant kapp, when the PS dose increases from 3 to $6 \mathrm { m M }$ (Fig.8c). These findings could be expected, since more the PS dosage is added at the reaction medium, more the ROS are generated to degrade efficiently the OG. However, The OG has a negative effect when the dye concentration has increased (Fig. 8c) resulting mainly from the saturation of the carbocatalyst surface sites. The aqueous phase pH is also an important parameter that affects the OG degradation reaction. Thus, different initial $\mathrm { p H }$ values were examined as depicted in Fig. 8d. This figure shows that both acidic $\mathrm { p H }$ and basic $\mathrm { p H }$ , led to efficient OG degradation. Hence, the activation of PS at acidic $\mathrm { p H }$ , results from the stabilizing effect of $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ on $\mathrm { S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ -. Further, under basic pH, the carbocatalysts bear negative charges which facilitate the $\mathrm { S } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ -. In overall, the NS-C/PS system effectively degrades the OG over a wide $\mathrm { p H }$ range from 3 to 10, involving conventional free-radical oxidation reactions. The effect of the reaction temperature was also studied for the catalytic reactions NS-C/PS (Fig. S7). The temperature effect on the catalytic degradation process was investigated and the results clearly indicate that the OG degradation increases remarkably when the temperature increases. Therefore, the temperature was found to be the driving force for the target pollutant OG degradation.

# 3.4.3. Application on real water

To further explore practical applications, the effects of several common matrix species on the catalytic performance of NS-C carbocatalyst have been systematically investigated realized. The inorganic anions in an aquatic environment can act as radical deactivators to limit the catalytic oxidation performance in radical processes. The PS and PMS systems which traditionally produce $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and OH• radicals are sensitive and deactivate in the presence of inorganic anions [31,61–64]. Non-radical oxidation has been an effective strategy to overcome this practical limitation, and various previous works showed that the oxidation of organic molecules is efficient in the systems dominated by $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ [31, 59–62]. We confirmed that the NS-C/PS system dominated in a non-radical pathway according to the results below. Therefore, the effects of various anions on the degradation of various studied organic molecules such as OG, ATZ and BPA by NS-C/PS system have been investigated for their practical application. Encouragingly, and as can be shown in Figs. 9 a-c, negligible effects were observed on the diver organic molecules in the presence of ions of nitrate $( \mathrm { N O 3 } ^ { - } )$ , phosphate $\left( \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 } { } ^ { 3 - } \right)$ , bicarbonate $\left( \mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - } \right)$ and chloride $\left( \mathbf { C l } ^ { - } \right)$ . Side reactions between anions and radicals have been avoided, and high oxidation of OG, ATZ and BPA by using the NSC/PS system, have been observed.

In the other hand, the oxidation process was also achieved by using different water matrices, such as surface water, and tap water. Surface water contains inorganic ions and organic substances, and Tap water mainly contained inorganic anions [31, 62]. As shown in Figs. 9 d-f, similar phenomena were observed in the OG, ATZ and BPA oxidation, and the removal were greater than $90 \%$ in $2 1 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , in surface water and tap water. Therefore, in comparison to OG removal from distilled water, small removal efficiency deviations (less than $5 . 0 \%$ ) were observed in OG removals from surface and tap waters. These results confirm that this non-radical-led process in the NS-C/PS system which has a synergistic effect is favorable to decrease the effects of various impurities and inorganic anions. Further, this nonradical reaction has a strong elimination capacity and the NS-C/PS system exhibits incomparable advantages for the radical reaction-based PS system and has wide applicability and practical application possibilities.

# 4. Conclusion

In summary, one-step pyrolysis strategy was used to prepare new $_ \mathrm { N }$ and S doped and codoped carbon carbocatalysts from conjugated polymer (PPy and PTh) and copolymer (PPyco-PTh). Integrated characterization and experimental investigation were both conducted to identify the active sites in the N, S co-doped carbon catalysts, for the PS activation via an electron-transfer mechanism. It is shown that the heteroatoms co-doping process significantly improves the catalytic activity, producing more reactive species than the mono-doping process, due to the synergistic effect occurring between N and S doping atoms. In addition, the synergistic effect was found to depend on the heteroatoms relative position and the doping levels importance, in good agreement with the experimental data. From the quenching tests and the EPR detections, it was revealed that the significant contribution of the singlet oxygen $^ 1 { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ , produced via the non-radical pathway, and few radicals generated from the radical pathway, might also contribute to the oxidation process. Benefitting from the non-radical pathway, the NS-C/PS system shows high resistance to inorganic anions and real water matrix in an aquatic environment. On the other hand, the prepared hydrogel beads-based alginate showed excellent stability and separation after use. We believe this work will deepen the understanding of the N, S co-doped carbon synergistic effect to persulfate activation for future wastewater remediation applications.
