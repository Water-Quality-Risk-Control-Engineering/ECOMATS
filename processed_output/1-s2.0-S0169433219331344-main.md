In this study, we developed a facile mechanochemical method to prepare ultrafine nickel oxide nanosheets with preferential high energy $\left\{ 1 \ 1 \ 0 \right\}$ -facets, an ultrathin thickness of $2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and a platelet size of $2 { - } 2 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . These ultrafine nanosheets were synthesized by hand grinding hydrated copper nitrate salt with solid NaOH, while spherical nanoparticles with no preferential facets were obtained using ${ \bf N a } _ { 2 } { \bf C O } _ { 3 }$ as base instead. The ultrafine NiO nanosheets exhibited a catalytic efficiency as high as $9 8 . 3 \%$ at $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ for degrading RhB by activating persulfate (PS) at a handful of catalyst $( 0 . 3 \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L } )$ and oxidant $( 0 . 3 { \gimel } / \mathrm { L } )$ , much higher than the $6 4 . 6 \%$ efficiency of the ${ \bf N a } _ { 2 } { \bf C O } _ { 3 }$ -obtained NiO nanoparticles. The superior catalyzing ability of the ultrafine NiO nanosheet was attributed to its high proportion of the high-energy NiO $\lbrace 1 \ 1 \ 0 \rbrace$ -facets, although its specific surface area $( 6 4 . 7 4 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ was lower than that of the ${ \bf N a } _ { 2 } { \bf C O } _ { 3 }$ -obtained NiO $( 8 4 . 0 8 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ . The effects of NiO nanosheet dosage, PS concentration, initial $\mathsf { p H }$ and anions on RhB degradation were systematically investigated. The ultrafine nanosheets also exhibit low metal leaching and good recyclability. Furthermore, the mechanism for PS activation by NiO nanosheets was elucidated by Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectra, scavenger experiments and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. The results revealed that surface-bound radicals were the primary active species in the NiO nanosheet/PS system. This work offers a quick, simple, cheap, facile and scalable way to prepare highly efficient catalysts for advanced catalytic oxidation.

# 1. Introduction

During the past years, environmental pollution has become a challenging multidisciplinary problem, which was due to the miscellaneous organic pollutants and synthetic dyes discharged into the environment [1–3]. There was an incredibly difficult time in exploring an effective deep treatment method for wastewater. And all this time, recalcitrant organics in wastewater were removed mainly relying on the traditional treatment process, including adsorption and biodegradation technology [4,5]. However, most of them suffer from high cost or unstable performance [6].

Recently, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) based on highly reactive radicals are causing great interest because of its excellent degradation performance [7,8], among which AOPs based on sulfate radicals $\mathrm { ( } S 0 _ { 4 } \mathrm { ' ~ - ~ } )$ have received widespread attention in the degradation of organic contaminants from wastewater [9]. Compared with HO% , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ has a higher redox potential (2.3 to $3 . 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs $2 . 7 ~ \mathrm { V }$ , wider operative $\mathtt { p H }$ range (3–11 vs 2–4), and much longer lifetime $( 3 0 - 4 0 ~ \mu s$ $\mathrm { v s } \sim 1 ~ \mu s )$ [10]. Now therefore, more researches focuse on the exploitation of various technologies to activate persulfate (PS) or peroxymonosulfate (PMS) to generate $S O _ { 4 } \cdot -$ .

PS has received attention for water treatment due to its high stability, aqueous solubility, high efficiency and relatively low cost compared to PMS [11–13]. And it has been successfully used in environmental applications to remediate recalcitrant organics in aqueous [14]. Generation of $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot -$ from PS itself is slow, but it will be accelerated when PS is activated by heat, ultraviolet light or transition metals (Fe (II), Co(II), Ag(I), $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 \cdot }$ [15–17]. And activating PS by transition metals has been demonstrated to be the most simple and efficient method [18,19]. Corresponding reactions of different activation ways are

summarized in Eqs. (1)–(4):

$$

Transition metal oxide and composite metal oxide in nanoscale, such as CuO, $\mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ , $\mathrm { C u F e O } _ { 4 } ,$ , are often used in activating PS to produce $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ [20-22]. However, these catalysts are often prepared by cumbersome sol-gel and co-precipitation methods and with large lateral size and thickness. Obviously, their catalytic performance still has room for improvement. Thus, efforts are focused on developing facile preparation methods and obtain ultrathin nanomaterials. And nanosheets are the most anticipated materials because active sites tend to occupy a higher proportion in the sheet structure [23,24].

More and more NiO nanomaterials have been applied in electrochemistry [25,26], sensors and catalysis[27]. It has been reported that the surface energy of NiO facets follows the order: $\left\{ 1 \ 1 \ 0 \right\} \ \approx$ $\{ 1 ~ 0 ~ 1 \} > \{ 1 ~ 1 ~ 3 \} > \{ 1 ~ 0 ~ 0 \}$ [28]. Study has proved that surface chemical reactivity generally increases with surface energy. Therefore, researchers developed a series of methods to prepare NiO with high energy facets for enhancing catalytic properties. For instance, Astam K. Patra et al. prepared 110 preferential NiO using the hydrothermal method in the presence of salicylate and found its high catalytic ability for the reduction of carbonyl compounds to their respective alcohol in the presence of 2-propanol [29]. Su et al. prepared Mesoporous NiO crystals with dominantly exposed {1 1 0} reactive facets using hydrothermal methods with ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } S { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ as morphology modifiers for ultrafast lithium storage [28]. However, these methods are complicated and time-consuming and often needs morphology inducers. Besides, as far as we know, there is no investigation on the catalytic properties of NiO on activating persulfate for degrading organic pollutants.

In this work, we developed a facile mechanochemical method to prepare nickel oxide nanosheets (denoted by NiO-1) with dominantly exposed $\lbrace 1 \ 1 \ 0 \rbrace$ -facets. This hand-grinding-based synthesis method is cheap, solvent-free, fast, simple and easy for large-scale production. When applied as catalyst for activating PS to degrade RhB, NiO-1 exhibited excellent and stable catalytic performance. This unique morphology of ultrafine and ultrathin nanosheets combined with dominantly exposed $\lbrace 1 \ 1 \ 0 \rbrace$ -facets are considered as the main cause of its excellent catalytic performance on PS. To comprehensively evaluate NiO-1/PS system, the influence of experimental parameters (such as PS dosages, catalyst dosage, initial solution $\mathsf { p H }$ and anions) and reaction mechanisms of NiO-1/PS system were systematically investigated. Furthermore, surface-bound radical mechanism was proposed for NiO1/PS system.

# 2.2. Preparation of nickle oxide nano catalysts

In a typical experiment, 10 mmol nickelous nitrate $( \mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } )$ and 20 mmol NaOH or 10 mmol $\mathrm { N a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C O } _ { 3 }$ were weighted and ground for about $5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ separately in agate mortar to ensure the evenness of starting materials, then they were mixed and grounded for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ .

Finally, the precursor was obtained by washing the samples thoroughly with distilled water and ethanol. The precursor was dried in the oven for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and then calcined at $3 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ in air atmosphere by raising the temperature at a rate of $5 ~ { ^ \circ } \mathrm { C } / { \mathrm { m i n } }$ , the obtained sample was denoted as NiO-1 and NiO-2 for the NaOH and ${ \mathrm { N a } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { C O } } _ { 3 }$ resulting product, respectively.

For comparison, another NiO catalyst denoted as NiO-3 was synthesized by the hydrothermal method as follows. $1 . 4 5 \ : \mathrm { g } \ : \mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ and $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { g }$ urea were added into $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m l }$ deionized water and then bath ultrasonicated for $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . This as-prepared mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at $1 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ \mathrm { 1 0 \ h }$ and then collected by filtering and washing, and finally dried at $5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ .

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected from DX2700 diffractometer (Haoyuan, Dandong, China). Morphology of the materials were characterized using Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM, JEOL 2100F) and Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM, Hitachi SU8220). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out by using an ESCALAB 250 X-ray photoelectron Spectrometer. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) results were obtained on a Micromeritics ASAP2010 surface area analyzer.

# 2.4. Experiments and analysis

The details for RhB degradation and quenching experiments are presented as Supporting Information. The absorbance of RhB was measured at $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = 5 4 0 \ : \mathrm { n m }$ using a $7 5 2 \mathrm { N }$ UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The concentration of dissolved nickel ions in the solution was measured using an inductive Coupled Plasma Emission Spectrometer (ICP, Agilent 5100, USA). Electron spin resonance paramagnetic spectrometer (ESR, JEOL-FA200) was applied to monitor the anticipative signals of radicals trapped by 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO). At designated time, an aliquot $( 2 ~ \mathrm { m l } )$ of the reaction solution was filtrated by a polypropylene membrane $( 0 . 2 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ pore size) and transferred to a $5 \mathrm { m l }$ vial that contained $6 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ DMPO for ESR analysis. The settings for Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrometer were as follows: center field, $3 2 4 ~ \mathrm { m T }$ ; sweep width, $5 \ \mathrm { m T }$ ; microwave frequency, $9 4 5 0 ~ \mathrm { M H z }$ .

The mechanism through which the $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ nanosheets break up into smaller NiO-1 nanosheets was presented in Scheme1. According to the chemical reaction kinetics, the worse the crystallinity, the easier the thermal decomposition occurs. The defects, micropores or regions with poor crystallization in $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ nanosheets may provide favorable conditions for the thermal decomposition. With the increase of thermal decomposition temperature, these areas are becoming active and adjacent oxyhydrogen bonds rapidly combine, dehydrate and shrink, forming many cracks. However, in the well-crystallized region, the generated water vapor is arduous to disperse, and the reaction is slow. Finally, $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ nanosheets break up into many smaller NiO-1 nanosheets. Besides, the crystal mismatch between $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ and NiO-1 is as high as $5 4 \%$ , based on the calculations on the crystal planes of (1 1 0) in $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ and (1 1 1) and (2 0 0) in NiO-1, which is unfavorable for maintaining single crystallinity throughout the chemical conversion process [31]. Although the NiO-1 nanosheets have a small size, the (1 1 0) facets still occupy a high proportion of total surface area due to the ultrathin thickness $( \sim 2 \ \mathrm { n m } )$ of NiO-1 nanosheets.

The formation of the NiO nanosheets by the mechanochemical method is very interesting. Recent work shows that the precipitation of 2 M $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 }$ and NaOH solution leads to the formation of $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ nanoparticles [32], instead of the $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 2 }$ nanosheet precursors in our study. We consider the formation of nanosheets may be caused by the mash-like environment in our work. The grinding of hydrated $\mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 }$ and NaOH results in the mash-like mixture with high viscosity, which may hinder the diffusion of the ions and facilitate the formation of nanosheets. Similar reasons were also ascribed to the formation of CuS nanosheets by similar grinding methods [33].

# 3.3. Effect of NiO dosage, PS concentration, initial $p H$ and anions on RhB degradation by NiO-1/PS system

Effect of NiO-1 dosage, PS concentration and initial pH were investigated to find appropriate reaction conditions for NiO-1/PS system. The detailed discussion is presented as Supporting Information.

It has been reported that there are various anions in native water, which could be negative factors for advanced oxidation processes [39–41]. # 3.4. Reaction mechanisms in the NiO-1/PS system

# 3.4.1. Surface-bound radical-dominated pathway

The prepared NiO-1 nanosheets have presented an outstanding activity in the NiO-1/PS system. In order to investigate the mechanism, electron spin resonance (ESR) and quenching experiments were applied in this study.

# (a) Electron spin resonance (ESR)

Electron spin resonance (ESR) was used to detect the generated radicals during the reaction. DMPO could react with HO% and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ to generate stable adduct, which could be detected by ESR [46]. From Fig. 5, we can observe different detected results at different time (0, 1 and $1 0 \mathrm { m i n } \dot { }$ ). Before reaction of NiO-1 with PS $\operatorname { D m i n } )$ , no signal can be detected, which indicated that PS could not produce free radicals itself. However, after a reaction time (1 and $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n } \dot { }$ ), signals of the HO% and $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot -$ were still undetectable. The detected signals at 1 and $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ refer to DMPOX signal. It has been reported that DMPOX signal was identified as the adduct of peroxy radical (ROO·) and DMPO [47].

In other report, DMPOX signal was also observed when cobalt (Co) reacts with $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , which produces an intermediate of Co(Ⅰ)-OO% [48]. As we all know, persulfate (PS) is an oxidant. We proposed that the generation of DMPOX in this work was due to the oxidation of DMPO by ${ \mathrm { \cdot } } _ { \mathrm { O O - } S ( \mathrm { V I } ) 0 _ { 3 } }$ (an intermediate from the reaction of PS with $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } )$ ) [47,48]. And the possible generation pathways were presented in Fig. S6.

(b) Quenching experiment

Radical quenching experiment is often used to test radicals in advanced oxidation process. Ethanol (EtOH), tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) and phenol were used as quenching agents. EtOH with an R-hydrogen can rapidly quench both HO% and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ with rate constants of $( 1 . 2 – 2 . 8 ) \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ and $( 1 . 6 { - } 7 . 7 ) \times 1 0 ^ { 7 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively. However, TBA (without R-hydrogen) could rapidly react with HO% with a rate constant of $( 3 . 8 - 7 . 6 ) \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ , which is 418–1900 times greater than that for $\mathbf { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { - } \mathbf { \Phi } ( ( 4 \mathrm { - } 9 . 1 ) \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } ~ \mathbf { M } ^ { - 1 } ~ \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 } )$ $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ [22]. It has been reported that Phenol could react with both HO% and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ with the rate constants of $6 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ and $8 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 }$ , respectively, which has better inhibitory effect than EtOH and TBA [42].

We can see from Fig. 6 that phenol quenched the catalytic reaction more than EtOH or TBA. The degradation efficiency declines from $9 8 . 3 \%$ to $7 7 . 9 \%$ with the addition of $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ phenol. However, the efficiency decline is still insignificant compared with reported literature.

The dielectric constants of Phenol, TBA and EtOH are 9.78, 12.47 and 25.1, respectively [42,51]. Their water solubility increases gradually with the increase of polarity. Due to the hydrophilicity, TBA and EtOH is more easily dispersed in liquid phase instead of the catalyst surface, and react with free radicals in liquid phase [20,35,40]. However, the results show un-conspicuous quenching effect. It is because most of generated radicals are surface-bound radicals. On the other hand, Phenol, with smaller dielectric constant compared with EtOH and TBA, is easier to approach catalyst surface. Therefore, phenol could react with free radicals in close proximity to catalyst surface. Based on the failure of the quenching test, we proposed a surface-bound radical route for NiO-1/PS system. The surface of catalyst instead of liquid phase is the main sites for the generation of reactive radicals. This also explains why ESR couldn’t detect any signals of the HO% and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ . The main reason is because DMPO was preferred to be oxidized to DMPOX by PS in liquid phase.

To gain further insight to the dominant surface-bound radical species at different pH, the quenching test at different pH was carried out in the NiO-1/PS system with alcohols addition (see Supporting Information for details). The results are presented in Table S2 which indicated $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot -$ ads radicals were major surface-bound radicals at $\mathrm { p H } 7$ .

# 3.4.2. XPS analysis of NiO-1 before and after reaction

It has been proved above that radicals are generated mainly on the catalyst surface in NiO-1/PS system. For better understanding the surface composition and chemical states, catalyst before and after reaction was characterized by XPS

It has been reported that the reversible evolution between different valences of transition metal ions is always considered to be very important for the generation of radicals [52]. It can be seen from the XPS spectrum of Ni $\boldsymbol { 2 } \mathrm { p } _ { 3 / 2 }$ (Fig. 7a), the binding energies are 854, 856.2, and 861.1 eV corresponding to $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + }$ and 857.1 and $8 6 3 \mathrm { e V }$ corresponding to $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ [53–55], respectively. The extents of the variation of the $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions are surveyed by calculating the ratio of the sum of $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ peak area to that of the $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + }$ peak area. The ratio of $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions increases from $2 6 . 2 \%$ to $3 4 . 8 \%$ after reaction, where a change of $8 . 6 \%$ between $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ are observed. It manifests the involvement of $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + } { \bf - N i } ^ { 3 + } { \bf - N i } ^ { 2 + }$ redox processes during the reaction, which can be explained according to Eqs. (14)–(16).

$$
\begin{array} { l } { { \equiv \mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { I I } ) \ + \ S _ { 2 } { \ O _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - }  \equiv \mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { I I I } ) \ + \ S { O _ { 4 } } ^ { - } \ + \ S { O _ { 4 } } ^ { 2 - } } } \\ { { \mathrm { } } } \\ { { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } O \ + \ S _ { 2 } { O _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - }  \mathrm { H S O _ { 5 } } ^ { - } \ + \ \mathrm { H S O _ { 4 } } ^ { - } } } \\ { { \equiv \mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { I I I } ) \ + \ \mathrm { H S O _ { 5 } } ^ { - }  \equiv \mathrm { N i } ( \mathrm { I I } ) \ + \ S { O _ { 4 } } ^ { - } \ + \ \mathrm { H } ^ { + } } } \end{array}
$$

Moreover, sulfate radical $\mathrm { ( } S 0 _ { 4 } \mathrm { ' ~ - ~ ) ~ }$ can react with water to generate hydroxyl radical (HO% ) in accordance with the Eq (5) as mentioned above.

Fig. 7b shows the high-resolution O1s spectrum of the NiO-1 before and after catalytic reaction. Three individual peaks at 529.08, 530.6, and $5 3 1 . 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ are assigned to the lattice oxygen (NiO, denoted as $\mathrm { O _ { \mathrm { I } } } )$ , the lattice oxygen $( \mathrm { N i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ , denoted as $0 _ { \mathrm { { I I } } } )$ , and hydroxyl oxygen (NiOOH, denoted as $\mathrm { \bf O _ { \mathrm { I I I } } } )$ ), respectively [42]. Meanwhile, it can be observed that the peak of hydroxyl oxygen peak has a decrease of $1 . 8 \%$ after reaction. According to the literature [35], we proposed that hydroxyl oxygen generation on the catalyst surface would also be responsible for the excellent activity. # 3.4.3. Mechanism of the PS activation on NiO-1

In the RhB degradation process (Scheme 2), the negatively charged $S _ { 2 } { 0 _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - }$ ions are more prone to be adsorbed onto the NiO catalyst surface as the isoelectric point of NiO is generally at $\mathsf { p H }$ above 8.0 [56]. The $\equiv \mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + }$ on NiO surface is first oxidized to $\equiv \mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ by transferring electrons to the electrophilic oxygen of adsorbed $S _ { 2 } { 0 _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - }$ (Eq. (14)), which results in homolytic cleavage of $\mathrm { o - } 0$ bonding and generates surface-bounded sulfate radicals $( { \cal S } { \cal O } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - } \mathrm { \bf ~ \underline { { ~ a d s } } } )$ . Meanwhile, part of PS will react with $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ to form ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ (Eq. (15)), which will transfer electrons to $\equiv \mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ to generate surface-bound sulfate radicals $\mathrm { ( } \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { \cdot \mathrm { ~ - ~ } }$ $_ \mathrm { a d s } )$ (Eqs. (16)), and the reaction of Eq. (16) could make the metal centers return to the initial oxidation state and lead to the recovery of $\equiv \mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + }$ center. Also, $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { N i } ^ { 3 + }$ shown by XPS analysis (see Fig. 7a) in the fresh and reacted NiO-1 confirm the above deduction. Furthermore, reaction between $S O _ { 4 \mathrm { a d s } }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ will occur on the catalyst surface with the generation of $\mathrm { H O } _ { \mathrm { a d s } } ^ { * } ,$ especially under alkaline conditions (Eq. (5)). On the other hand, trace dissolved nickel ions in liquid phase will react with ${ \mathrm { H S O } } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ or $S _ { 2 } { 0 _ { 8 } } ^ { 2 - }$ via a homogeneous catalytic oxidation to generate free sulfate radicals $( S { \cal O } _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot \cdot - } ~ _ { \mathrm { f r e e } } )$ , part of which can react with $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ to produce $\mathrm { H O } _ { \mathrm { f r e e } } ^ { \cdot }$ (Eq. (5)). What’s more, nickel (hydr) oxides (NiOOH) could also activate PS to generate sulfate radicals (Eqs. (17) and (18)). Finally, the adsorbed and non-absorbed RhB molecules as well as its intermediates as electron donors will be degraded by the formed reactive radicals $\mathrm { ( } \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } \mathrm { ) } ^ { \cdot \cdot - }$ ads, $s 0 _ { 4 } \cdot -$ free, $\mathrm { H O } _ { \mathrm { a d s } } ^ { \bullet }$ and $\mathrm { H O } _ { \mathrm { f r e e } } ^ { \cdot } )$ . Besides, the sulfate radicals also can be consumed by dimerization or quenching reaction by residual PS (Eqs. (19) and (20)), which is unfavorable for the degradation process.

# 3.5. Reuse of NiO-1 nanosheets

Reusability of catalysts is an important index for further application in industry. Guo et al. used sulfur-doped hierarchically porous carbon to activate PS to degrade 4-chlorophenol with a degradation efficiency of $1 0 0 \%$ within $2 1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , but in the third runs, the degradation efficiency was only $^ { 1 5 \% }$ [57]. Yang et al. used activated carbon to catalyze PS to degrade Azo dye acid orange 7 with a degradation efficiency of ${ \sim } 8 5 \%$ within $3 0 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and ${ \sim } 6 0 \%$ of AO7 removal was achieved in the fourth runs [35].

Reuse experiments of the as-prepared catalysts have been carried out and the results are shown in Fig. 8. The degradation efficiency of RhB after $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ reaction for every reuse cycle gradually decreases, but is still as high as $7 2 . 1 \%$ for the fourth cycle system. It implies that the NiO-1 slightly deactivates and shows a good recovery performance. The decreased degradation efficiency may be caused by the following reasons: (1) the incomplete degradation of RhB adsorbed on NiO-1 surface inhibits the interaction of NiO-1 and PS; (2) the intermediate products of RhB decomposition remaining on the surface of NiO-1 are disadvantageous for the degradation reaction; (3) The catalysts suffer from a slight weight loss after each use due to its partial dissolvation into ions.

# 4. Conclusion

In summary, ultrathin NiO nanosheets were successfully synthesized via a simple and facile mechanochemical method and used as a heterogeneous catalyst to activate PS for the degradation of RhB for the first time. Its unique morphology of ultrafine and ultrathin nanosheet with high proportion of exposed (1 1 0) facets of NiO was considered as the main cause of its excellent catalytic performance. The degradation efficiency of the NiO nanosheets are better than most reported values and it can degrade $9 8 . 3 \%$ of RhB in $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . ESR tests, quenching experiments and XPS analysis indicate that surface-bound radicals were the primary active species in the NiO/PS system and corresponding catalysis mechanism was proposed based on the results. This work not only gives further insight into the relationship of NiO surface energy and catalytic properties, but also provides a facile preparation route of high-performance catalyst for deep degradation of toxic and refractory industrial wastewater.
