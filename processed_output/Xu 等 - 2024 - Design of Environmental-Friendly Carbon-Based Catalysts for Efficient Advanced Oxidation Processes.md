Abstract: Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) represent one of the most promising strategies to generate highly reactive species to deal with organic dye-contaminated water. However, developing green and cost-effective catalysts is still a long-term goal for the wide practical application of AOPs. Herein, we demonstrated doping cobalt in porous carbon to efficiently catalyze the oxidation of the typically persistent organic pollutant rhodamine B, via multiple reactive species through the activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS). The catalysts were prepared by facile pyrolysis of nanocomposites with a core of cobalt-loaded silica and a shell of phenolic resin $( \mathrm { C o - C } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } )$ ). It showed that the produced $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ could effectively attack the electron-rich functional groups in rhodamine B, promoting its molecular chain breakage and accelerating its oxidative degradation reaction with reactive oxygencontaining radicals. The optimized $\mathrm { C o - } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ catalyst exhibits impressive catalytic performance, with a degradation rate of rhodamine B up to $9 6 . 7 \%$ in $1 4 \mathrm { m i n }$ and a reaction rate constant $( k )$ as high as $0 . 2 2 7 1 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , which suggested promising potential for its practical application.

Keywords: advanced oxidation processes; rhodamine B; peroxymonosulfate; $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$

# 1. Introduction

With the development of industry, water pollution poses serious threats to human health and well-being, and the organic pollution of freshwater resources has become one of the most serious problems to solve. In particular, industrial dye is the main contributor to the pollution of water, rivers, and groundwater. It not only threatens the surrounding ecosystem by preventing light from reaching the surface, hindering plant photosynthesis, and lowering the oxygen content of the water leading to the death of aquatic organisms and plants, but also causes respiratory injury, tissue necrosis, reproductive system harm, and even genetic mutations in humans [1]. It has been reported that the worldwide production of rhodamine dyes alone will reach USD 232.5 million by 2027.

To achieve the goal of cleansing the water, many methods for removing organic pollutants have been examined, such as physical adsorption [2–4], biological methods [5,6], and advanced oxidation processes (AOPs). Among them, the AOPs have been widely proposed for the degradation of recalcitrant organic contaminants by the highly reactive species generated from oxidizing agents, such as $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ [7,8], peroxymonosulfate (PMS) [9–12], and peroxodisulfate (PDS) [13–17]. Over the past decades, various methods have been explored to activate oxidizer precursors, such as ultraviolet [18], ultrasound [19], thermal [20], and inorganic and organic activation [21]. Notably, heterogeneous catalysts, especially transition metal-based materials, have been extensively studied as heterogeneous catalysts in AOPs [22] owing to their lower energy requirements and easy scale-up for practical applications. However, despite the number of exciting advances achieved using heterogeneous catalysts for oxidizing agent activation, the practical application of such AOPs is often limited. It has been reported that the general metal ion leaching [23] and low catalytic activity [24–26] are the main reasons for the limited performance of AOPs. Therefore, the development of green and cost-effective catalysts is essential to realize the wide practical application of AOPs in environmental remediation.

The key to AOPs is the use of highly reactive oxygen species (ROS), including free radicals $( \bullet \mathrm { O H } ,$ $S O _ { 4 } ^ { - \bullet }$ , $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \mathrm { } ^ { - \bullet }$ , etc.) and strongly oxidizing non-free radicals (e.g., $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ $O _ { 3 } ,$ etc.), to degrade and even completely mineralize organic pollutants into $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ { \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } }$ through complex electron exchange processes [27]. It is clear that the oxidizing species involved in AOPs determine the degradation and transformation behavior of organic matter, such as degradation rates, pathways, and by-products. For example, the reactive oxygen-containing radicals of $\bullet \mathrm { O H , }$ with high oxidation potential $( E _ { 0 } = 1 . 8 – 2 . 7 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ , are considered as some of the most powerful oxidants, which can react with numerous organic compounds [28]. However, such radical-based AOPs are less effective in degrading certain persistent organic pollutants (POPs) like the highly toxic halogenated phenols. The oxidation potential of $\bar { } ^ { 1 } \bar { \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } }$ is relatively mild $( E _ { 0 } = 1 . 4 – 2 . 2 \ V )$ , but it tends to attack the electron-rich organic compound [29,30]. Therefore, taking into account the degradation characteristics of ROS in AOP as well as the structural properties of specific molecules to design catalysts is expected to be effective in improving the advanced oxidative degradation of POPs.

In order to develop green and cost-effective catalysts for AOPs to deal with the evergrowing pollution of industrial dye and to purify freshwater resources, in this paper, we design catalysts based on the structural characteristics of pollutant molecules. Typically, for the electron-rich persistent organic pollutant rhodamine B (RhB), we synthesized a catalyst with cobalt doped in porous carbon for its efficient catalytic degradation via the activation of PMS. The catalysts were prepared by facile pyrolysis of nanocomposites with a core of cobalt-loaded silica and a shell of phenolic resin $( \mathrm { C o - C } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } )$ ). Tiny amounts of Co ions that are effectively anchored by the presence of -OH in phenolic resins (RF) inhibit sintering during calcination and leaking during organic removal. Radical quenching experiments indicate the generation of active species like $\bullet \mathrm { O H }$ , $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { - \bullet }$ , and $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ in the AOPs catalyzed on $\mathrm { C o - } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ catalysts. Since $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ can efficiently attack the electron-rich functional groups in rhodamine, the chain of rhodamine molecules is rapidly broken, accelerating its oxidative degradation. The optimized ${ \mathrm { C o - } } C / { \mathrm { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ catalyst exhibits impressive catalytic performance, with a degradation rate of rhodamine B up to $9 6 . 7 \%$ in $1 4 \mathrm { m i n }$ and a reaction rate constant (k) as high as $0 . 2 2 7 1 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . Our study details useful insights into the design of catalysts used in AOPs based on the characteristics of organic molecules for the efficient and sustainable remediation of organic pollutants.

# 2.2. Synthesis of $C o / S i O _ { 2 }$

Colloidal silica nanospheres were prepared through a modified Stöber method [31]. In a typical synthesis for ${ \sim } 2 5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ particles, $2 . 2 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of tetraethyl silicate (TEOS) was added into a mixture containing $3 6 . 7 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of ethanol, $7 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of water, and $3 . 5 \mathrm { m L }$ of aqueous solution of ammonia $( 2 8 \% )$ . After stirring for $2 \mathrm { { h } }$ at room temperature, the silica particles were collected by centrifugation, washed with ethanol and water, and then re-dispersed in $1 2 \mathrm { m L }$ of water. Then, $2 \mathrm { m L }$ of the as-prepared silica spheres was mixed with $2 2 \mathrm { m L }$ of water and $1 \mathrm { m L }$ of $5 0 \ \mathrm { m M } \ C o C l _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 H _ { 2 } O$ and kept overnight under stirring. Then the samples were collected by centrifugation, washed several times with water, and labeled as ${ \mathrm { C o } } / { \mathrm { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ .

# 2.3. Synthesis of $C o { \mathrm { - } } C / S i O _ { 2 }$

A layer of resorcinol-formaldehyde (RF) was coated on the surface of the formed ${ \mathrm { C o } } / { \mathrm { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ according to a modified version of a reported procedure [32]. In a typical process, the as-prepared ${ \mathrm { C o } } / { \mathrm { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ particles were mixed with $4 6 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of water and $4 \mathrm { m L }$ of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) aqueous solution $( 5 \mathrm { w t \% } )$ ). After stirring overnight, the above ${ \mathrm { C o } } / { \mathrm { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ particles were collected by centrifugation, re-dispersed in $5 6 \mathrm { m L }$ of water, and then combined with resorcinol $_ { ( 0 . 1 0 \mathrm { g } ) }$ , formaldehyde aqueous solution $( 3 7 \% , 0 . 1 4 \mathrm { m L } )$ ), and $1 \mathrm { m L }$ of dilute ammonia aqueous solution $( 2 . 8 \% )$ . After heating the solution at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for two hours and then $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for an hour, particles with RF were collected by centrifugation, washed with ethanol and water, and subsequently freeze-dried at $- 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in a freeze dryer overnight. The obtained samples were carbonized in a tube furnace under a flowing nitrogen atmosphere at a high temperature of $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } , }$ with a rate of temperature rise of $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , and labeled as ${ \mathrm { C o - } } C / { \mathrm { S i O } } _ { 2 }$ .

# 2.5. Catalytic Activity

The catalyst degradation performance was conducted at $2 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in a $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ reactor containing $2 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ RhB as an initial solution. In a typical experiment, $8 \mathrm { m g }$ of catalyst was added into $4 0 \mathrm { m L }$ of RhB solution and stirred for $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ to establish the adsorptiondesorption equilibrium. The reaction was initiated by adding $0 . 2 \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ of PMS aqueous solution. Next, $2 \mathrm { m L }$ of the reaction solution was withdrawn at a certain time interval, filtered through a $0 . 2 2 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ filter membrane, and immediately quenched with $0 . 2 \mathrm { m L }$ of methanol solution. For the cycling test, the catalyst was recycled after each run of the experiment with ethanol and deionized water and then recalcined at $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under a $\Nu _ { 2 }$ atmosphere. The concentration of RhB was analyzed by an ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrometer at $5 5 4 \mathrm { n m }$ wavelength. The concentration of other typical organic contaminants, including bisphenol A (BPA), methyl orange (MO), tetracycline (TC), and sulfamethoxazole (SMZ), were also analyzed by the UV-Vis spectrometer at the detection wavelengths of $2 7 8 \mathrm { n m }$ , $4 6 5 \mathrm { n m }$ , $3 5 7 \mathrm { n m } ,$ , and $2 6 5 \mathrm { n m }$ , respectively.
