Although the advanced oxidation process based on persulfate (PS) is an attractive approach due to its high efficiency and environmental friendliness, the difficult degradability and low solubility of naphthalene (NAP) hinder its practical application for in situ chemical remediation of contaminated sites. Herein, this study reported that alkaline activated PS with acetonitrile (ACN) as assistant solvent for in-situ remediation of NAP contaminated soils to improve the removal efficiency. The results showed that the degradation rate of NAP in contaminated soil was up to $3 6 . 7 ~ \%$ on the 1st day in the presence of $\mathrm { C _ { P S } = 3 8 2 \ m m o l \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ , and $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { p H } } = 1 2$ , which was significantly higher than the NAP removal rate without the addition of ACN $( 8 . 6 ~ \% )$ under the same conditions. This indicated that the addition of ACN promoted the solubility of organic pollutants in the liquid phase and improved its degradation efficiency. The NAP removal rate (the 21st day) was up to $9 7 . 5 ~ \%$ . ESR tests and free radical quenching experiments indicated that $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , ⋅OH and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ were the dominant active species. The degradation mechanism of NAP mainly involved hydroxylation, decarboxylation, and ring opening reactions. The toxicity assessment using the Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (T.E.S.T.) and bioluminescent bacteria assays showed that NAP could eventually be degraded into less toxic degradation intermediates. In summary, this study confirmed the suitability of ACN enhanced alkaline activated PS system for the degradation of organic pollutants and provided some new ideas for the in situ large-scale remediation of organic contaminated sites.

# 1. Introduction

Over the past decades, increased industrialization, agriculture and anthropogenic activities, such as the burning of fossil fuels, petrochemical processing, over-exploitation of land and the widespread use of pesticides [1,2], have produced large quantities of soils contaminated with polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which have resulted in serious site contamination [3,4]. PAHs have brought a wide range of environmental hazards due to their high toxicity, low volatility, and poor water solubility [5,6]. Among, naphthalene (NAP) is the simplest and most common PAH formed by the condensation of two adjacent carbon atoms shared by two benzene rings. It can pose a serious threat to human health and surrounding ecosystems due to its carcinogenicity, teratogenicity, mutagenicity and high refractoriness and strong adsorbability to soil medium [7]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop effective approaches to remove NAP in contaminated soils.

Among the existing remediation technologies, advanced oxidation technology based on persulfate (PS) has gradually been developed as an alternative to traditional oxidants for the degradation of PAHscontaminated sites due to its good stability, high safety, wide pH range, slow and mild oxidation, and environmental friendliness [8,9]. In-situ chemical oxidation technologies based on PS essentially attack the contaminants by generating ⋅OH $( E _ { \mathrm { . O H } } ^ { 0 } { = } 2 . 8 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ , $t _ { 1 / 2 } { = } 2 0$ ns) [10,11] and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ [12]. In general, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ can be produced by activating PS using iron materials, UV irradiation, alkali, heat or carbon catalysts [8,13,14]. Compared to other radicals, $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ exhibits a higher oxidation potential $( E _ { \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } } } ^ { 0 } = 2 . 5 \mathrm { - } 3 . 1 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ [15] and a longer half-life period $( t _ { 1 / 2 } = 3 0 – 4 0 \mu s )$ [16]. Thermal activation, metal activation, and carbon activation based on persulfates have been extensively studied [8,17,18]. For instance, Zeng et al. degraded NAP in soil slurry by activating PS with nano zero valent iron (nZVI) combined with citric acid (CA), which showed a removal rate of $9 6 . 8 \%$ after $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ [18]. However, the application of these oxidation processes in soil remediation is constrained by limitations such as high energy consumption, secondary pollution, and limited propagation distance, which affect the efficiency and wide application of oxidation processes in soil remediation [12].

Compared with other systems, the alkaline activated PS system can be considered as an ideal technology for the remediation of organic contaminated sites [8]. Under strong alkaline conditions, the conversion mechanism of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ to ⋅OH can significantly enhance the degradation efficiency of organic pollutants [19,20]. At the same time, the system effectively avoids the common problems of limited transmission distance and high energy consumption [21]. This feature is undoubtedly of great significance in promoting the wide application of advanced oxidation technology in the field of practical engineering. However, PS-based AOPs for in situ remediation of PAHs-contaminated soils are limited by the low oxidative utilization, which is mainly due to its extremely poor water solubility [22,23]. The strong adsorption of highly hydrophobic PAHs in aging soils with long-term contamination causes the PAHs to be tightly attached to the soil particles [24,25]. Since tightly adhered contaminants cannot be effectively contacted and removed by oxidants or cleaning agents, they may be re-released into the soil during the later stages of remediation, which can lead to the occurrence of contamination rebound phenomenon [26]. Therefore, more effective remediation strategies need to be developed for the adsorption of highly hydrophobic NAP in soil.

In order to overcome this drawback, in recent years, surfactants have been utilized to improve the solubility of low water-soluble pollutants in the degradation of contaminated soils [27]. For instance, Fan et al. degraded polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) in contaminated soil by electrokinetic remediation, with the highest degradation rate of PCBs at $3 8 . 0 ~ \%$ when Igepal CA-720 and PS were used [28]. However, some studies have found that the addition of surfactants even inhibits the oxidative degradation of pollutants. This is due to the biodegradability of surfactants, which leads to their reaction with oxidizing agents, consuming large amounts of oxidants and seriously increasing costs of remediation [29]. Currently, scholars have studied degradation of organic pollutants in acetonitrile (ACN)/water mixtures [30,31]. For instance, Dhaouadi et al. used a 30:70 (v/v) $\mathrm { A C N / H _ { 2 } O }$ mixture $\mathrm { ( p H 3 . 0 ) }$ as a compound solvent and it was found that rotenone was completely degraded after $4 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ by the electrofenton system [31]. Inspired by these works, the aim of this study was to explore the use of organic solvents with excellent solubility as assistant solvents, which would facilitate the effective desorption of adsorbed contaminant in soil and its mass transfer process in soil.

NAP is used as the target PAH in this work due to the wide distribution of NAP in actual contaminated sites. The aim of this work is to develop enhanced in situ remediation of NAP contaminated soil using alkaline activated persulfate with co-solvent assistance for improved removal efficiency. This study explored the effects of assistant solvents on the dissolution of pollutants and the desorption performance of pollutants in soils. The catalytic mechanism of active free radicals and the degradation process were explored. The toxicity assessment using the Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (T.E.S.T.) and bioluminescent bacteria assays were employed to analyze the toxicity of the degradation intermediates and provide the guidance for the remediation of organicrich contaminated sites.

# 2.4. Toxicity determination of luminescent bacteria

After complete degradation of the contaminated soil has been achieved, the remediation solution (containing ACN) should be considered as a potential contaminant, given the complexity of the components in the remediation solution and its potential environmental risks. Therefore, the remediation solution must be collected and properly treated before it can be discharged or reused. Soil column flushing experiments were conducted on contaminated soils using the method of Long et al. (using deionized water as the washing solution) [37]. The soil leachate collected during the flushing process will be treated using physical, chemical or biological methods in subsequent work.

It should be noted that the presence of any contaminants (including ACN) was not detected in the flushed soil samples. Meanwhile, the ecotoxicity of the flushed soil was evaluated using the bioluminescent bacterial assay [38]. Soil extracts were prepared according to the method of Płaza et al. (1993) [39]. The soil samples were placed in a Soxhlet extraction unit and extracted for $^ { 1 6 \mathrm { h } }$ using methylene chloride as the extractant. Due to the high toxicity of methylene chloride, the soil extract was concentrated to $5 \mathrm { m L }$ by rotary evaporation after extraction and $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ m L } }$ dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added. Subsequently, the mixture was further concentrated to $1 \mathrm { m l }$ and stored in a refrigerator at 4 $^ \circ \mathrm { C }$ for subsequent toxicity testing of luminescent bacteria.

# 3. Results and discussion

3.1. Establishment of the alkaline activated persulfate system for NAP removal in contaminated soil

# 3.1.1. Phase diagram construction of the ternary system (assistant solvent/ water/NAP)

In view of the aromatic properties of the contaminants which constitute an obstacle to the dissolution process [40], the dissolution characteristics and phase behavior performance of the contaminants in a mixed system of assistant solvents and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ were investigated. The maximum solubility of NAP at different volume ratios of assistant solvents to $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ was obtained by turbidimetric titration, and the ternary phase diagram of the assistant solvent/ $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } / \mathrm { N A P }$ system was constructed [41].

As shown in Fig. 1, the area above the solubility curve is the homogeneous region and the area below the solubility curve is the heterogeneous region. The active component can be fully contacted with organic pollutants in the homogeneous region, which can improve the reaction rate between free radicals and NAP in the reaction system. However, there is a clear interface between the oil and water phases in the heterogeneous zone, which will be detrimental to the dissolution and degradation of NAP. The maximum solubility of NAP in the homogeneous region was $0 . 1 7 4 \ \mathrm { g \ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ , $0 . 1 6 6 \mathrm { ~ g ~ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ and $0 . 2 4 7 ~ \mathrm { g ~ m L ^ { - 1 } }$ when ACN, DMSO, and DMF were used as assistant solvents, respectively. Fig. 1 illustrated that the degree of solubilizing effect of assistant solvents on NAP was in the following order: $\mathrm { D M F } > \mathrm { A C N } > \mathrm { D M S O }$ .

# 3.1.2. The selection of assistant solvents in the alkaline activated persulfate system

The desorption of NAP contaminated soil by different assistant solvents at different volume fractions was investigated separately (runs 1–15, Table S2). Fig. 2(a) showed that the desorption of NAP by all the assistant solvents was enhanced with the increase in their concentration. Among them, DMF showed the most significant desorption effect. When the volume fraction of the assistant solvent was $5 0 ~ \%$ , the desorption rates of NAP $9 1 . 8 ~ \%$ to $9 5 . 6 ~ \%$ ) were eventually saturated. This indicated that assistant solvents with high desorption capacity can enhance the solubility of contaminants in the aqueous phase and effectively overcome the mass transfer limitations of adsorbed contaminants in soil during degradation [41,42]. However, the effect of assistant solvents (ACN, DMSO and DMF) on the oxidative degradation of NAP needs to be further evaluated (runs 16–19, Table S2). As can be seen in Fig. 2(b), under the aqueous catalytic systems without assistant solvents, the degradation rate of NAP was only $8 . 6 \%$ (the 1st day), in the presence of $\mathrm { C _ { P S } } = 3 8 2 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ and $\mathrm { p H } = 1 2$ . In contrast, the NAP degradation rate was increased to different degrees with the addition of different assistant solvents. Among them, the best degradation effect of NAP $( 3 6 . 5 \% )$ was achieved when ACN was used as the assistant solvent. This is due to the fact that acetonitrile presents a relatively high activation energy barrier to nucleophilic attack and deprotonation processes [43,44]. This makes acetonitrile chemically relatively stable and not susceptible to oxidation under general conditions. In contrast, DMF and DMSO compete with pollutants for a limited number of reactive radicals in systems where strong oxidants are present [45,46], leading to inhibition and blockage of the oxidative degradation of NAP. Fig. 2(b) showed that ACN as the assistant solvent can form a homogeneous and stable system between NAP and aqueous phases, which promoted full contact between the oxidant and NAP. Consequently, ACN would be selected as the assistant solvent in the following study.

The degradation of NAP by alkaline activated PS was investigated under different volume fractions of ACN (runs 3–9, Table S3). Figure 2 (c) showed that the removal of NAP basically tends to increase and then decrease as the ACN content increases from $2 0 ~ \%$ (v/v) to $5 0 ~ \%$ (v/v). This will limit the effective contact between the active components and the target pollutants, and ultimately lead to a significant reduction in the reaction rate of free radicals and pollutants. The highest NAP degradation rate of $3 6 . 7 \%$ was observed when the volume fraction of ACN was $4 0 ~ \%$ . And it was obvious that the addition of a certain amount of ACN can facilitate the desorption of NAP from contaminated soil and significantly enhance the solubility of NAP in the aqueous phase, which can effectively improve the NAP degradation rate.

# 3.2. In-situ chemical oxidation of alkaline activated PS for the removal of NAP in contaminated soil: the key parameters

# 3.2.1. Optimization of the key parameters

The influence between the dosing ratio and degradation efficiency of NAP was investigated in the absence and presence of ACN addition to obtain the optimal dosing parameters. The effect of different PS concentrations (runs 10–19, Table S3) and pH values (runs 20–29, Table S3) on NAP degradation is shown in Fig. 3(a-b). In addition, the lower part of Fig. 3(a-b) presented the effect of different dosing ratios on the degradation of NAP without the addition of ACN. As can be seen in Fig. 3 (a), the degradation rate of NAP increased from the initial $1 1 . 7 \%$ to 28.3 $\%$ (the 3rd day) as the PS concentration increased from $7 6 \mathrm { m m o l } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ to $3 8 2 \ \mathrm { \ m m o l \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ under ACN-free addition. Obviously, increasing the amount of oxidizer played an important role in enhancing the degradation efficiency of NAP, which is in agreement with the results of previous studies [21,48]. This was mainly due to the fact that the higher the concentration of PS, the more sulfate radicals $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ were activated under the action of alkali and the increased number of hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH) [49]. Therefore, there were more free radicals that can react with NAP for oxidation, causing an increase in the removal rate of NAP with the increase of PS concentration. In addition, the removal rate of NAP increased to $6 0 . 4 ~ \%$ with the extension of degradation time (the 21st day, $\mathrm { C _ { P S } } = 3 8 2 \ \mathrm { m m o l } \ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , $\mathrm { p H } = 1 2 ,$ . However, under the condition of ACN addition, a significant enhancement of NAP removal up to $9 7 . 5 ~ \%$ was realized (the 21st day, $\mathrm { C _ { P S } } = 3 8 2 \ \mathrm { m m o l } \ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , $\mathrm { p H } = 1 2 $ ).

For the same reason, as shown in Fig. 3(b), the apparent removal rate of NAP gradually increased from an initial $7 2 . 9 ~ \%$ to $9 6 . 7 ~ \%$ (the 21st day) as the pH of the oxidant system increased from $\mathrm { p H } = 8$ to $\mathrm { p H } = 1 2$ under the condition of ACN addition. Therefore, the appropriate $\mathsf { p H }$ of the reaction system and extended treatment time were favorable for NAP degradation efficiency. This further illustrated that strong alkaline conditions activate PS to produce more reactive radicals with oxidizing properties [32].

# 3.2.2. Oxidant consumption

The evolution of PS concentration (runs 25–29, Table S3) and pH (runs 15–19, Table S3) with reaction time were monitored and analyzed during the degradation process. As shown in Fig. 4(a), it was observed that only $3 9 . 6 \%$ ((the 21st day)) of PS was consumed in the soil under the condition of $\mathtt { p H } 8$ . The decomposition of PS increased significantly to $4 3 . 8 \%$ , $6 1 . 2 \%$ , $7 9 . 7 \%$ and $9 3 . 0 \%$ as the pH was raised to 9, 10, 11 and 12, respectively. This suggested that the decomposition rate of PS showed an enhanced trend with the rise of pH value in the reaction system, which was consistent with the reports in the existing literature [32].

The pH changes at different PS concentrations were monitored at different time points during the reaction (Fig. 4(b)). It was observed that the pH value remained around 11 after 21 days at a PS concentration of $7 6 \ \mathrm { m m o l / L }$ . When the concentration of PS increased to $3 8 2 ~ \mathrm { m m o l ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ , the pH significantly decreased to the neutral range. This indicated that the pH showed a gradual decrease with increasing PS concentration and eventually dropping to the neutral range, which was consistent with the reports in the existing literature [48,50]. # 3.3. The mechanism analysis of NAP degradation by alkaline activated PS

# 3.3.1. ESR analysis for free radical generation

The types of active species produced during the degradation of NAP were investigated through ESR analysis using DMPO and TEMP as the spin trapping agent [53]. As shown in Fig. 5(a), typical peaks of $\mathrm { D M P O - S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } }$ and DMPO-⋅OH were observed in the ESR spectra in the alkaline activated PS system $\left( \mathbf { p } \mathbf { H } = 1 2 \right)$ ) which was consistent with reports from previous studies [54,55]. Compared with that in the absence of ACN, the signals of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and ⋅OH in the system were significantly enhanced by the addition of ACN, which indicated that the homogeneous system facilitated the generation and stable existence of radicals. And a triple peak of TEMP- $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ was observed in Fig. 5(b). ESR tests indicated that $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , ⋅OH and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ were formed during the degradation of NAP by the alkaline activated PS system.

# 3.3.2. Free radical scavenging quenching experiment

In order to further determine the contribution of active species to NAP degradation, MeOH, TBA, and L-histidine were used as quenchers to identify reactive oxygen species as illustrated in Fig. 6. Tert-butanol (TBA) and methanol (MeOH) were used to identify the generation of ⋅OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ . MeOH is a great quencher for $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and ⋅OH radicals because it reacts efficiently with both free radicals simultaneously $( \mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } / M e O H } } { = } 1$ $\times \ 1 0 ^ { 7 } \ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { S } ^ { - 1 }$ , $\mathrm { K _ { O H / M e O H } { = } 1 } \ \times 1 0 ^ { 9 } \ \mathrm { M ^ { - 1 } } \ \mathrm { S ^ { - 1 } } )$ [56]. TBA is an excellent scavenger for ⋅OH, due to the fact that TBA has a high reaction rate with ⋅OH $( \mathrm { K _ { O H / T B A } { = } 5 . 2 ~ \times ~ 1 0 ^ { 8 } ~ { \bf ~ M } ^ { - 1 } ~ { \bf ~ S } ^ { - 1 } } )$ and reacts rather slowly with $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } ( K _ { S O _ { 4 } ^ { - } / T B A } } { = } 8 . 4 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { M ^ { - 1 } } \mathrm { S ^ { - 1 } } )$ [57]. And L-histidine can be used as $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ quencher [58].

# 3.3.3. Degradation pathways

In order to detect the mineralization results after remediation of NAP contaminated soil, TOC analysis was performed on samples before degradation (run 2) as well as at different reaction times (run 19). As shown in Fig. S1, the TOC removal rate of NAP gradually increased with the reaction time and achieved approximately $3 8 \%$ TOC removal within 21 days. This indicated that NAP was degraded into some intermediates and other small molecules. These intermediates may be more toxic than

NAP. In order to investigate the degradation mechanism of NAP in an alkaline activated PS system, the degradation intermediates of NAP were detected by GC-MS.

The GC-MS spectra of the intermediates were supplied in Fig. S2. Based on the degradation intermediates, the degradation mechanism of NAP in alkaline activated persulfate system was determined, which mainly involved hydroxylation, decarboxylation, and ring opening reactions (Fig. 7). Firstly, 1,2-naphthoquinone and 1,4-naphthoquinone were detected in the degradation intermediates of NAP. Generally speaking, NAP was attacked by ⋅OH to form 1,2-naphthol and 1,4-naphthol, which then underwent tautomerization to produce 1,2-naphthoquinone and 1,4-naphthoquinone [53,59]. Although naphthol was not detected in our study, the formation of other unidentified products such as 1,2-naphthol and 1,4-naphthol in alkaline activated persulfate systems cannot be excluded. Subsequently, 2-oxo-4-phenylbut-3-enoic acid was generated from 1,2-naphthoquinone through hydroxylation and ring-opening. In addition, 1,4-naphthoquinone underwent ring opening and CO loss reaction to form phthalic anhydride, which was then oxidized to phthalic acid [53,60,61]. Further oxidation of intermediates led to ring opening and decarboxylation reactions and the formation of short-chain aliphatic compounds (e.g., maleic acid, oxalic acid and 2-hydroxyacetic acid). The fatty acid products were eventually mineralized to $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ and $_ \mathrm { H _ { 2 } O }$ .

# 4. Conclusion

This work investigated the effect of ACN on the degradation of NAP in the in-situ remediation of contaminated soils with alkali-activated persulfate. When ACN was used as the assistant solvent, the degradation efficiency of NAP reached $3 6 . 7 \%$ , which was much higher than the degradation efficiency of NAP without ACN addition $( 8 . 6 \% )$ under the same conditions. This suggested that the assistant solvent (ACN) promoted the solubility of NAP in the liquid phase and enhanced degradation efficiency of NAP. Increasing the amount of oxidant, raising the $\mathtt { p H }$ value of the system and the prolonging of reaction time were all favorable for the degradation of NAP. The removal rate of NAP was as high as $9 7 . 5 ~ \%$ , within 21 days. ESR tests and free radical quenching experiments indicated that the degradation of NAP in alkaline activated PS system occurs via two main pathways: the radical pathway dominated by ⋅OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and the non-radical pathway triggered by $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ . In addition, the degradation mechanism of NAP by alkaline activated PS system mainly involved hydroxylation, ring opening and decarboxylation reactions. And The toxicity assessment using the Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (T.E.S.T.) and bioluminescent bacteria assays showed that NAP could eventually be degraded into less toxic degradation intermediates. The luminescence inhibition rate significantly decreased to $2 . 7 \%$ , which indicated that the addition of ACN effectively promoted the desorption of NAP from the soil, and most of the residual contaminants were successfully removed by degradation and flushing treatment. This study confirmed the suitability of ACN enhanced alkaline activated PS system for the degradation of NAP and provided some new ideas for the remediation of soil contaminated with other organic contaminants.
