# . Introduction

Endocrine Disrupting Compounds (EDCs) are emerging environmental pollutants that include various types of natural and synthetic compounds exhibiting adverse effects on reproduction through mimicking or inhibiting the endocrine systems of animals and humans (Marty et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020). The presence of EDCs and the resulting adverse effects in aqueous media have been widely demonstrated (Annamalai and Namasivayam, 2015; Giulivo et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2018) and various technologies to decrease their concentration including adsorption/biosorption (Ahmed et al., 2017), biochemical oxidation (Wang et al., 2022), ozonation (Esplugas et al., 2007), and treatment with hypochlorite (Romanucci et al., 2020; Zarrelli et al., 2012) have been investigated. For example, the oxidative degradation of some EDCs is associated with the aromaticity of their structures, implying that hydrophobicity may be an important feature in their study and control (Chang et al., 2009). Techniques such as treatment with activated carbon (AC) (Ghazal et al., 2022) or membranes (Kimura et al.,

2004; Wijekoon et al., 2014) can effectively remove EDCs from water. However, all of these methods have some disadvantages such as long reaction times, high cost, low efficiency, incomplete removal, and production of additional toxic by-products. Recently, advanced oxidation processes (A0Ps) (Jia et al., 2021a; Liu et al., 2022; Phan et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2021) have been explored, which show great promise for the elimination of estrogenic compounds from aqueous solution, and they have been widely applied due to the non-selectivity and high redox potential of •OH.

In AOPs systems, the formation of strong and non-selective radicals such as $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ or $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ is most prevalent. Through dehydrogenation and electron-transfer reactions between free radicals and organic pollutants, the latter are oxidized and decomposed into small molecules, ultimately being mineralized as carbon dioxide, water, and inorganic ions. $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ has similar or even higher redox potential (2.53.1 V) and longer halflife $( 3 0 { - } 4 0 ~ \mu s )$ than $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ (1.82.7 V and 20 ns respectively) (Lin et al., 2020). Moreover, its oxidation reactions can proceed efficiently over a wider pH range (from 2 to 8). Sulfate-based advanced oxidation processes (SR-AOPs) have shown excellent performances in the removal of refractory organic pollutants from aqueous environments (Giannakis et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2019). Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and peroxydisulfate (PS) are important sources of sulfate radicals. Due to their cost-effectiveness, good solubility, and environmentally benign, they have been widely studied and applied in environmental remediation.

Traditional activation catalysts for SR-AOPs are mainly based on transition metal ions (Jia et al., 2021b; Wu et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2014) (Fe, Co, Mn, etc.), or heterogeneous photoactivation (Gowrisankaran et al., 2022; Kakavandi et al., 2022; Khan et al., 2017) or ultrasound (Gujar et al., 2023). Compared to other activation methods, transition metal activation has advantages of strong activity and low energy consumption. However, single use of some of the activation techniques mentioned above can leave SR-AOPs with limitations, such as the large number of free radicals generated in a short period of time not being able to react sufficiently with the pollutant, but being quenched directly, resulting in high chemical compounds consumption, high treatment cost and low degradation efficiency of the AOPs system. The application of heterogeneous catalytic materials can enhance the generation of free radicals and promote degradation efficiency. For example, Takdastan et al. (2018) prepared magnetite nanoparticles supported on carbon as a heterostructure catalyst and combined it with UV light and ultrasonication to efficiently activate PMS for the degradation of bisphenol A (BPA), achieving a removal efficiency of more than $9 0 \%$ within $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ Compared to energy-based activation methods, activation of PMS/PS using heterogeneous catalysts is simpler and more economical in terms of reactor/system configuration. Iron-based materials are generally regarded as excellent heterogeneous catalysts because of their non-toxicity, environmental benignity, low cost, and high catalytic activity, and so have become the most studied materials in SR-AOPs (Li et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2018; Yan et al., 2019a).

Nano zero-valent iron (nZVI), which has been widely assessed as a replacement for iron cations or iron oxides with a view to long-term use and potential reusability, has poor stability due to its high surface energy (Sun et al., 2012). To circumvent this disadvantage, stable, porous supports are usually used. The presence of carrier material also reduces the rate of oxidation of nZVI by dissolved oxygen prior to application. Carbonaceous materials have been widely used due to their high specific surface areas, adjustable pore sizes and low cost. Compared to AC, GC has abundant $\pi$ -electrons and its high affinity for organics favours the adsorption of organic matter on its surface (Chen et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2021). Zhao et al. in a recent review reported that generation of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ and OH can occur from the $\pi$ -electrons in hybridized carbon of biochar-based catalysts to PMS (Zhao et al., 2021).

This increases the contact between them and thereby improves the degradation efficiency of AOPs. Moreover, nZVI can be uniformly dispersed on the carbon-based materials, and providing more active sites. Carbon material can also be used as a substrate for the reduction of iron oxides to nZVI. For example, Jiang et al. (2019) prepared an Fe functionalized biochar composites that consisted of ZVI, porous carbon bearing abundant functional groups, and nanofibers, which could activate PMS to efficiently remove BPA. B. Wang and co-workers (Wang et al., 2021) investigated the p-nitrophenol degradation using PS activation by n-ZVI-loaded biochar. The authors found a complete degradation after using $0 . 8 \mathrm { ~ g ~ L ~ } ^ { - 1 }$ of catalysts and $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ of PS. Tan and al. investigated the removal of levofloxacin using nZVI/carbon fiber by achieving a good removal efficiency $( \sim 9 3 \% )$ after 1h of reaction in the presence of $1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ of PMS in the dark. Furthermore, they indicated that this reaction was also due to the $5 0 \%$ adsorption obtained during the first minutes of the reaction (Tan et al., 2021).

EDCs are represented by $^ { 1 7 } \alpha$ -estradiol (EE2), $1 7 \beta$ -estradiol (E2), and BPA. BPA is a compound showing estrogenic activity at very low concentrations, and is of prime concern with regard to water quality (Chang et al., 2009). These pollutants are stable in the environment, so it is important to evaluate their degradation in aquatic systems. In this context, the main purpose of this study is to assess the performance of nZVI supported on GC (GC-nZVI) for heterogeneous SR-AOPs at circumneutral $\mathsf { p H }$ values. First, the BPA degradation kinetics and steady-state concentration of different oxidant species, such as $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , OH, and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I V } } \mathrm { O } ^ { 2 + }$ were determined to assess their contributions to BPA degradation under thermal (dark) and UVA activation. Thereafter, the use of GC-nZVI in the presence of PS and PMS was evaluated for the degradation of E2, EE2, and BPA in tap and sewage treatment plan (STP) water samples. In our work, we use the thermal reduction of zero-valent iron with carbon and metal-catalyzed graphitization to improve the graphitization of the carbon carrier, and all the degradation data were measured after desorption. Another originality is to have differentiated the adsorption from the degradation of selected pollutants (which is not always done in other works) and to have quantified the reactive species involved in the degradation in the dark and under UVA radiation. In addition, we have shown a significant formation of the ferryl ion and determined its contribution to the selective degradation of bisphenol A in solution.

# 2.2. Synthesis and characterization

Ferric nitrate nonahydrate $( \mathrm { F e } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 3 } { \cdot } 9 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } ;$ . $6 . 0 6 \ g , 9 . 0 9$ or $1 2 . 1 2 g$ was first dissolved in Milli-Q water $\mathrm { 2 5 \ m L }$ and then $3 . 0 ~ g$ commercial activated carbon (from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; $3 . 0 \ \& \ ,$ giving Fe:C ratios [Fe]/ $[ \mathbf { C } ] _ { n }$ of 0.06:1, 0.09:1, and 0.12:1, respectively) was added. Each mixture was stirred at room temperature for $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ and then heated and stirred at $6 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to evaporate the water, leaving a black solid. Each solid was placed in a corundum reactor in a tubular furnace and heated for $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at $2 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under a nitrogen flow of $3 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ $\operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , then heated to $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at a rate of $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and maintained at this temperature for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . To determine the total iron in the obtained samples, portions $\left( 5 \ \mathrm { m g } \right)$ were dissolved in concentrated HCl $\mathrm { ( 3 0 ~ m L ) }$ . Each experiment was performed in duplicate. Chemical analysis was performed with an Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analyzer (5800 Agilent) in radial and axial modes, using a wavelength of $2 3 8 . 2 \ \mathrm { n m }$ for the quantitative determination.

The actual Fe:C ratios $( [ \mathrm { F e } ] / [ \mathrm { C } ] _ { n } = 0 . 0 6$ , 0.09 and 0.12) in the samples was measured as 0.054, 0.073, and 0.096, respectively. So, the samples are denoted as 0.05-GC-nZVI, 0.07-GC-nZVI, and 0.10-GC-nZVI, respectively.

Functional groups on the surfaces of the samples were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, PerkinElmer 1725X, USA). The valence states of the constituent elements were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) and Xray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo-Fisher ESCALAB $\mathrm { X i + }$ USA), and binding energies were calibrated with reference to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Specific surface areas were analyzed by recording BrunauerEmmettTeller (BET) nitrogenadsorption-desorption isotherms. The morphological and textural properties of the samples were characterized by fieldemission scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Tescan Mira3, Czech Republic, equipped with a Zeiss Sigma 300 analyzer) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; H-7650, AMT40, Germany) with an attachment for energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis for element mapping. The surface chemical composition of the samples was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (NICOLET 5700, Thermo Electron, USA). The order of their graphitic structures was examined by Raman spectroscopy $( 5 1 4 \mathrm { n m } )$ (LabRAM HR Evolution, HORIBA Scientific, France) and the surface charged condition by zeta potential analysis (ZEN 3700 Zetasizer, Malvern, UK). Before the zeta potential measurement, samples were prepared in MilliQ water at a concentration of $0 . 5 { \ g \mathrm { L } } ^ { - 1 }$ . The suspension was adjusted to a pH range of 111 using NaOH or HCl solution and then sonicated for 30 min.

The formation of $\mathrm { F e _ { 3 } C }$ was mainly due to the reduction of pyrocarbon at high temperatures. However, the peak of GC could not be observed due to the strong signal peak of zero-valent iron. After removing the iron from the GC-nZVI using hydrochloric acid, there was a obvious signal peak at $2 \theta = 2 6 ^ { \circ }$ , which can be thought of as graphite crystal diffraction peak (JCPDS No. 261097). As given in the Raman spectra (Fig. S3), the G bands and D bands were found in the sample, as a feature of graphitized materials. And the ratio of the intensity of the Dband to the G-band $\mathrm { ( I _ { D } / I _ { G } ) }$ , is also shown in the graph, and it can be seen that the GC-0.10-GC-nZVI has the smallest ratio of the three catalysts, indicating that it has the least amount of defective carbon and the highest degree of graphitization.

The derived textural properties were calculated by non-local density functional theory function of SAIEUS software and compiled in Table S1 (the specific nitrogen adsorption and desorption isotherms of the samples and pore size distributions are shown in Fig. S4). With the increase of Fe proportion, $S _ { \mathrm { B E T } }$ and the micropore and mesopore volume decreased. This could be attributed to the adsorption of ZVI on the carrier GC resulting in the blockage of the pores. It follows that the ZVI particles were not accumulated on the surface of the carbon but dispersed in its pores (Lv et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2009). SEM image (Fig. S5) also clearly showed that at low iron loading, the iron was preferentially dispersed in the pores of the carbon rather than on the surface, spreading to the surface of the carbon when the iron was overloaded. In contrast, when there was no carbon carrier (Fig. S5d), the ZVI tends to gather together and has a larger particle size. The corresponding EDS elemental mappings (Figs. S6ad) clearly illustrate the homogeneous distributions of C, O, and Fe in the samples. The presence of O may be due to the formation of iron oxide on the surface of the sample in contact with $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ in the air. Moreover, the TEM image (Figs. S7a and b) revealed that ZVI in the material was evenly dispersed on the carbon, which had a typical graphitized structure, facilitating the transfer of photogenerated electrons (Wang et al., 2019b; Zhuang et al., 2017)

From the infrared spectrum of GC-nZVI in Fig. S8, it can be seen that the sharp absorption peak near $3 4 3 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ was caused by the stretching vibration of -OH in the supported iron compound. At the same time, there was a strong absorption peak near $1 6 3 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , which was formed by the $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = C }$ stretching vibration of alkenes, and the peak at $1 3 8 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ was formed by the CH deformation vibration of alkanes and alkenes. The zeta potential of the particles reflected the variation of surface charge with pH in aqueous solutions of different acidity (Fig. S9). The zero point of charge (pHzpc) of 0.05-GC-nZVI, 0.07-GC-nZVI and 0.10-GC-nZVI was observed at 3.25, 6.21 and 7.62, which were consistent with the results of previous studies(Wang et al., 2019a) (Ren et al., 2021). The loaded iron significantly reduces the specific surface area and hydrophobicity of the carbon. To investigate the impact of the loaded iron on reactivity, batch experiments were conducted.

# 3.2. Degradation of BPA using GC-nZVI with PS or PMS

The activation of PS and PMS by 0.07-GC-nZVI was investigated through assessing the efficiency of BPA degradation under different conditions. In the first step, BPA adsorption on 0.07-GC-nZVI, AC and GC were tested at pH 7 (Fig. S10a). From the kinetic results it was clear that the adsorption efficiency of GC was higher compared to AC. This was due to that the mesopore volume of GC was larger than that of AC, which was more conducive to the dispersion of BPA in the pore, as well as the high graphitization of GC which facilitates the $\pi { - } \pi$ interaction with the organics. The decrease in adsorption efficiency of 0.07-GC-nZVI was due to iron blocking the pore structure of GC, resulting in a decrease in the specific surface area of GC. But the data after desorption of the three indicated that the material itself had no degradation effect on BPA. But it can be observed that the adsorption was very fast, almost reaching equilibrium within $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Therefore, the dark reaction adsorption time in subsequent experiments was set at $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ .

In order to correlate the effect of iron doping amount to the degradation of BPA in the presence of PMS, a series of experiments was carried out as shown in Fig. S11. BPA was completely degraded within $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in the presence of GC-nZVI $( [ \mathrm { F e } ] / [ \mathrm { C } ] _ { n } ~ = ~ 0 . 0 7 )$ and PMS, while BPA removal efficiencies of $4 7 \%$ and $8 5 \%$ within $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ were achieved with [Fe], $/ [ \mathrm { C } ] _ { n } 0 . 0 5$ and 0.10, respectively. The results implied that an Fe to C ratio of 0.07 was optimal for BPA degradation.

To further optimize the BPA degradation efficiency of 0.07-GC-nZVI, the effects of pH and oxidant concentration were investigated. As shown in Fig. S12, 0.07-GC-nZVI showed good degradation efficiency as the pH was increased from 3 to 7. Under $\mathrm { p H } 3  – 7$ , most of the BPA was in uncharged forms (Yan et al., 2019b). No effect of $\mathsf { p H }$ on BPA removal towards 0.07-GC-nZVI composites may be attributed to no change in BPA species and surface charge of GC-nZVI (Fig. S9). At a PMS concentration of $0 . 1 \mathrm { \ m M }$ , the degradation efficiency reached $1 0 0 \%$ within $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . When the PMS concentration was increased from 0.25 to $1 . 0 \ \mathrm { m u }$ , the time for complete removal of BPA was shortened to less than $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . Considering the cost and the fact that the pH of actual environmental water is near-neutral, the conditions used in the subsequent experiments were 0.07-GC-nZVI at $\mathrm { p H } _ { 0 } = 7$ and an oxidant concentration of $0 . 2 5 \mathrm { m m }$ .

In the PMS or PS systems under dark conditions (Fig. S13), BPA was slowly degraded by the added ZVI after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of treatment $2 0 \%$ with

PMS, $1 5 \%$ with PS). In contrast, in the 0.07-GC-nZVI/PS or 0.07-GCnZVI/PMS systems, significant increases in removal efficiency were observed, with complete removal of BPA within $4 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . And under UVA irradiation, without the addition of catalysts and oxidants, there is no degradation effect on BPA. Blank experiments showed that at most no BPA degradation was observed when PS and PMS used with or without UVA irradiation, confirming the catalytic effect of 0.07-GC-nZVI. To confirm the role of GC in the degradation effect of the catalyst, the removal and degradation effects of GC in different systems were compared (Fig. S10b) and the results showed that the addition of oxidants or UVA irradiation had little or no contribution to the removal of BPA. The data after desorption showed that the addition of PS or PMS to the system only improved the degradation by about $5 \%$ , and then only by $8 \%$ after the addition of UVA. This suggests that the use of GC alone as a catalyst does not improve the degradation of BPA. The BPA degradation constant $( \mathbf { k } , \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ (Fig. 1c) increases considerably in the presence of GC-nZVI with the two oxidants (Fig. 1). Furthermore, in contrast to the system with PS, the use of UVA radiation using PMS accelerates the degradation of BPA from $0 . 1 8 5 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ to $0 . 2 6 1 \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ . This synergistic effect indicates the contribution of a photo-induced process that increases the production of oxidizing species.

The catalytic ability of 0.07-GC-nZVI in different systems was also investigated. The introduction of UVA in the 0.07-GC-nZVI/PS system (Fig. 1a) accelerated the BPA degradation such that $1 0 0 \%$ degradation of BPA was achieved within $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ instead of $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ . A marked effect was also observed in the ZVI/PS system, with BPA degradation increasing from $^ { 1 5 \% }$ to $5 6 \%$ after $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ The introduction of UVA to the 0.07-GC-nZVI/PMS system (Fig. 1b) significantly reduced the reaction time for complete degradation of BPA from $4 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ to $1 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Thus, it is clear that the introduction of UVA promotes the generation of radicals, providing insight into the mechanism in these two systems.

The contributions of radical species in the degradation of BPA were determined through quenching experiments. Typically, PMS/PS generate $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ during the activation process. In system $0 . 0 7 \mathrm { { \cdot G C - n Z V I + P S } }$ . the addition of $5 \ \mathrm { m M }$ TBA had almost no effect on BPA degradation, whereas in the presence of $4 0 \ \mathrm { m M }$ Isop, up to $6 2 \%$ inhibition was observed after $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ of reaction (Fig. S14a). A similar trend was observed under UVA irradiation, whereby inhibition by Isop was about $4 5 \%$ (Fig. S14b). When $0 . 2 5 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ PMS was used in the dark and under UVA irradiation, negligible effect on BPA removal was observed in the presence of TBA $( 5 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ , whereas the BPA degradation efficiency was inhibited by about $6 0 \%$ within $6 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ under both dark and light conditions in the presence of Isop.

# 3.4.High-valent iron species

The addition of Isop did not completely inhibit the degradation of BPA, suggesting that other reactive species besides $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ were also generated. The formation of high-valent iron in heterogeneous Fenton and Fenton-like processes has been reported in different works (Farinelli et al., 2020; Litter and Slodowicz, 2017).PMsO has been reported as a chemical probe for ferryl ions $( \mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I V } } 0 ^ { 2 + } )$ giving $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ as a product. Through an oxygen atom transfer mechanism, sulfoxide is readily oxidized to the corresponding sulfone by high-valence metal oxygen species which differs from the $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ mediated pathways. In order to assess whether the high-valent metal oxide species $[ \mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I V } } ]$ were produced in the different systems, the production of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ by selective oxidation of PMSO was determined. As shown in Fig. 3a, in the GC-nZVI/PS system, $5 7 \mu \mathrm { M }$ PMSO was oxidized after $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and 26 $\mu \mathrm { M } \mathrm { P M S O } _ { 2 }$ was produced. And after the addition of UVA irradiation, 72 $\mu \mathrm { M }$ PMSO was oxidized and $2 7 \mu \mathrm { M }$ $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ was produced.

Blank experiments showed neither degradation of PMsO nor production of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ in the presence of PS alone, with or without UVA irradiation, indicating that PS alone could not react with PMSO. In the PMS system (Fig. 3b), some oxidation of PMSO was observed with the production of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } }$ . However, by calculating the percentage of $\mathrm { [ P M S O _ { 2 } ] }$ to $[ \mathrm { P M S O } ] _ { 0 } ,$ it transpired that ${ } ^ { 3 \% }$ and $4 \%$ of $\mathrm { P M S O } _ { 2 }$ were produced in the dark and light reactions, respectively, with corresponding increases of $7 \%$ and $9 \%$ upon the addition of catalyst, suggesting a direct contribution of $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I V } }$ in the PMS system. Thus, BPA degradation is not only attributable to radicals, but also to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I V } }$ , and we can argue that exposure to UVA promotes the degradation of PMSO and the generation of $\mathrm { P M S O _ { 2 } } ,$ ,suggesting that the positive effect of UVA on BPA degradation is due to both the promotion of radical production and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { \mathrm { I V } }$ .

# 3.7. Recyclability and Suitability of GC-nZVI in PMS/PS activation

Catalyst stability is an important property for practical applications. For its assessment, we used twofold higher concentrations of each species in solution. Rrecycling experiments in this study were performed by directly adding fresh BPA and PS/PMS to the reaction solution for multiple runs. As shown in Fig. 6, a slight decrease in degradation efficiency was observed in the second and third runs. This trend can be explained by considering different factors arisring during the experimental procedure. (i) We did not wash the sample and add again PS/ PMS during the cycle; only BPA was added to re-establish an initial concentration of $5 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ , Moreover, BPA by-products in the solution may play a competitive role in the oxidation of BPA. (ii) The GC-nZVI concentration decreased with each cycle. This problem was partially circumvented by increasing the initial GC-nZVI concentration. However, a loss of catalyst was incurred when samples were withdrawn for analysis. (iii) During each cycle, a fixed volume of BPA solution was added, resulting in an additional dilution of PS/PMS and GC-nZVI. Despite this, a removal efficiency of $7 0 \%$ was maintained after three runs at $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , indicating good stability and reusability of 0.07-GC-nZVI.

As shown in Fig. S2c, comparing the XRD patterns before and after reactions in the PS and PMS systems, it can be seen that the peak due to ZVI $( 2 \theta = 4 4 . 7 ^ { \circ } )$ was significantly weakened, which indicates that Fe showed a certain degree of dissolution during the reaction process. Nevertheless, SEM and TEM images showed that the structure of the sample was not destroyed, confirming the stability of the material (Figs. S6e, f and $s 7 \mathrm { c } , \mathrm { d } )$ . The FITR spectrum after the reaction can see that the fluctuation at $1 1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ was weakened, and the wavenumber at $5 8 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ was changed, which may be because the dissolution of iron affects the bonding effects between ZVI and carbon (Yan et al., 2015).

# 4. Conclusion

Nano zero-valent iron (nZVI) has been deposited on graphitized carbon (GC) to prepare catalysts suitable for environmental remediation. Of the prepared catalysts, 0.07-GC-nZVI exhibited high BPA removal efficiency in the presence of PMS or PS. BPA $( 2 5 ~ \mu \mathrm { { M } ) }$ could be fully removed by $\mathbf { \bar { 0 . 0 5 \ g L ^ { - 1 } 0 . 0 7 - G C - n Z V I } }$ in the presence of only 0.25 m of PMS or PS within $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ .Compared with pure ZVI, it can be seen that GC significantly enhances the adsorption of compounds at the active sites. Exposure to UVA irradiation slightly improves the degradation efficiency of BPA. Reactive species responsible for BPA degradation at near-neutral pH have been systematically identified.
