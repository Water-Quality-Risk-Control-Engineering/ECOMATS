Carbon materials have been demonstrated to be green catalysts in advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) for removing a wide range of organic pollutants from water. This study presents an innovative approach to transforming waste tyres into defective multi-element coordinated copper single-atom catalysts (CuSA-Tyre) for AOPs. CuSA-Tyre demonstrates superior efficiency in activating various oxidants, in particular peroxydisulfate (PDS), to oxidize organic pollutants through a nonradical electron transfer pathway. The high catalytic performance of CuSA-Tyre under diverse environmental substrates indicates its promising application for real wastewater treatment. A continuous fixed-bed test demonstrates high efficiency and stability of the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system for long-term purification, and the catalysts can be easily recycled after the reaction. This research offers a costefficient solution to upcycling waste tyres into value-added functional catalysts for catalytic water purification.

# 1. Introduction

Freshwater resources are precious, as only $1 \%$ of Earth’s freshwater is suitable for direct human use (Abou-Shady et al., 2023). Rapid industrialization has significantly contaminated freshwater resources with non-biodegradable and toxic organic contaminants, such as insecticides, pharmaceuticals, and artificial additives. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have proven to be effective in removing a wide range of organic pollutants from water (Glaze et al., 1987; W. Ren et al., 2022). Recently, persulfates have gained great attention in AOPs (PS-AOPs) due to their cost-effective storage, ease of transportation, and high efficiency in generating reactive oxygen species such as sulfate $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ , hydroxyl (•OH), and peroxide ion radicals $( 0 _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ , singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ , and electron transfer processes (ETP) (Duan et al., 2018). ETP offers several advantages in water decontamination, including specific targeting of electron-rich contaminants, maintaining high catalytic efficiency across a broad pH range, and delivering excellent performance in complex aqueous environments.

Single-atom catalysts (SACs) have shown exceptional efficiency in various catalytic reactions including activating persulfates for water decontamination and are increasingly popular in PS-AOPs (Chen et al., 2022; Cheng et al., 2023; Qing Wang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). SACs ensure full utilization of metal sites on a substrate, combining benefits of both heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysts (Wu and Kim, 2022). The well-defined and isolated metal atoms in SACs enhance selectivity during catalytic processes and improve recyclability. Additionally, the strong metal-support interactions in SACs make the metal active sites more stable and efficient than traditional nanoparticles in harsh conditions (acid or base environments). The adjustable coordination and electronic structures of SACs provide tremendous opportunities for precise material engineering at the atomic level in environmental catalysis (Ni et al., 2023; Shan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2020).

Tyres are critical components of automobiles with surging annual production of growing number of vehicles. By 2050, the number of global vehicles is expected to reach 2.4 billion, generating about 17 million tons of waste tyres annually (Okafor et al., 2020; Sienkiewicz et al., 2012). Unfortunately, approximately $4 0 ~ \%$ of these waste tyres end up in landfills or stockpiles, posing serious environmental risks due to their non-biodegradable nature and toxic composition (Fajimi et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Joseph and Christopher, 2024; Srinivasan et al., 2024; Valentini and Pegoretti, 2022). Therefore, upcycling of waste tyres into value-added products is essential for circular economy and environmental sustainability. As shown in Table S1, tyres usually include rubber or elastomers $( 4 7 ~ \% )$ , carbon black $( 2 2 ~ \% )$ , metals (17 $\% )$ , sulfur $( 1 ~ \% )$ , zinc oxide $( 1 ~ \% )$ , and additives $( 8 ~ \% )$ (Fajimi et al., 2024). Given their predominant carbon composition, transforming waste tyres into functional single-atom catalysts is a viable solution to mitigating their environmental impacts, reducing $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emissions, promoting carbon neutrality, and contributing to water purification (Ren et al., 2022).

In this study, we developed a salt template method to efficiently convert waste tyres into porous carbon with uniformly dispersed Cu single atoms (CuSA-Tyre). Cu catalyzed a $\mathrm { c - c }$ coupling process to selectively transform waste tyres into defective multi-element coordinated CuSA-Tyre. During this process, cuprous chloride (CuCl) pyrolyzed from copper chloride $\mathrm { ( C u C l _ { 2 } ) }$ served as both a carbonization catalyst and pore template for the production of carbon porous structure and metal single atoms. Benefiting from the highest specific surface area (SSA), CuSA-Tyre exhibited superior activation efficiency of peroxydisulfate (PDS) for contaminants degradation. Additionally, a fixed-bed reactor using sand as a catalyst carrier was developed to achieve longterm and high-performance water treatment. The used CuSA-Tyre can be easily separated and recycled. This study provides a new technique for upcycling waste tyres into functional SACs and new insights into Fenton-like chemistry to safeguard water quality.

# 2. Experimental methods

# 2.1. Synthesis of CuSA-Tyre and references from tyres

Firstly, waste tyres were chopped into small pieces using a blender. Next, $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ (or another metal salt) and the tyre pieces (mass ratio 40:1) were added to $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ ethanol. The mixture was then stirred at $7 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ until ethanol evaporated. The resulting precipitate was placed into a tube furnace, annealed at $2 1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ , and subsequently heated to 800 $^ \circ \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 3 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ under an ammonia atmosphere. The obtained carbon materials were ground into powder and mixed with $2 . 4 \mathrm { M }$ hydrochloric acid, stirring for $4 8 \mathrm { h }$ to thoroughly remove salt compounds. The mixture was then filtered and washed with ultrapure water until the pH of the washed solution reached 7. The purified carbon materials were dried in an oven at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for two days, and the dried carbon samples were named according to the metal salt $\mathrm { \ C u C l _ { 2 } }$ , $\mathrm { C o C l } _ { 2 }$ , $\mathrm { F e C l } _ { 3 }$ , $\mathrm { N i C l } _ { 2 }$ , and $\mathbf { M n C l } _ { 2 } \mathrm { , }$ ), such as CuSA-Tyre, Co-Tyre, Fe-Tyre, Ni-Tyre, Mn-Tyre, and C-Tyre (without a metal salt).

A Cu-N-C catalyst was synthesized by using carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and copper phthalocyanine (CuPc) according to a previous publication (Cao et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2023). CNTs $\left( 1 0 0 \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g } \right)$ were mixed with 200 mL N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) while CuPc ( $\mathrm { 1 0 ~ m g ) }$ ) was dissolved in $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m L } }$ DMF. Then, the CuPc/DMF mixture was dropped into the CNT/DMF mixture under vigorous stirring for $4 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ . The hybrid of assembled CuPc on the CNT support was separated by filtering and then processed via a freeze-drying treatment to obtain the Cu-N-C catalyst.

# 2.2. Material characterizations

The gas products pyrolyzed from tyre were collected by a gas bag and analyzed with gas chromatography (Agilent, 8890 GC). The morphologies of carbon samples were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU 7000) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 Spirit $1 2 0 ~ \mathrm { k V } )$ . The high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF) images and elemental mapping were conducted on the FEI Titan Themis 80–200. In-situ Raman spectra were performed on a Raman spectrometer (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution) with a $5 3 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ green laser. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted on the Rigaku MiniFlex 600. The specific surface areas of carbon samples were measured using the Brunauer− Emmett− Teller (BET) method from $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption− desorption isotherms (V-Sorb 2800P, Ultrametrics). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker ESR-5000 Magnettech instrument using 5,5- dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidinol (TEMP) as spin-trapping agents. The X-ray absorption data were collected on the X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) and soft X-ray spectroscopy (SXRS) beamline at Australian Synchrotron (Melbourne). All X-ray absorption spectra were measured in a transmission mode at room temperature, with Cu foil used for calibration.

# 2.3. Catalytic performance of carbon materials in AOPs

Phenol (or other organic pollutants, $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m M } )$ was dissolved into 50 mL ultrapure water, followed by addition of $0 . 0 5 ~ \mathrm { g ~ l } ^ { - 1 }$ catalyst (CuSATyre or other catalysts). After $1 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ pre-adsorption, $0 . 2 \mathrm { m M }$ PDS (or a specified amount) was added. The solution samples were periodically withdrawn using a syringe equipped with a $0 . 2 2 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ filter. All degradation tests were performed at $2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with constant stirring at $8 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ . The pseudo-first-order kinetic rate constants were calculated by the following equation.

$$
\ln ( C / C _ { 0 } ) = - k _ { o b s } t
$$

Where, $C _ { O }$ is the initial organic concentration, $C$ is the organic concentration at time $t ,$ and $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ is the reaction rate constant.

During the quenching tests, radical scavengers and the pollutants were added in solution before the addition of catalysts, and the following procedures were the same as in the degradation tests. The dosage of scavengers was adjusted based on the requirements of each test. For the actual water impact tests, inorganic ions were added after phenol, and the other procedures were identical to the quenching tests. In the tests for pH effect, the pH of the solution was tuned by $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } S O _ { 4 } }$ and NaOH after adding phenol or buffer solution, and the other procedures were the same as in the quenching tests. The concentration of organics was analyzed using ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Ultimate 3000, Thermo Scientific) with a C-18 column and the analytical conditions are listed in Table S2.

# 2.4. Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical measurements were performed using a CHI760E electrochemical workstation. In preparation for a working electrode, 5 mg carbon samples were mixed with $4 5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ isopropanol, $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ water, and $5 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ Nafion $^ { \mathrm { \textregistered } }$ 117, forming a suspension liquid through sonication. The suspension was then coated on a glassy carbon electrode, serving as the working electrode. A platinum wire was used as the counter electrode, and an $\mathbf { A g / A g C l }$ electrode served as the reference electrode.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Synthesis and analysis of waste tyre-derived CuSA-Tyre catalysts

Fig. 1 illustrates the catalytic transformation processes of tyre pyrolysis with various metal chloride salts. Transition metals, such as Fe, Co, Ni, and Mn, exhibit relatively higher carbon affinity, attributed to their partially filled d-orbitals. These metals preferentially cleave $\mathrm { C - H }$ and $\mathsf { C } { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { C }$ bonds within the rubber matrix and facilitate the conversion of tyres into $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ , $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } .$ , and hydrocarbon products (Fig. S1). This mechanism bears resemblance to the Fischer-Tropsch processes. In contrast, Cu, with its fully filled d-orbital configuration, demonstrates significantly low carbon affinity. During tyre pyrolysis, Cu predominantly promotes the cleavage of $\mathrm { C - H }$ bonds while simultaneously enhancing C–C bond formation and resulting in the highest carbon formation. This unique Cucatalyzed C–C coupling selectively transformed $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ and tyre-derived components into carbon materials (Fig. S1 and Table S3), HCl, and CuCl (Fig. S2). These findings not only reveal a novel pathway to transform waste tyres into functional carbon materials but also offer a promising strategy for advancing solid waste management and carbon recycling technologies.

# 3.2. Atomic structure analysis of CuSA-Tyre derived from tyres

Synchrotron soft X-ray spectroscopy (SXRS) is a powerful technique for high-resolution probing the chemical bonding configurations of materials. The carbon K-edge spectra of all samples in Fig. S12 showed that three clear peaks were located at 285.2 (peak A), 288.1 (peak B), and 293.1 eV (peak C). Peaks A and C were assigned to the dipole transition of the C 1 s core electron to $\pi ^ { * } \ : C = C$ and $\sigma ^ { * } { \mathrm { C - C } }$ orbitals, respectively, and peak B was attributed to the charge transfer induced by the bonding of C–N/S/Cl (Coleman et al., 2008; Han et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022; Shang et al., 2020). Fig. 3a depicts the N K-edge spectra of all samples, and CuSA-Tyre exhibits three distinct peaks, corresponding to pyridinic, pyrrolic, and graphitic nitrogen (Li et al., 2022; Shang et al., 2020). The S K-edge and Cl l-edge spectra of CuSA-Tyre were observed at 2466 and $2 1 5 \ \mathrm { e V }$ , respectively (Fig. 3b), indicating S and Cl were functionalized on CuSA-Tyre. The Cu l-edge spectrum of CuSA-Tyre was shown in Fig. 3c. The $\mathrm { L } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { L } _ { 3 }$ edges observed at 950 and $9 3 0 ~ \mathrm { e V } $ , respectively, were ascribed to the excitation of $\mathrm { C u } 2 p$ orbital electrons to the unoccupied 3d orbital (Shang et al., 2020). The Cu-N-C sample exhibits a sharp and symmetric L₃ peak, which is the characteristic of a ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ center in a well-defined square-planar CuN₄ coordination with high D₄h symmetry (Cao et al., 2023). In contrast, the L₃ peak of CuSA-Tyre is broader and less intense, indicating a lower symmetry coordination environment and possible deviation from a pure $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ oxidation state. The inclusion of soft donor ligands (S and Cl) in CuSA-Tyre likely leads to enhanced 3d-ligand orbital hybridization and electron delocalization, which broadens the absorption feature.

Synchrotron X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses were employed to explore the local electronic structure and coordination environment of CuSA-Tyre and references. The Cu K-edge XANES patterns in Fig. 3d indicated that CuSA-Tyre exhibited similar spectra to copper phthalocyanine (CuPc), suggesting their comparable $\mathrm { D } _ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ symmetry (Zhang et al., 2019). The pre-edge features of all samples were highlighted in the inset of Fig. 3d The peak at 8979 eV is attributed to quadrupole-allowed dipole-forbidden $1 s \  \ 3 d$ excitation, which signalled the mixing of $3 d$ and $4 p$ orbitals of Cu atoms. The weak pre-edge intensity of CuPc implies its high $D _ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ symmetric geometry, and the increased pre-edge peak intensity in CuSA-Tyre was assigned to the increased dipole-allowed $1 s  4 p$ electron transition and the distorted $D _ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ symmetry (Ren et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2018). The adsorption edge of CuSA-Tyre was positioned between CuPc and Cu foil (the inset in Fig. 3d), indicating that the average oxidation state of Cu species in CuSA-Tyre was between $0 \sim + 2$ (Shang et al., 2020; Su et al., 2022). The Fourier transformed (FT) EXAFS spectra of CuSA-Tyre and references (Cu foil, CuPc, $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ , and $\mathrm { C u _ { 2 } } S _ { \mathrm { . } }$ ) were displayed in Fig. 3e. An FT peak for CuSA-Tyre was observed at $1 . 6 3 \mathrm { \AA }$ , sitting between the peaks of Cu-N (1.5 Å), Cu-Cl (1.72 Å), and Cu-S (1.87 Å), implying the scattering of Cu-N, Cu-Cl, and Cu-S coordination in CuSA-Tyre. Furthermore, no Cu-Cu peak at $2 . 2 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ }$ or longer radical distance was observed for CuSA-Tyre, which excluded Cu clusters or nanoparticles and confirmed the single-atom structure for CuSA-Tyre. The quantitative EXAFS curve fitting analysis for CuSA-Tyre was investigated in Fig. 3f and Table S4. The fitting curves matched quite well with the experimental EXAFS curves, indicating that the first shell of the central Cu atom was coordinated with two N atoms, one S atom, and one axial Cl atom $\mathrm { ( C u N _ { 2 } S C l }$ , inset of Fig. 3f) (Cheng et al., 2022; Shang et al., 2020). The wavelet transform (WT) contour plots of CuSA-Tyre and references $\mathrm { C u _ { 2 } } s _ { \mathrm { : } }$ , CuPc, and $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ ) further support the co-existence of Cu-N, Cu-Cl, and Cu-S bonds (with a maximum peak at $4 . 7 \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } ^ { - 1 } ]$ in CuSA-Tyre (Fig. 3g).

# 3.3. Catalytic performance and oxidation pathway of CuSA-Tyre/PD

Product yield is critical in both materials synthesis and tyre upgrading. Fig. 4a shows that the product yield of CuSA-Tyre reached 66.4 wt. $\%$ (transforming $_ { 1 g }$ tyres into $0 . 6 6 4 g$ catalyst). $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ achieves the highest tyre transformation efficiency compared to other transition metal (TM) salts (including Co, Mn, Fe, and Ni). In catalytic application, the adsorption curve of CuSA-Tyre (Fig. S13) displayed that the adsorption dynamic equilibrium was achieved in $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , and then the degradation tests were performed after $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ pre-adsorption. Fig. 4b shows the catalytic performance of different TM-derived samples, and CuSA-Tyre demonstrated the best degradation efficiency, achieving 100 $\%$ phenol removal in $2 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Meanwhile, we also synthesized a Cu-N-C catalyst composed of CuPc assembled on CNTs for comparison. The Cu$\mathrm { N - C + P D S }$ system only removed $4 6 ~ \%$ phenol in $2 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , which further verified the excellent catalytic performance of CuSA-Tyre with the unique active $\mathrm { C u N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S C l }$ sites.

The impact of PDS dosage on the catalytic process was investigated (Fig. S14). The degradation curve plateaued after $6 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ with $6 8 ~ \%$ phenol removal at a PDS dosage of $0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ . When the PDS dosage was $0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ or higher, CuSA-Tyre achieved $1 0 0 ~ \%$ phenol removal, suggesting that $0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ was the optimal dosage. To further explore the catalytic performance of CuSA-Tyre, we calculated the normalized kinetic rate constant $( k _ { \mathrm { o b s } } ,$ Table S5) based on the loadings of pollutant, oxidant, and catalyst. Compared with other SACs (Fig. 4c), CuSA-Tyre/ PDS system achieved the highest normalized $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } }$ at the expense of the lowest PDS dosage for the removal of phenol and bisphenol A (BPA). Moreover, the price of PDS (116 USD/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) is cheaper than that of peroxymonosulfate (PMS, 142 USD/kg, Sigma-Aldrich), which makes the CuSA-Tyre+PDS system more competitive in actual water remediation.

Phenol was used as a pollutant probe to investigate the oxidation pathway of the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system because of its vulnerability to attack by both radical and nonradical species. Fig. 4d illustrates a negligible effect of various radical scavengers (methanol, ethanol, tertiary butanol (TBA), TEMP, and NaF) on the phenol removal performance in the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system, which excludes the generation of free radicals, surface-bound radicals, or $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ during the phenol oxidation process (Chen et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2022). Nitrobenzene serves as a chemical probe for •OH radicals, while atrazine and benzoic acid are typical chemical probes for both •OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals (Chen et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020). The nearly flat curves observed after adding PDS for these chemical probes (Fig. S15) suggested that both •OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ radicals were not generated in the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system. A probe of DMPO was used as the spin-trapping agent to capture short-lived •OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , producing long-lived spin adducts that can be detected by EPR (Duan et al., 2015). Fig. S16a showed a signal of 5,5-dimethyl-2-oxopyrroline-1-oxyl, which is a direct oxidation product of DMPO rather than a radical adduct. Fig. S16b displayed that no $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ was trapped by TEMP in the EPR analysis (Tian et al., 2021). Based on these investigations, free radicals and $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ were excluded from being generated in the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system.

In-situ Raman analysis (Fig. 4e) was used to explore the interaction between CuSA-Tyre and PDS. The PDS solution exhibited characteristic peaks at 978 and $1 0 7 5 . 6 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ for $S _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 8 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ . After adding CuSA-Tyre, new peaks appear at 979.8 (for $S O _ { 4 } { } ^ { 2 - }$ ) and $1 0 4 9 . 6 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ , indicating the formation of intermediates $\boldsymbol { ( \mathrm { P D } 8 ^ { * } }$ ) on the single Cu site (Wang et al., 2020). When phenol was added, these peaks diminished, demonstrating that the non-radical $\mathbf { P } \mathbf { D } \mathbf { S } ^ { \ast }$ complex was the active species that might coordinate an electron transfer process (ETP) for pollutant oxidation. Open circuit potential tests (Fig. 4f) further verified the ETP pathway for the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system. Upon stabilizing the CuSA-Tyre electrode in water, adding PDS at varying concentrations induced a dramatic increase in potential, and the equilibrium potential is positively correlated with PDS concentration $_ { 0 . 8 \mathrm { e V } }$ for $0 . 1 \mathrm { m M }$ , $0 . 8 5 \mathrm { e V }$ for $0 . 2 \mathrm { m M }$ , $0 . 8 8 \mathrm { e V }$ for $0 . 3 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ , and $0 . 8 9 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ for $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ ). When phenol was added, the potential gradually decreased as $\mathbf { P } \mathbf { D } \mathbf { S } ^ { \ast }$ extracted electrons from phenol, confirming that ETP-based phenol degradation in the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system. Chronopotentiometry tests (Fig. S17) showed that applying a potential of $0 . 8 8 \mathrm { e V }$ , aligned with the equilibrium potential of PDS in the open circuit potential, resulted in a sharp current drop to $4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 7 } \mu \mathrm { A } ,$ indicating CuSA-Tyre was the electron donor during $\mathbf { P } \mathbf { D } \mathbf { S } ^ { \ast }$ formation. The current significantly increased to $4 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \mu \mathrm { A }$ upon the addition of phenol and then gradually decreased to $9 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 7 } \mu \mathrm { A } ,$ , suggesting that CuSA-Tyre acted as an electron shuttle during phenol oxidation, abstracting electrons from phenol and transferring them to PDS (Ren et al., 2022).

To further confirm the ETP regime in the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system, a galvanic oxidation system (GOS) was developed (Fig. 4g). The CuSATyre catalyst was coated on graphite paper electrodes, with PDS and phenol solutions in separate cells connected by a proton exchange membrane and ammeter. The CuSA-Tyre+GOS system (Fig. 4h) showed a high instantaneous current $( 1 3 0 9 ~ \mu \mathrm { A } )$ and $1 0 0 ~ \%$ phenol removal, whereas the GOS system with a graphite paper electrode only exhibited a low current of $6 6 ~ \mu \mathrm { A }$ and $\sim 6 ~ \%$ phenol removal. The significant difference between the two systems provides a strong evidence for the ETP regime triggered by PDS activation on CuSA-Tyre, where electrons were transferred from phenol through the CuSA-Tyre electrode to $\mathbf { P } \mathbf { D } \mathbf { S } ^ { \ast }$ to realize phenol oxidation. The CuSA-Tyre catalyst acted as both a PDS activator and the electron mediator in the ETP regime. As demonstrated above, the Cu single site played a dominant role in PDS activation, which could be further identified by a poisoning experiment using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and potassium thiocyanate (KSCN) as the complexing reagents to screen the Cu sites (Chen et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 4i, the removal rate of phenol was significantly inhibited when EDTA or KSCN was added, suggesting that the single-atom Cu species were the dominant catalytic sites for PDS activation.

We also assessed the efficiency of the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system for purification of a diversity of organic pollutants, including phenol (PE), 4-chlorophenol (4CP), bisphenol A (BPA), 2-(4-chlorophenoxy)-2- methylpropionic acid (CFA), 4-hydroxyacetophenone (HAP), 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (HBA), 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde (HBAl), 4-bromophenol (4BP), and sulfanilamide (SA). All the contaminants can be effectively removed with over $7 0 \%$ purification efficiency within $2 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ (Fig. 5a, Fig. S18 and S19). Adsorption capacity is a significant factor in PS-AOPs (Wang et al., 2015), and the adsorption capacities of different organic pollutants were summarized in Fig. S20. A positive correlation was established between the adsorption capacity and oxidation rate constants (Fig. 5b), highlighting the importance of pre-adsorption of pollutants in the ETP regime in PS-AOPs (Al Zoubi and Ko, 2018; Zhang et al., 2021). The half-wave potential $\varphi _ { 1 / 2 }$ (approximate one-electron oxidation potential) of various pollutants is another crucial factor in assessing the reactivity of organic oxidation in the ETP regime (Ren et al., 2019). The $\boldsymbol { \Phi } 1 / 2$ values of different pollutants were collected in Fig. S21, and Fig. 5c showed a negative correlation between $\varphi _ { 1 / 2 }$ and $k _ { \mathrm { o b s } } ,$ indicating the pollutant oxidation follows the ETP mechanism in which $\mathbf { P } \mathbf { D } \mathbf { S } ^ { \ast }$ is the primary reactive species in the CuSA-Tyre/PDS

After the reaction, the used CuSA-Tyre was separated and the filtrate was used to conduct the degradation to assess the contribution of dissolved Cu to PDS activation. Fig. S22 reveals that the post-reaction solution failed to activate PDS and phenol could not be removed, indicating the leached Cu did not contribute to the degradation process. The post-reaction solution was also analyzed by ICP-MS, and only 1.5 ppb of Cu was detected, indicating the structural robustness of the Cu single-atom sites in CuSA-Tyre. In addition, Fig. S23 and S24 compared the XRD patterns, SEM, and TEM images of CuSA-Tyre before and after the reaction, where the carbon structures manifested identically similar, indicating the stable structure of CuSA-Tyre. The total organic carbon (TOC) in the reaction solution was reduced from 9.05 to $1 . 1 8 ~ \mathrm { m g ~ l } ^ { - 1 }$ (Fig. S25) after the treatment, demonstrating the excellent pollutant removal efficiency of the CuSA-Tyre/PDS oxidation system.

Natural water resources typically contain various inorganic ions (such as $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ , ${ \mathrm { N O } } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ ) and humic acid (HA), which can potentially affect pollutant removal efficiency in AOPs (He and O’Shea, 2020; Hu et al., 2018; Wang and Wang, 2021). However, Fig. 5d depicted that the presence of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ , $S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ , $\mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ , HA or the use of tap water, river water, and lake water had a negligible impact on the degradation of phenol by the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system. These results suggest that the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system is effective in actual water remediation scenarios. Harsh conditions, such as changes in $\mathsf { p H }$ , also influence degradation in PS-AOPs (Peng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). Nevertheless, Fig. 5e and S26 demonstrated that the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system maintained high removal efficiency under both acidic and basic conditions, achieving $8 5 \ \%$ phenol removal at $\tt p H 1 1$ and $1 0 0 ~ \%$ removal from $\mathrm { p H } 3$ to 10. This resistance to harsh conditions highlights the potential of the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system in extreme water purification applications. In addition to PDS, CuSA-Tyre can also activate other oxidants such as PMS, periodates $\left( \mathrm { I O } _ { 4 } ^ { - } \right)$ and peroxyacetic acid (PAA), maintaining excellent pollutant removal efficiency (Fig. 5f and S27).

The long-term practical treatment of phenol in simulated wastewater was evaluated using a fixed-bed reactor filled with a mixture of sand and CuSA-Tyre (Fig. 5g, S28 and S29). The mixed solution of phenol and PDS flowed through the catalyst in the reactor, which was driven by a peristaltic pump, and the removal efficiency was monitored by analyzing the phenol concentration in the treated effluent. The system achieved $1 0 0 \%$ phenol removal in continuously treating $^ { 4 0 \mathrm { ~ L ~ } }$ of wastewater (Fig. 5h), demonstrating the high decontamination efficiency and stability of the CuSA-Tyre/PDS system in practical wastewater treatment. After the reaction, CuSA-Tyre can be easily separated from the sand by stirring, shaking and precipitation in water due to their different buoyancies (Fig. S30 and the inset of Fig. 5h). The cost-effectiveness evaluation in Table S6 shows that the cost of treating 1 t of simulated wastewater was only 1.06 dollars. The low treatment cost indicates the competitiveness of CuSA-Tyre/PDS for practical water purification.

# 4. Conclusion

In the study, we successfully developed a facile salt template method for the transformation of waste tyres into functional carbon materials with embedded copper single atoms coordinated with multi-element and defects. In tyre pyrolysis, Cu catalyzed a $\mathsf { C } { \mathrm { - } } \mathsf { C }$ coupling process to selectively transform waste tyres into CuSA-Tyre and $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ derived CuCl acted as the template and carbonization catalyst for tyre transformation and conversion into porous carbons spontaneously loaded with Cu single sites. The product yield achieved $6 6 . 4 ~ \%$ and the SSA of the resulting CuSA-Tyre reached $\dot { 1 } 2 1 8 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ . The CuSA-Tyre exhibited superior catalytic performance in activating different oxidants (PDS, PMS, PAA, and $\mathrm { I O _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ ) for pollutant degradation through an electron transfer pathway. Characterisations confirmed the robustness and high stability of CuSA-Tyre under various environmental conditions. A continuous system using the catalyst achieved long-term water decontamination and recycling of CuSA-Tyre. This research provides a promising approach to upcycling waste tyres to value-added functional SACs and contributes to the development of AOPs for practical water purification.
