# Phase-Regulated Interfacial Polarization in High-Entropy Materials Enhances Fenton-Like Oxidation and Self-Induced Coagulation for Environmental Remediation

Tenghui Jin, Jiajie Xu, Kaizhou Huang, Wei Qu,\* Zhuoyun Tang, Ming Chen, Rajasekhar Balasubramanian, and J. Paul Chen\*

High-entropy materials (HEMs) have garnered significant interest in Fenton-like catalysis for water treatment due to their tailorable structures and unique high-entropy effects. However, conventional single-phase solid-solution HEMs often suffer from rigid active sites, untunable electronic structures, and non-equilibrium phase transitions induced by lattice stress, limiting their application in multi-step Fenton-like reactions. To overcome this, a Rhombohedral (R-3c) phase is successfully derived within a Face-Centered Cubic (FCC)-based HEM by employing amorphous carbon to disrupt the crystal structure and optimizing calcination conditions. Facilitated by citric acid decomposition promoting oxygen penetration into the metal lattice, this optimization yielded polycrystalline CoCuMnAlLaCr composites with abundant structural defects and phase interfaces. The intense polarization from built-in electric field at FCC/R-3c interfaces enabled ultrafast peroxymonosulfate activation, achieving over $9 3 . 6 \%$ pollutant removal within 2 min across $p H 3 - 7 0$ , with robust resistance to real water matrix interferences. Crucially, phase reconstruction combined with manganese incorporation enabled multi-step pathways: a self-initiated $\scriptstyle { \mathsf { M n o } } _ { 2 }$ -based coagulation process followed initial oxidation step, efficiently recycling leached trace metal ions and removing intermediates. This work breaks the limitations of single-phase HEMs by synergistically combining multiple phase interfaces with distinct work functions and creating a dual oxidation-coagulation system, providing a novel framework for designing advanced environmental catalysts.

# 1. Introduction

High-entropy materials (HEMs), comprising five or more elements randomly occupying cation lattice sites, represent a distinct class of multi-metal catalysts for environmental remediation.[1-3] Unlike conventional catalysts, their high-entropy structure induces lattice distortion and sluggish diffusion kinetics, which enhance surface area and charge redistribution. Compared to conventional mono-doped or binary/ternary alloy, the combination of transition metals in HEM generates a unique "cocktail effect", enhancing catalytic performance with varying reactive oxygen species (ROS)generation and selectivity.[4] The diverse atomic arrangements and surface microstructure facilitate varied reactants/intermediates adsorption modes, ensuring efficient oxidation.[5] Furthermore, the partial occupation of $3 d$ orbitals in transition metals (e.g., Co, Cu, Mn, and Fe) promotes electron transfer in Fenton-like chemistry, making HEM highly promising for peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation[6,7].

While functional HEMs, including perovskite,[8] spinel and other oxides,[9] have been successfully synthesized for PMS activation, prevailing design strategies deliberately restrict phase composition to single Face-Centered Cubic (FCC), Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) or Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP) structures to preclude second-phase interference.[10] Nevertheless, inherent limitations persist in such single-phase systems. Even with atomically dispersed metals forming solid solutions, the rigid crystalline lattice confines most active sites, hindering their full exposure and thereby impeding mass transfer by forcing reactants to overcome significant surface barriers. Concurrently, electronic interactions between constituent metals become highly averaged, exemplified by the FeCoNiCuPd system where only Fe and Co functioned as primary catalytic sites, while Ni, Cu, and Pd merely facilitated electron transport, failing to leverage full multicomponent synergy.[11] This site-function fixation inherently constrains electron-transfer pathway diversity, limiting reaction kinetics. Furthermore, single-phase structures predominantly favor a singular activation mechanism (radical or non-radical), severely compromising their adaptability and interference resistance in complex pollutant systems, particularly in the presence of anions like $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ or $\mathrm { H S O _ { 4 } } ^ { - }$ [12] Contrary to prevailing misconceptions that (I) the high-entropy effect ensures simple solidsolution phases and (II) single-phase systems represent ideal configurations, secondary phases intrinsically generate interfacial defects (e.g., phase boundaries). These defects furnish additional active sites and enable diversified activation pathways. Critically, dynamic compositional changes between phases can also facilitate multi-step reaction mechanisms.[13]

Interestingly, HEM often contains multiple phases at room temperature. Although initially forming a single-phase solid solution upon solidification, annealing at intermediate temperatures typically induces phase separation into metallic and intermetallic phases.[14] This behavior stems from strong interatomic interactions and sluggish atomic diffusion kinetics, which collectively generate a hysteretic diffusion effect. Consequently, precipitated phases undergo retarded growth post-nucleation, thereby facilitating the formation of nanoscale secondary phases. From a compositional perspective, constituent elements exhibit distinct phase-forming tendencies that govern the overall microstructure during synthesis. Tong et al. systematically investigated this phenomenon through Al doping in $\mathrm { A l _ { x } }$ CoCrCuFeNi alloys.[15] With minimal aluminum addition, the alloys maintained a simple FCC solid-solution structure. As the aluminum content increased to ${ \tt X } = 0 . 8$ , a BCC structure emerges, forming a mixed FCC and BCC eutectic phase. Ultimately, higher aluminum levels $( \mathbf { x } = 2 . 8 )$ yield a single ordered BCC phase. Building upon such two-phase architectures, a space charge region spontaneously develops at the phase interfaces due to work function disparities, generating a built-in electric field. This field drives directional carriers (electron-hole pairs) migration, significantly extending their lifetime. In spinel/perovskite biphasic high-entropy oxides, interfacial electron redistribution shifts the redox potential of ${ \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 2 + } / { \mathrm { C o } } ^ { 3 + }$ by $0 . 2 8 \mathrm { V } ,$ significantly reducing the activation energy barrier of PMS activation.[16] This polarization-induced band bending enables sustained efficient electron transfer across broad pH ranges. Consequently, developing controllable methodologies for precise phase composition tuning is of paramount importance for advancing high-performance catalyst design.

The introduction of high-entropy structures offers a promising approach to balance the trade-off between reactivity and stability in heterogeneous Fenton-like catalyst design.[17] However, activating PMS without metal leaching or external energy input is challenging due to energy limitations. Alternatively, leveraging the multi-step functionality of dual-phase systems, PMS can be strategically coupled with $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ or other transition metals to establish oxidation-coagulation processes.[1821] This integrated approach enhances contaminant removal efficiency: (I) coagulants facilitate pollutant aggregation via charge neutralization/bridging, while (II) concurrently capturing oxidative byproducts, and provides a more holistic solution. Manganese (Mn)-based coagulants can effectively remove a wide range of contaminants, including organic matter, heavy metals, and suspended solids, which can be regarded as an ideal self-initiated coagulant especially in the ROS-rich environment.[22] Thus, the compositional diversity and selectivity of Mn-based HEM show great potential in the formation of coagulation systems. To the best of our knowledge, the use of multiphase high-entropy structures in Fenton-like oxidation-coagulation processes to eliminate organic pollutants has not been reported yet.

Herein, a novel dual-phase HEM (CoCuMnAlLaCr) was synthesized by selecting specific elements and controlling oxygen penetration. The inclusion of Mn is essential for establishing a self-initiated oxidation-coagulation reaction system, while cobalt (Co) serves as the primary active site for efficient peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation.[23] The incorporation of lanthanum (La) and Copper (Cu), transition metals with multiple valence states, enhances activation as additional active sites while serving as the A-site in perovskite $\left( \mathsf { A B O } _ { 3 } \right)$ and contributing to the FCC crystal framework separately. Chromium $( \mathrm { C r } )$ acts primarily as an electron donor to regulate the electronic configuration.[24] Aluminum (Al) stabilizes the FCC configuration and induces significant lattice distortion with its relatively large atomic radius (143 pm). This integrated multi-metal system was evaluated for PMS activation performance using atrazine (ATZ, a common pesticide) as a model contaminant, with particular focus on elucidating the mechanistic contributions of multi-phase structures with identical elemental composition to ATZ removal. Additionally, it aims to develop a dual oxidation-coagulation system to ensure effective intermediate/by-product management. Collectively, the study proposes a novel design strategy of Fenton-like catalysts for high-performance, low-dosage, and green integrated water treatment.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Synthesis and Characterization of HEM

The HEM, CoCuMnAlLaCr, was synthesized via a solvothermal synthesis route as schematically illustrated in Figure 1A. During the anaerobic thermal treatment stage, chromium's exceptional oxygen affinity drives the incorporation of three distinct oxygen sources: organic oxygen from citric acid $( \mathrm { C } _ { 6 } \mathrm { H } _ { 8 } \mathrm { O } _ { 7 } )$ ligands, surface-absorbed oxygen species, and adventitious oxygen (Text S2, Supporting Information).[25] Oxygen species initially diffuse into the FCC lattice via vacancy-mediated pathways. As temperature increases, interstitial oxygen acquires sufficient activation energy for lattice incorporation, ultimately forming stable lattice oxygen. At this point, La becomes the primary A-site cation due to its ionic radius, while the other elements occupy the Bsite, inducing a phase transition and the formation of perovskite structure based on the FCC arrangement.

The phase composition of HEM is influenced by various fctors, including elemental composition, doping proportions,[15,27] calcination temperature and time, as well as annealing conditions.[8,29] To evaluate the impact of intrducing a second phase without altering the overall composition, the study focused on calcination temperature as the variable. The crystalline structure of HEM formed in an argon atmosphere at temperatures ranging from 600 to $1 1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The microstructure of 900CoCuMnAlLaCr was further examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Figure 1D, the HEM morphology presents as hanging spherical particles. Under the same magnification, distinct alloy clusters were observed, indicating the presence of at least two or more arrangements within the material. The energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mappings (Figure 1GL) reveal the presence of all six metallic elements in the catalyst. The observed elemental heterogeneity suggests the coexistence of two distinct phases within the 900CoCuMnAlLaCr material. X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF) analysis (Appendix Figure S1B, Supporting Information) further confirms that the six metal elements are present in approximately equal molar ratios.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analyses were conducted to investigate the lattice structure and crystal planes arrangement. The SAED results (Appendix Figure S1E, Supporting Information) reveal polycrystalline rings corresponding to multiple crystal planes that align with XRD findings. Additionally, TEM observations identified three crystal planes: (024) and (104) corresponding to the R-3c structure, and (111) associated with FCC structures. The individual crystal planes exhibit well-ordered nodes of varying sizes, reflecting the short-range order typical of HEM. At the junction of the (111) and (024) planes, noticeable lattice expansion occurs, with the spacing of the (024) crystal plane increasing significantly from $\overset { \cdot } { 1 . 9 5 2 } \overset { \cdot } { \mathrm { A } }$ to $3 . 9 0 4 \mathrm { ~ \AA } .$ The enlarged HRSTEM and HRTEM images (Figure 1MN) reveal abundant structural defects, including discontinuous lattice fringes, stacking faults, and point defects. Furthermore, distinct interface defects and lattice distortion are observed at the intersection of the three crystal planes within the densely packed structure (Figure 1O-P). In 900CoCuMnAlLaCr, the hetero-interfaces resulting from dual phases, along with severe lattice distortion and defects induced by the high-entropy stabilization effect, contribute to its enhanced catalytic Fenton-like activity.

To understand the phase composition, the surface chemistry of 600CoCuMnAlLaCr, 900CoCuMnAlLaCr, and 900CoCuMnAlLaCr-O was studied by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As displayed in the high-resolution C 1s XPS spectra (Figure 2A), the peak at $2 8 4 . 8 \ \mathrm { e V }$ indicates the presence of amorphous carbon across all three HEMs. Under anaerobic conditions at $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , the ${ \mathrm { C } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { O } }$ bond peak is notably stronger, suggesting secondary oxidation of the organic carbon skeleton. The O 1s XPS spectra (Figure 2B) display three characteristic peaks at 529.6, 531.2, and $5 3 3 . 5 \mathrm { e V } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ , assigned to lattice oxygen in metal-oxygen (M—O) bonds, surface-adsorbed oxygen/hydroxyl groups, and ${ \mathrm { C } } { \mathrm { - } } { \mathrm { O } }$ bond in hydroxyl groups, respectively.[31] Critically, the $\mathrm { { O } _ { \mathrm { { s u r f } } } / \mathrm { { O } _ { \mathrm { { L } } } } }$ ratio varies significantly across materials: 2.38 (600CoCuMnAlLaCr), 0.37 (900CoCuMnAlLaCr-O), and 1.49 (900CoCuMnAlLaCr). This progressive decrease in surface oxygen dominance correlates with the emergence of dual-phase structure in 900CoCuMnAlLaCr. Notably, the dual-phase system exhibits: (I) A $0 . 4 { - } 0 . 8 \mathrm { e V }$ blue shift in O 1s binding energy versus 600CoCuMnAlLaCr, indicating electron transfer from oxygen to multivalent metal cations; (II) Enhanced lattice distortions from high-entropy cation mixing, which stabilize lattice oxygen. Collectively, these results confirm that lattice oxygen enrichment facilitates second-phase crystallization, while suppressed surface oxygen activity accompanies phase stabilization in the HEM.

Complementary electronic and structural analyses reveal three key characteristics of the dual-phase 900CoCuMnAlLaCr system: (I) Valence band spectroscopy positions its $d$ -band center closer to the Fermi level than reference materials (Figure 2G), enhancing adsorbate-catalyst interactions; (II) Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) signals at $\mathrm { g } = 1 . 9 6$ (Figure 2D) confirm increased unpaired electron density within the dual-phase architecture, facilitating interfacial charge transfer during redox cycles; and (III) TEM reveals agglomerated particles of varying size containing abundant crystalline heterointerfaces formed by intersecting lattice planes (Figure 2LN), providing high-density active sites for PMS activation. Mott-Schottky analysis further elucidates the electronic structure of the individual phases and their composite. The isolated FCC and R-3c phases exhibit p-type semiconducting behavior, with flat-band potentials of $- 0 . 6 0$ and $- 1 . 3 8 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs. SCE, respectively. The significant interfacial potential offset is directly corroborated by KPFM (Figure 2IK), which reveals a pronounced work function difference between the two phases. Together, these results confirm the establishment of a robust built-in electric field at the heterophase boundary, providing a fundamental mechanism for enhanced charge separation and catalytic efficiency.

This decrease in efficiency with higher catalyst dosage may be attributed to: (I) radical scavenging caused by an excessive amount of the catalyst; (II) increased turbidity or precipitation at higher catalyst concentrations, hindering mass transfer and reducing contact between radicals and ATZ molecules; and (III) potential self-quenching reactions among the excess radicals. When PMS concentration was increased to $1 . 2 \mathrm { m m }$ (Figure 3E), the ATZ degradation occurred almost instantly within $2 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ across all groups, suggesting that even at $0 . 0 1 6 7 \ \mathrm { g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ , the catalyst could effectively activate the excess PMS and generate sufficient ROS. PMS alone had negligible impact on ATZ degradation (less than $1 0 \%$ . Figure 3E indicates that PMS concentration is the primary factor influencing both the efficiency and extent of the reaction, with degradation increasing as PMS concentration rises. Additionally, gradient experiments showed that contaminant concentration did not limit degradation efficiency (Figure 3F), suggesting that the abundance of active sites or ROS in the system results in degradation behavior resembling zero-order kinetics.

The Fenton-like activity of dual-phase 900CoCuMnAlLaCr was further assessed using nine additional organic microcontaminants (OMCs) of environmental and public health concern, including methyl orange (MO), 2,4,6-trichlorophenol (TCP), sulfamethoxazole (SMX), ibuprofen (IBU), erythromycin (ERY), ciprofloxacin hydrochloride (CIP), tetracycline hydrochloride (TCH), ofloxacin (OFL), and trimethoprim (TMP) (Figure 3G). The chemical structures of selected OMCs are given in Appendix Figure S17 (Supporting Information). Removal efficiencies ranged from $8 5 \%$ to $100 \%$ , demonstrating the robust degradation capacity of 900CoCuMnAlLaCr across various OMCs. The coexistence of electron-donating groups (EDGs, e.g., hydroxyl, amino, methyl, and organic sulfide groups) and electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs, e.g., carboxyl groups) in these OMCs necessitates versatile degradation pathways. In the 900CoCuMnAlLaCr/PMS system, this challenge is addressed through dual-pathway synergy. Typically, radicals $( { \bullet } \mathrm { O H } / { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } } ^ { { \bullet } - } )$ indiscriminately degrade both EDG-rich and EWG-rich contaminants via unselective oxidation, while non-radical pathways $( ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 } /$ electron transfer) preferentially target EDGs through electrophilic attack.[37] This bifunctionality stems from the catalyst's engineered heterointerfaces between R-3c and FCC phases, where interfacial polarization creates complementary active sites.Consequently, the system dynamically adapts its dominant pathway based on contaminant electronic properties, achieving ${ > } 8 5 \%$ removal efficiency for both nucleophilic (e.g., sulfamethoxazole) and electrophilic pollutants (e.g., trichlorophenol). Such interfacial site-selectivity underscores the material's precise reactivity control beyond singlephase catalysts.

# 2.3. Roles of ROS in Fenton-like and Self-Induced Coagulation Processes

Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was employed to qualitatively identify ROS generated during PMS activation by 900CoCuMnAlLaCr. Using DMPO as a spin-trapping agent for radical species and TEMP for ${ } ^ { 1 } { \mathsf { O } } _ { 2 }$ [38] characteristic signals corresponding to DMPO-OH (1:2:2:1), $\mathrm { D M P O { \cdot } S O _ { 4 } { \cdot } ^ { \bullet - } }$ (1:1:1:1:1:1), $\mathrm { D M P O { \cdot } { \bullet } O _ { 2 } } ^ { - }$ (1:1:1:1), and $\mathrm { T E M P \cdot } ^ { 1 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ adduct (1:1:1) adducts were clearly observed (Figure 4BD), confirming the generation of multiple ROS. Radical generation commenced within the initial $_ { 1 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ } }$ ,correlating with rapid ATZ degradation observed at the onset. # 2.5.1. Formation of $M n O _ { 2 }$ Based Coagulants

To elucidate the pathway of self-induced coagulation, control experiments were conducted using catalysts with systematically varied compositions. Among six different five-element HEMs tested in ATZ solutions containing $0 . 6 \mathrm { \ m m }$ PMS (Appendix, Figure S19, Supporting Information), reddish-brown particulate matter formed only in systems containing both Mn and La. The $\mathrm { M n } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { P M S }$ system produced sand-like reddish-brown precipitates via Equation (16), confirming the essential role of Mn in coagulation. In contrast, the $\mathrm { L } \mathsf { a } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { P M S }$ system remained transparent, indicating that La alone does not initiate floc formation. Instead, La stabilizes the R-3c phase through A-site incorporation, promoting the dual-phase structure necessary for coagulation. In the trimetallic/PMS system, the addition of $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + }$ significantly altered particle morphology, changing it to a flocculent structure and markedly increasing particle size (Appendix Figure S20A, Supporting Information). Compared with $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathsf { A l } ^ { 3 + }$ additions, $\mathrm { C o } ^ { 2 + }$ also notably reduced particle adhesion to the beaker wall, facilitating better floc growth and enhancing the adsorption and removal of dissolved impurities. Conversely, $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + }$ addition completely suppressed floc formation, likely due to: (I) $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + }$ competitive consumption of PMS via Equation (17); (II) complexation with Mn ions inhibiting $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 }$ formation; (III) catalytic reduction of $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 }$ , maintaining Mn in the dissolved $\mathrm { M n } ^ { 2 + }$ state. SEM analysis of 900CoCuMnAlLaCr covered with coagulants formed under different pH conditions (Figure 4GI) revealed that the overall morphology remained largely consistent with the pre-reaction structure, despite ongoing PMS activation and adsorptioncoagulation of intermediates. # 2.5.3. Coagulation Performance

To assess the aggregation degree of coagulants under different water matrix conditions, we determined Zeta potential of each reaction solution filtered through a $0 . 2 2 ~ \mu \mathrm { m }$ PVDF membrane. Zeta potential of untreated ATZ solution without any treatment was $\approx - 4 . 5 3 \mathrm { e V } ,$ reflecting a net negative surface charge resulting from deprotonation of amino groups. Under neutral and acidic conditions, the absolute value of the Zeta potential was relatively low, which promotes floc growth and aligns with previous experimental observations.

The isoelectric point of the particles in the ATZ solution was measured at a $\mathsf { p H }$ of ${ \approx } 7$ , suggesting an initially positive catalyst surface. Following the reaction, the $\mathrm { p H }$ was generally ${ \approx } 3$ while the measured Zeta potential was predominantly negative. It is noteworthy that in the $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ -containing system, where no floc formation occurred (Appendix Figure S32, Supporting Information), the concentration of residual sulfate ions reached $2 6 9 \mathrm { m g / L }$ (Appendix Figure S34, Supporting Information). This stands in marked contrast to the floc-forming system, in which only 14.41 $\mathrm { m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ (Appendix Figure S16, Supporting Information) of sulfate remained—a value well below the $2 5 0 \mathrm { \ m g \ L ^ { - 1 } }$ permissible limit set by the Chinese National Standard (GB 3838-2002) for drinking water.[57] This pronounced difference serves as direct evidence of the effective adsorption and removal of PMS decomposition products by the $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 }$ -based coagulants, underscoring their strong affinity for PMS-derived species and organic intermediates. Additionally, in experiments with anions (Appendix Figure S32, Supporting Information), the $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { - 2 }$ group, exhibiting a lower Zeta potential, was capable of forming coagulants, consistent with the observed experimental phenomena.

To further evaluate the removal effectiveness of $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 }$ floc formation in removing organic compounds, we measured the total organic carbon (TOC) before and after the reaction (Figure 4F). The contribution of coagulation was quantified by terminating the oxidation at $1 \mathrm { { h } }$ ( $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ adsorption $+ 3 0$ min oxidation) with an overdose of sodium sulfite, followed by TOC measurement immediately after quenching and again after $2 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ of floc settlement. The difference, representing the coagulation-driven removal, accounted for $1 9 . 4 5 \%$ of TOC reduction (Appendix Figure S33, Supporting Information), confirming its significant role. In floc-forming systems (pH 3, pH 7, $\mathrm { S O _ { 4 } } ^ { - 2 }$ ), the TOC removal efficiency exceeded $7 0 \%$ .By contrast, in the $\mathrm { H P O _ { 4 } } ^ { - 2 }$ group, the high Zeta potential prevented the floc formation. The absence of these flocs removed a key platform for the enrichment and subsequent oxidation of reaction intermediates, resulting in only $2 8 . 1 \%$ TOC removal despite the degradation curve of ATZ remaining largely unaffected. In the homogeneous catalytic system, well-formed flocs were observed, yet the overall TOC removal remained low $( 2 2 . 1 \% )$ , which we attribute to limited catalytic activation efficiency and competitive consumption of metal ions during the process. Conversely, in the system containing $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ , an exceptionally high TOC removal of $9 9 . 1 \%$ was achieved, which correlated with the ATZ degradation efficiency. The absence of flocs in this case highlights the exceptional ability of 900CoCuMnAlLaCr/PMS system to activate $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ and effectively mineralize ATZ. The primary removal mechanism in floc-forming systems is sweep coagulation and physical entrapment: the developing $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 }$ -based flocs form an extensive 3D network that enmeshes organic pollutants, colloidal particles, and reaction intermediates as they grow and settle. Unlike conventional Al/Fe-based coagulants, this $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 }$ -based system remains effective under acidic conditions $\mathrm { ( p H < 7 ) }$ . In summary, the enhanced removal performance stems from a multi-metal enhanced coagulation process in which $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ flocs form in situ and efficiently sequester pollutants.

# 3. Conclusion

This study overcomes the inherently weak catalytic activity of HEM by developing a novel dual-phase HEM, CoCuMnAlLaCr, which significantly enhances its Fenton-like catalytic performance. By tuning the calcination temperature to control the phase composition, a new R-3c spatial structure was obtained from the single FCC phase. Experimental characterization confirmed the presence of point defects and stacking faults near the phase interfaces, with numerous defects observed within the complex phase due to stress concentration and lattice distortion. Phase-regulated interfacial polarization promotes diverse ROS generation, enabling effective ATZ oxidation and its removal through degradation without interference from pH or commonly inorganic/organic compounds in wastewater. Manganese doping triggered a $\mathrm { M n } ^ { 2 + }$ oxidation mechanism, leading to $\mathrm { M n O } _ { 2 }$ precipitation and the formation of an oxidation-coagulation system. This system not only recycles low-leaching metal ions due to the stable high-entropy structure but also facilitates the removal of ATZ degradation products via condensed flocculation, ultimately controlling metal ions and preventing secondary pollution. Furthermore, the powder particles dispersed in the solution readily combine with the HEM, simplifying separation. The excellent Fenton-like catalytic activity and self-induced coagulation of dualphase HEM highlight its potential in Fenton-like systems, offering valuable insights for designing multi-phase HEM and their applications in environmental remediation.
