# A B S T R A C T

A novel ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ nanocatalyst functionalized $\mathbf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 3 }$ ceramic membrane (CoFCM) with a honeycomb structure was prepared via an optimized surface-nucleated zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF-67) growth method. The $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ hollow structure which was confirmed via chemical characterizations (XRD, FTIR, Raman and XPS), was formed on the membrane surface by one-step calcination of ZIF-67 membrane. The CoFCM was characterized by various techniques including the field emission scanning electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy. The results revealed the formation of well-defined $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ layer on the porous $\mathbf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 3 }$ ceramic membrane with the thickness of $1 . 5 \mathrm { - } 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ . The growth mechanism of CoFCM was further proposed via XPS and the catalytic activity of CoFCM was investigated for the removal of sulfamethoxazole (SMX) with the addition of Oxone. The membrane performance was examined under a home-made dead-end mode. Results indicated that CoFCM has a rougher surface with an initial membrane resistance of $1 . 1 9 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 1 } \mathrm { m } ^ { - 1 }$ and performs outstanding catalytic activity. The pure water permeability of CoFCM is $3 0 2 4 \mathrm { L m } ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { b a r } ^ { - 1 }$ , which is comparable to that of the pristine ceramic membrane $\ l < 1 0 \%$ difference). The SMX removal efficiency achieved to $> 9 0 \%$ in $9 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ with an Oxone addition of $0 . 1 { \ g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ . Meanwhile, the membrane showed excellent durability by retaining $> 9 5 \%$ of initial flux for at least 3 operational cycles with a low cobalt ion leaching via Oxone-assisted cleaning. Furthermore, the reaction mechanism of Oxone activation by CoFCM was proposed from the results of the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and radical scavenger experiments.

# 1. Introduction

In recent years, ceramic membrane is gaining increasing acceptance for treatment of municipal and industrial wastewater streams because of its high stability and permeability in extreme environments [1,2]. In view of these properties, the ceramic membrane offers a potential novel application as a hybrid filtration and catalytic system. In this hybrid system, much faster conversion of reactants can be achieved due to the unlimited mass transfer process [3]. Over the recent decades, the coupling of ceramic membranes with oxidation has proven to be an effective technology to enhance pollutants treatment performance by generating reactive oxygen species and preventing membrane fouling [4–6]. Up to now, the oxidation-filtration coupling technologies are mainly limited to photocatalysis or ozonation to generate hydroxyl radicals for pollutant degradation [4,7–9].

Compared with hydroxyl radical-based advanced oxidation technologies, sulfate radical $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { . - } )$ -based advanced oxidation processes (SR-AOPs) have gained increasing attention due to its properties of higher oxidation potential, wider pH range (2–8) and longer half-life time $( 3 0 - 4 0 \mu s )$ [10]. The $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \cdot - }$ is generally produced from peroxymonosulfate (PMS, Oxone) and persulfate (PS) via transition metals or metal oxides [11–13]. Among them, cobalt-based nanoparticles exhibit excellent catalytic activity. To date, lots of work have been done to prepare high performance Co-based nanomaterials for PMS activation [14–17]. However, nanoparticles tend to aggregate, leading to loss of catalytic activity [18]. Furthermore, surfaces of the nanocatalysts could be covered by deposited solute species including pollutants and their degradation products, leading to deactivation of active sites on their surface [19]. To address these limitations, one possible way is to load nanocatalysts onto membrane surface to increase the surface area as well as decrease the aggregation.

To prepare a catalyst functionalized membrane, dip-coating and hydrothermal methods have been investigated [20,21]. However, these methods need multiple steps and the morphologies are very difficult to control. Herein, we present a facile and scalable method to synthesize a magnetic hollow cobalt oxide functionalized ceramic membrane (CoFCM) for sulfamethoxazole (SMX) degradation. SMX is one kind of widely used antibiotics and has been detected in various systems [22–25]. Meanwhile, it is hard to be removed by the traditional methods because of its resistance to natural biodegradation [26,27]. To address this problem, one potential strategy is the advanced oxidation processes owing to their non-selectivity to a wide variety of organic pollutants via generation of strong reactive species.

In this work, a Co-based zeolitic imidazolate framework, ZIF-67, was formed on the ceramic membrane surface via surface-nucleated growth. The hollow CoFCM structure could be achieved easily through direct calcination of ZIF-67 by taking advantages of its thermal behavior and chemical reactivity [16,28]. To our knowledge, this is the first report on synthesis of CoFCM via ZIF-67 template method and use it for PMS activation. The performance of the resulting CoFCM was examined in a home-made dead-end membrane filtration system. Also, the plausible PMS activation mechanism in CoFCM/Oxone system was evaluated via the radical trapping and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) experiments.

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Materials

All chemicals were of reagent grade or higher and were used without further purification. Sulfamethoxazole (SMX), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), methanol $\mathrm { ( C H _ { 4 } O ) }$ , tert-butanol $\mathrm { ( C _ { 4 } H _ { 1 0 } O ) }$ , powder $\mathrm { { A l } } _ { 2 } \mathrm { { O } } _ { 3 }$ (100 mesh, $9 9 \%$ ), cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate $( \mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } )$ and 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Singapore. Oxone (PMS, $2 \mathrm { K H } S \mathrm { O } _ { 5 } { \cdot } \mathrm { K H } S \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } { \cdot } \mathrm { K } _ { 2 } S \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } )$ was supplied by Alfa Aesar, Singapore. 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrrolidine N-oxide (DMPO) was provided by Aladdin, China. Porous $\mathrm { { A l } } _ { 2 } \mathrm { { O } } _ { 3 }$ ceramic membrane (CM) with a pore size of $5 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ and porosity of around $5 5 \%$ was provided by Nanjing Shuyihui Scientific Instruments CO., LTD (Nanjing, China). The diameter of the ceramic membrane is $2 2 \mathrm { m m }$ and the thickness is $2 \mathrm { m m }$ .

# 2.2. Preparation of $C o _ { 3 } O _ { 4 }$ functionalized ceramic membrane (CoFCM)

Before the CoFCM preparation, the synthesis of zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF-67) was optimized via changing the precursor concentrations $3 3 \mathrm { m M } { - } 3 3 0 \mathrm { m M }$ based on Co concentration). To prepare CoFCM, the ceramic membranes were firstly washed with $1 \mathrm { M N a O H }$ to remove the impurities followed by washing with DI water to neutral pH and drying at $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ for later use. Surface-nucleated heterogeneous growth method was used for the synthesis of porous hybrid CoFCM and the schematic illustration is shown in Fig. 1. First, the ceramic disc was immersed in the 2-MIM (2-methylimidazole) solution (Solution A, $_ { 0 . 6 6 g }$ of 2-MIM in $1 5 \mathrm { m L }$ methanol) for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , after washing several times with methanol to remove the redundant solution, the disc was placed into the metal precursor solution (Solution B, $0 . 2 9 1 \ g$ of $\mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ in $1 5 \mathrm { m L }$ methanol) for another $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . After washing with methanol, the disc was positioned into the mixture of two solutions (Solution A and Solution B) which was settled at room temperature in the quiescent solution. After aging for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ , the purple disc was washed with methanol to remove the reductant solution followed by washing with DI water and dried overnight at $1 0 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . The remaining purple ZIF-67 powders were collected from the mother solution and washed with methanol and DI water several times with the assistance of centrifuge for later test. To confirm the formation of covalent bonds between ceramic disc and ZIF-67, a control experiment in which the disc was replaced by $\mathrm { { A l } } _ { 2 } \mathrm { { O } } _ { 3 }$ powder was conducted in the same condition. In the surface-nucleated heterogeneous growth process, Al could be linked with 2-MIM via Al-N bond followed by the formation of ZIF67 on the membrane surface, which would minimize the infiltration of ZIF-67 particles into the internal pores. The infiltration of ZIF-67 particles into the internal pores will result in undesirable pore plugging and decreased water permeability.

The CoFCM was then prepared through a one-step calcination of ZIF-67 functionalized membrane in a furnace with the temperature raising from room temperature to the desired temperature at a ramping rate of $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ , and then stabilized for $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ . The resulted CoFCM was denoted as M-temp while the pristine membrane without ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ was referred as M0. In addition, $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ powder was also prepared under the same condition using ZIF-67 as the precursor and was denoted as ZIF$6 7 @$ temp in the following sections. After the calcination process, all membranes were washed with DI water followed by drying in an oven at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ overnight for later use.

# 2.3. Characterization techniques

The thermo-transformation of ZIF-67 was investigated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (SDT-Q600, TA instrument, USA) in the temperature range of $1 0 0 { - } 9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ under air atmosphere (heating rate of $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and air flow of $1 0 0 \mathrm { m L m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption analyses were conducted on a Quantachrome Autosorb-1 Analyzer at liquid $\Nu _ { 2 }$ temperature of 77 K. The resulting specific surface areas as well as porosities were calculated from $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption data by the multipoint Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) and DFT methods.

The surface morphologies as well as elements distribution were acquired by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, JSM-7600F, JEOL). Meanwhile, atomic force microscopy (AFM, Cypher S, Asylum Research) was employed to characterize the membrane surface roughness.

X-rays diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) was used to analyze the crystal structures of the material. The 2θ range was scanned from 5 to $7 5 ^ { \circ }$ at a step size of 0.02 and the crystallite sizes of as-synthesized particles were calculated using Scherrer’s equation:

$$
D = \mathbb { R } \lambda / \beta \mathrm { c o s } \theta
$$

where D is the estimated diameter of the particles (nm), R is 0.89 (Scherrer’s constant), $\lambda$ means the incident wavelength, which is $0 . 1 5 4 \mathrm { n m }$ in this study, $\beta$ (rad) is the peak width at half height and θ (°) is the diffraction angle.

The vibrational absorbance of as-synthesized particles was investigated by Fourier transformed infra-red spectrometer (FTIR, Perkin Elmer) and Raman spectrometer (HORIBA Scientific). Meanwhile, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Kratos Axis Supra, Shimadzu) was conducted on to detect the elements’ chemical states in ZIF-67 and ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ .

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) experiments were employed on a Biospin ELEXSYS II E500 EPR spectrometer (Bruker) using DMPO as spin trapping agent to probe the free reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the information was analyzed by Xeon software (Bruker). Furthermore, chemical quenching experiments were conducted by adding methanol and tert-butanol as the scavengers due to their different reaction rates to hydroxyl radical and sulfate radical.

# 2.4. SMX degradation experiments with CoFCM

SMX degradation with CoFCM was evaluated in a dead-end filtration mode, which was operated under a constant flux (Fig. S1). The initial pH of the feed solution $\left( 5 0 \mathrm { m L } \right)$ is 5 with a SMX concentration of $1 0 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ . A high-pressure metering pump (5979 Optos Pump 2HM, Eldex) was used to feed the solution into the membrane cell module (SS316 stainless steel), in which the membrane was secured tightly. The constant flow mode (flow rate was set at $1 \mathrm { m L m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ) was used and trans-membrane pressure (TMP) was recorded as 0.07 bar in a dead-end system and the permeate was then re-circulated back to the feed tank. A control experiment with the pristine $\mathbf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 3 }$ membrane (M0) was also performed to quantify the performance of membrane. The membranes (M0 and CoFCMs) were compacted to a steady state for ${ \mathsf { 5 } } \operatorname* { m i n }$ before filtration experiment.

# 2.5. Analytical methods

The SMX concentration was quantified using LC-2030C-3D liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu) with a reversed phase column (Inertsil ODS-3, $5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ , $4 . 6 \times 1 5 0 \mathrm { m m } )$ . The detection wavelength was set at $2 6 4 \mathrm { n m }$ and the mobile phases are $6 0 \%$ methanol and $4 0 \%$ water $_ { ( 0 . 1 \% }$ acetic acid). The flow rate was $0 . 8 \mathrm { m L } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ and the injection volume was $1 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ . $$
\begin{array} { r l } & { 2 \mathrm { I } ^ { - } + \mathrm { H S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - } + 2 \mathrm { H } ^ { + }  \mathrm { H S O } _ { 4 } ^ { - } + \mathrm { I } _ { 2 } + \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } } \\ & { } \\ & { \mathrm { I } _ { 2 } + \mathrm { I } ^ { - }  \mathrm { I } _ { 3 } ^ { - } } \end{array}

The concentration of ${ \mathrm { I } } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ was then determined by a UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) at $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = 3 5 2 \mathrm { n m }$ (Fig. S2). # 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Study of the ZIF-67-template method

In this study, the effect of different precursor concentrations on ZIF67 synthesis was investigated and the results are shown in Fig. 2. The optimum mole ratio of $\mathsf { C o } ^ { 2 + }$ to 2-MIM, which is 1:8, is important for the successful synthesis of ZIF-67 [29]. The concentrations of precursors would affect both morphology and yield of ZIF-67. With increasing precursor concentration, the well-defined ZIF-67 particle size increased with a higher yield and subsequently became more irregular due to the presence of excessive precursors in solution. In this work, the $\mathrm { C o } ( \mathrm { N O } _ { 3 } ) _ { 2 } { \cdot } 6 \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ concentration of $3 3 \mathrm { m M }$ was chosen because it shows the most uniform particle distribution (Fig. 2b).

To examine the calcination temperature effect on the formation of ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ , different temperatures (350, 450 and $5 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ were used in the calcination process. The sizes and morphologies of as-synthesized ZIF67 and ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ calcined at different temperatures were observed by FESEM (Fig. 3). The results show that all particles perform good uniformity in this study (Fig. 3a–c). However, when the temperature increased to ${ 5 5 0 } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , the ZIF-67 crystals aggregated and the structural backbone of the metal–organic framework disappeared (Fig. 3d). A magnified FESEM image in Fig. 3a depicts the very smooth surface over the whole particle, indicating the single-crystal-like feature of the ZIF67. It displays a uniform rhombic dodecahedra structure due to the combination of Co ions and 2-methylimidazole through coordination bonds and shows a uniform particle size of approximately $2 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ with sodalite topology. After calcination at $3 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 3 \mathrm { h } }$ , the pristine polyhedral morphology is retained well, but the size of polyhedral crystals shrinks slightly (Fig. 3b). When the calcination temperature increased to $4 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , part of the pristine polyhedral morphology is still retained and the size of polyhedral shrinks a bit more (Fig. 3c). Meanwhile, the surface of $2 1 1 - 6 7 @ 4 5 0$ becomes rougher after calcination, indicating the formation of inter-particle pores during the calcination process, leading to the increase in average pore radius (Table 1) [30]. Once ZIF-67 was calcined to form ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ , its original sodalite structure deformed through concaving and shrinking due to the heat treatment. However, some edges of ZIF-67, even though skewed, can still be discerned. The morphology changes during calcination should be caused by the different rates of crystallization and specific plane growth under different calcination temperatures (Fig. S3a). The particle sizes observed in FESEM were slightly larger than that of those estimated from Scherrer’s equation (Table 1).

Fig. 4 shows ZIF-67 exhibits a gradual weight loss step of $4 . 5 \%$ up to $3 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ corresponding to the removal of guest molecule from the cavities and some unreacted reagents. A drastic weight loss of $6 5 . 7 \%$ is located in the temperature range of $3 5 0 { \mathrm { - } } 4 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , indicating the decomposition of organic ligands in ZIF-67. Meanwhile, the sample calcined at $3 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ still contains some carbon (Fig. S3b), showing the incomplete transformation. The presence of carbon in the sample is undesirable as it can compete with the pollutant for reaction with ROS during catalytic oxidation process. As the temperature further increased, a long plateau was obtained at the temperature range of $4 0 0 { \mathrm { - } } 9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with a residual weight of $3 4 . 3 \%$ , due to the formation of ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ . The results are consistent with the calculated weight loss from ${ \mathrm { C o } } ( { \mathrm { C } } _ { 4 } { \mathrm { H } } _ { 5 } { \mathrm { N } } _ { 2 } ) _ { 2 }$ (molecular weight of 220) to $\mathrm { C o O } _ { 4 / 3 }$ (molecular weight of 80). To ensure complete transformation form ZIF-67 to ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ as well as to conserve the pristine polyhedral morphology, $4 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ was chosen as the calcination temperature for the following studies and the ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ prepared at this condition was denoted as $2 1 1 - 6 7 @ 4 5 0$ .

The existence of ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ was also verified by spectroscopic analyses. As reported previously, the ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ with $\mathbf { C o } ^ { 2 + } ~ ( 3 \mathbf { d } ^ { 7 } )$ and ${ \bf C o } ^ { 3 + } ~ ( 3 { \bf d } ^ { 6 } )$ located at tetrahedral and octahedral sites respectively [32]. The active modes for spinal crystallizes can be predicted via the space group theory:

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { \Gamma = \mathrm { A _ { 1 g } } ( \mathrm { R } ) + \mathrm { E _ { g } } ( \mathrm { R } ) + \mathrm { F _ { 1 g } } ( \mathrm { I N } ) + 3 \mathrm { F _ { 2 g } } ( \mathrm { R } ) + 2 \mathrm { A _ { 2 u } } ( \mathrm { I N } ) + 2 \mathrm { E _ { u } } ( \mathrm { I N } ) } \\ & { \qquad + 4 \mathrm { F _ { 1 u } } ( \mathrm { I R } ) + 2 \mathrm { F _ { 2 u } } ( \mathrm { I N } ) } \end{array}
$$

where (R) is Raman active vibrations, (IR) means infrared-active vibrations and (IN) represents inactive modes.

Fig. 6c shows the Raman spectra of the ZIF-67 and $Z _ { \mathrm { I F - } } 6 7 @ 4 5 0$ . Four active Raman modes with the location at ∼470, 510, 608 and $6 8 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ could be distinguished, corresponding to $\mathrm { E _ { g } , F _ { 2 g } } ^ { 1 } , \mathrm { F _ { 2 g } } ^ { 2 }$ and $\mathbf { A } _ { 1 g }$ modes of $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ . All peaks are in agreement with the values of pure ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ spinel structure yet with an average shift of $\Delta \mathrm { V } < 5 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , which could be caused by the size effects and surface stress/strain. After calcination, the peak positions shift to low wavenumbers which is attributed to the optical phonon confinement effect in nanostructures that can cause uncertainty in the phonon wave vectors and then a downshift of Raman peaks [33].

In addition, the FTIR spectrum (Fig. 6b) shows two main relatively sharp absorptions centered at $\sim 5 8 0$ and $6 6 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ in ZIF- $6 7 @ 4 5 0$ , which originate from the fingerprint stretching vibrations of the Co–O bond of the ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ spinel oxides. More precisely, the observed $5 8 0 { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { - 1 }$ band is characteristic of $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C o - O }$ with Co denoting the ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 3 + }$ in the octahedral site while the $6 6 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ band is attributable to the ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ in the tetrahedral site vibration in the spinel lattice [34]. Meanwhile, the FTIR spectrum shows no residual organic compounds after calcination process. In the original ZIF-67 sample, the absorption bands at 425 and $1 5 8 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ are assigned to the stretching vibration of $\mathrm { C o - N }$ and C–N, respectively. The bands at $1 3 8 0 { - } 1 4 5 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ are associated with the entire imidazole ring stretching and the bands in the region of $8 0 0 { - } 1 3 5 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ are for the in-plane bending of the ring while those below $8 0 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ are related to the out-of-plane bending [35].

XPS investigations were carried out to characterize the elemental composition of the surface of the particles as well as the chemical environment of the cobalt and oxygen atoms. It is reasonably surmised that N atoms would disappear along with the calcination process with oxygen (Fig. 6d). In original ZIF-67, the N1s region spectra are deconvoluted into two components N1 (pyridinic-N, $3 9 8 . 2 \mathrm { e V } )$ and N2 (Co–N, 399.1 eV) while the Co $\mathsf { 2 p }$ core-level spectra are deconvoluted to display peaks of Co-Nx at $7 8 0 . 5 \mathrm { e V }$ (Fig. S5). In this structure, appropriately arranged N1 (pyridinic N) moieties with electron-donating properties are able to trap plentiful transitional metals to serve as outstanding metal-coordination $\left( \mathbf { C o - N _ { X } } \right)$ active sites. Furthermore, free N1 atoms have excellent electronic storage ability to activate molecular oxygen under mild conditions [36]. After calcination, N1s peak disappears due to 2-methylimidazole ligands ring opening and conversion of ZIF-67 to ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ (Figs. 6d and S5). The $\mathtt { C o 2 p }$ peaks show a slight broadening and shifting to lower binding energy, which could be deconvoluted to peaks related to the formed cobalt oxides (Fig. 7a).

In the Co2p core-level spectrum of $Z _ { Ḋ } \mathrm { I F } { - } 6 7 @ { 4 5 0 }$ , two major peaks with binding energies of 780 and $7 9 5 \mathrm { e V }$ are observed, corresponding to Co $\boldsymbol { 2 } \mathsf { p } _ { 3 / 2 }$ and Co $\boldsymbol { 2 } \mathrm { p } _ { 1 / 2 }$ respectively (Fig. 7a). The $1 5 \mathrm { e V }$ energy difference between the Co $\mathsf { 2 p }$ peaks is indicative of spinel ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ [37]. It is well known that both $\mathsf { C o } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathsf { C o } ^ { 3 + }$ exhibit similar binding energy peaks, however, the energy splitting (ΔE) between the two levels due to spin-orbit coupling should be different for the $\mathsf { C o } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathsf { C o } ^ { 3 + }$ configurations with $\Delta \mathrm { E } = 1 5 . 0 \mathrm { e V }$ for ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 3 + }$ and $\Delta \mathrm { E } = 1 5 . 7 \mathrm { e V }$ for ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ [38]. The XPS data (Fig. 7a) for the spinel ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 } .$ , which contains both ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ and ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 3 + }$ ions distributed on the A and the B sites as $[ \mathsf { C o } ^ { 2 + } ] \mathsf { A } [ 2 \mathsf { C o } ^ { 3 + } ]$ $\mathrm { { B O } _ { 4 } }$ , clearly show the presence of doublet at $\mathrm { D 1 } = 7 7 9 . 2 \mathrm { e V }$ and $\mathrm { D 2 } = 7 8 0 . 8 \mathrm { e V }$ for the $\mathrm { C o } 2 \mathrm { p } _ { 3 / 2 }$ level and a doublet for the $\mathrm { C o 2 p _ { 1 / 2 } }$ level centered at $\mathrm { D 3 } = 7 9 4 . 1 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and $\mathrm { D } 4 = 7 9 6 . 5 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ . The separations between the doublet peaks are $\Delta \mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { D 3 - D 1 } } = 1 4 . 9 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ and $\Delta \mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { D 4 - D 2 } } = 1 5 . 7 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , which are close to the above-mentioned values for ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 3 + }$ and ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ , respectively, thus confirming the presence of ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 3 + }$ and ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ in ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ . The peaks at 789.3 and $8 0 4 . 9 \mathrm { e V }$ are considered shakeup satellite peaks, which further confirms the well-defined ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ structure [39]. Other small peaks at 783 and $7 9 5 \mathrm { e V }$ may result from a chemical shift of the main spin-orbit components because of the chemical interaction between cobalt and hydroxide [40].

# 3.4. Catalytic performance of CoFCMs

Fig. 10a and b show the effect of Oxone dosage on removal of SMX over M0 and M450 in the membrane filtration system. During the filtration and catalytic oxidation processes, SMX can be retained on the membrane surface, which consists of high density of ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ particle. When Oxone is introduced into the system, the ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ particle acts as active sites for Oxone activation, generating ROS for SMX degradation. Once degraded, the smaller SMX intermediates can be constantly removed from the reaction zone on the membrane surface. This promotes more efficient SMX degradation. For the pristine membrane (M0), due to inactivity of $\mathrm { { A l } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 } ,$ even when Oxone dosage was increased to $_ { 0 . 1 g }$ $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , the SMX removal in $9 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ is insignificant (less than $1 5 \%$ , Fig. 10 a), which confirmed the negligible effect of Oxone oxidation in this process. However, with the M450 membrane, it shows significantly increased removal efficiency with increase of Oxone dosage (Fig. 10 b) because of the presence of ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ on the membrane, which works as the catalyst to activate Oxone for radicals’ generation. Three ROS including HO%, $S O _ { 4 }$ %− and $S O _ { 5 }$ %− have been reported in a Co-Oxone catalytic system [43]. To verify the ROS in the CoFCM, EPR experiments were performed using DMPO as the spin trapping agent. As shown in Fig. 11a, when only Oxone solution was tested with the addition of DMPO, no peaks were detected, suggesting that no significant ROS was formed. However, in the presence of $2 1 1 - 6 7 @ 4 5 0$ , the characteristic signals of 5,5-dimethyl-2-pyrrolidone-N-oxyl (DMPOX) with heptet were detected, which was formed from the oxidation of DMPO with strong ROS (HO% and $S O _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - } )$ [44,45].

To further validate the primary ROS in the system, quenching experiments were conducted by adding methanol and tert-butanol (TBA) as the scavengers. It is widely accepted that methanol with an α-hydrogen ensures the quenching of both $S O _ { 4 }$ %− $( 2 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 7 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ and HO% $( 9 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ [46,47], while TBA without $\alpha$ -hydrogen reacts more easily with HO% $( 3 . 8 – 7 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ , but in a slower reaction rate with $S O _ { 4 }$ %− $( 4 . 0 { - } 9 . 1 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ [11]. Meanwhile, $S O _ { 5 }$ %− is relatively inert to both methanol and TBA due to its low rate constant with alcohols $( \leq 1 0 ^ { 3 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ [48]. Fig. 11b depicts the inhibition effects of different scavengers on the SMX degradation in the CoFCM/Oxone system. The degradation of SMX was inhibited entirely by addition of $^ { 1 1 }$ methanol, which indicated that the ROS generated in the system were mostly quenched. The results also confirmed the insignificant contribution of $S O _ { 5 }$ %− on the SMX decomposition. Meanwhile, $\mathbf { 1 \mathbf { M } }$ of TBA has little influence on the degradation of SMX in this system, implying that HO% plays a smaller role in the SMX decomposition compared with $S O _ { 4 } \cdot ^ { - }$ . The main degradation pathway is accompanied with the cleavage of sulfonamide bond as well as the formation of nitro-SMX molecule via the electron transfer reaction, which is in accordance with the previous study [49].

# 4. Conclusions

A novel cobalt oxide functionalized ceramic membrane (CoFCM) was successfully prepared by a facile surface-nucleated zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF-67) growth method to activate Oxone for SMX degradation in a dead-end membrane filtration mode. The method to synthesize ZIF-67 as well as ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ was optimized. The porous ${ \mathrm { C o } } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 4 }$ hollow structure was produced after one-step calcination of ZIF-67 precursor at $4 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and was distributed as a honeycomb structure on the membrane surface. The CoFCM shows a high performance on the removal of SMX as well as a high durability via Oxone-assisted cleaning. Overall, the CoFCM shows a new prospect for plentiful catalytic applications. Meanwhile, the MOF-directed surface-nucleated growth followed by one-step calcination is a promising method for fabrication of nanocatalysts functionalized ceramic membranes.
