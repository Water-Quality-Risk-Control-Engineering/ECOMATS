# . Introduction

In 1854, the English physician John Snow discovered that cholera was transmitted through the consumption of water contaminated with fecal matter [1]. This discovery led to the birth of epidemiology as a scientific discipline and marked a turning point in human development. Treating water to reduce its microbiological load allowed for a decrease in the incidence of waterborne diseases. However, unfortunately, 169 years after that discovery, around 2 billion people still use a water source for human consumption that is contaminated with feces. This poses a high risk to health and the transmission of diseases such as diarrhea, cholera, dysentery, typhoid fever, and poliomyelitis [2]. According to WHO data, microbiologically contaminated water for human consumption can transmit all these diseases and is estimated to cause 800,000 deaths from diarrhea each year [3]. In addition to the mentioned problems of safe water supply in low- and middle-income countries, over 2 billion people live in water-scarce countries, a situation that will likely worsen in some regions due to climate change and population growth [3]. However, population growth and human activities continue to put pressure on water resources, particularly in terms of consumption and pollution [4]. In this regard, wastewater regeneration is gaining prominence as a sustainable way to increase available water resources [5]. Although the consumption of reclaimed water is not allowed in most countries, the main associated risks, in addition to the biological ones caused by microorganisms, would come from arsenic, fluoride, or nitrate, as well as new contaminants such as pharmaceuticals, pesticides, perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), and/or microplastics [69].

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are water treatment technologies based on the generation of highly oxidizing reactive oxygen species (ROs) capable of removing both chemical and biological contaminants [10]. There is a wide range of available AOPs, but among the most studied and widely applied technologies are photochemical processes, which utilize UV radiation in combination with an oxidant [11]. In some cases, a catalyst may be used to accelerate the reaction rate [12, 13]. One of the most well-known and extensively studied examples within this category is the photo-Fenton process, where the reaction between hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ and iron salts (Fe(II) or Fe(III)) assisted by UV radiation produces these oxidizing species [14].

The photo-Fenton process has been extensively studied in recent decades and has shown promising performance in eliminating various types of contaminants [14]. However, it also presents certain disadvantages, such as the need to operate at acidic pH (around 3), the generation of sludge after treatment, or the requirement for a radiation source. The latter issue can be addressed by utilizing natural solar radiation, while solutions for the other challenges include the use of heterogeneous catalysts, iron-complexing agents that allow operation at neutral pH, or modifications of the Fenton process through what are known as Fenton-like processes [15,16].

Within Fenton-like processes, sometimes advanced oxidation processes based on sulfate radicals (SR-AOPs) are included, especially those that combine the use of oxidants such as persulfate (PS) and peroxymonosulfate (PMS) with homogeneous or heterogeneous metal catalysts [17]. These types of processes have gained particular relevance in recent years by addressing some of the disadvantages of the photo-Fenton process, such as the need to operate at acidic pH. These processes have already been widely reported for the removal of organic and biological contaminants, combining their use with homogeneous and heterogeneous metal catalysts [1820]. Metals such as Co, Fe, and Cu have shown notable performance in generating sulfate radicals [21, 22].

Among heterogeneous materials, the successful use of metal oxides has also been described in water treatment and the removal of specific contaminants. One advantage is their ability to be easily separated from the aqueous matrix and reused multiple times. However, one of the main drawbacks of such heterogeneous materials is the cost of synthesis. In this regard, the use of natural minerals as catalysts is a low-cost alternative that can be applied in water treatment [15,19,2326]. Natural minerals can be clasiffied into clay minerals and metallic minerals. However, the former, due to their low amount of active metals, are less interesting from a catalytic perspective, while metallic minerals can be used as efficient activators of oxidants such as $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ PS, or PMS [27]. There are numerous references to the use of metallic minerals as catalysts in the removal of contaminants of emerging concern and pathogenic microorganisms in water treatment. One of the most commonly used families of metallic minerals corresponds to iron sulfides, specifically the use of Pyrite, Pyrrhotite, or Mackinawite, which has been described in the literature in combination with oxidants, achieving yields that in most cases exceed $9 0 \%$ [2832]. In these cases, the sulfide present in the mineral plays a role as an electron donor, promoting the reduction of the oxidized metal [33]. Another family of metallic minerals includes iron oxides, with examples such as the use of Maghemite, Magnetite, Goethite, Hematite, or Siderite [3438]. However, not only iron minerals have shown this capability. bimetallic minerals such as Chalcopyrite, Bornite, Titanomagnetite, or Ilmenite also exhibit this capacity [3942]. The presence of Cu, Fe, and Ti on the mineral surface contributes to efficiently activating the mentioned oxidants, similar to what occurs with multiple-metal minerals like Tourmaline or Vanadium-titanium magnetite [39,43,44].

Atacamite $\mathrm { ( C u _ { 2 } C l ( O H ) _ { 3 } ) }$ is a copper mineral that belongs to the group of halides. It occurs as crystals or granular masses ranging in color from green to bluish-green. It is named after its abundance in the Atacama region in Chile, where it was first discovered in the 19th century in the Atacama Desert [45]. Atacamite forms in the oxidation zone of copper deposits, where copper interacts with sulfide and chloride minerals in the presence of water. It is known for its high solubility in hydrochloric acid and its ability to release copper ions in aqueous solutions. In terms of applications, atacamite does not have significant commercial use on its own. However, due to its composition of copper and chlorine, it may have potential applications in the catalysis industry, as well as in scientific research and the production of pigments and dyes. Some authors have also reported the obtainment of synthetic atacamite [4648]. The use of atacamite has been reported in various research studies as a catalyst or adsorbent, and some authors have even reported its antifungal properties. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no references in the literature reporting its use as a catalyst in photo-assisted AOPs for the generation of oxidant species such as hydroxyl or sulfate radicals.

The main objective of this work is to evaluate the atacamite mineral $\mathrm { ( C u _ { 2 } C l ( O H ) _ { 3 } ) }$ as a potential catalyst in different photo-assisted advanced oxidation processes using solar radiation, through the application of different oxidants such as $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ , PS, and PMS, on the inactivation of Enterococcus faecalis in treated wastewater samples. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that the use of atacamite as a catalyst in different AOPs is reported. Additionally, the mechanisms taking place in the disinfection process are qualitatively studied.

# 2.4. Microbial methods

Simulated wastewater was deliberately inoculated with Enterococcus faecalis to achieve an initial concentration that mirrors that of authentic water samples, specifically ${ 1 0 } ^ { 6 }$ CFU/mL. To accomplish this, a commercially available strain of Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC® 29212TM) was procured in the form of MiStraCon®Swabs2 microorganism pellets (Scharlau). Cryovials containing the commercial strain of E. faecalis, stored in a freezer, were individually cultured in $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of Luria Bertani broth (Scharlau; Spain) and incubated for $2 4 \mathrm { h }$ at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to ensure the bacterial population reached the stationary phase. Subsequently, the contents of the Falcon tube were subjected to centrifugation for $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $4 5 0 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ , the supernatant was discarded, and the bacteria were re-suspended in a sterile saline solution at pH 7 (NaCl $0 . 9 \%$ to achieve a concentration of approximately $1 0 ^ { 9 } \mathrm { C F U / m L }$ . Bacterial colony counting was performed utilizing a methodology involving the assessment of $1 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ drops of three consecutive 10-fold dilutions in sterile $0 . 9 \%$ NaCl, which were plated on Petri dishes. In order to enhance the detection limit, seeding beads were employed to evenly spread $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ of the undiluted sample (according to Standard Method 9125 C). # 2.5. Disinfection reaction

The disinfection reactions were conducted within batch reactors, employing $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m L }$ of simulated wastewater that was deliberately inoculated with Enterococcus faecalis. Agitation of the reactions was accomplished by means of magnetic stirrers (LLG- Labware, Germany). The commencement of the reaction was initiated by the addition of atacamite at varying loads (ranging from 0 to ${ } ^ { 1 } \ g / \mathrm { L } )$ and subsequently the oxidant $\mathrm { ( H _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } }$ PS or PMS) by incorporating diverse concentrations (ranging from 0 to $1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M } )$ . Artificial solar radiation, essential for the experimental setup, was provided by the SUNTEST CPS solar chamber (Heraeus, Germany), which was equipped with a potent 1500 W Xenon lamp.

Where, P is the rated power (kW), t is the reaction time (h), V is volume $( \mathbf { m } ^ { 3 } )$ of reaction, Ci and Cf the initial and final concentration. The energy calculations were based on 5-log units of Enterococcus faecalis removal.

# 3.1. Characterization of atacamite

Although the material was purchased as a commercial pigment, a full characterization of it was performed. Fig. 1A shows the X-ray diffraction pattern of commercial atacamite. As it can be seen in the figure, the pattern of the atacamite sample fits perfectly the reference pattern (ICSD collection code 61252), without any significant unidentified peak, revealing that orthorhombic $\mathrm { C u _ { 2 } C l ( O H ) _ { 3 } }$ (space group Pnma) is the only crystalline phase present in the sample within the detection limit of XRD. The UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectrum showed in Fig. 1B, reveals a semiconductor-like absorption profile from which and indirect band gap of ca. $2 . 6 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ can be estimated by means of a Tauc plot, in accordance with previous literature [53]. In addition, a discrete absorption band expanding from 550 to $> 9 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ is observed, which can be related to d-d transitions in $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ ions [54] and gives the mineral its characteristic green color.

Additionally, solar radiation promotes a series of other mechanisms, such as the generation of radicals by breaking the peroxo bond in PMS, the photoreduction of the metal, or those associated with the mineral itself with photocatalytic capacity [58].

There are no similar studies in the literature where atacamite is used as a catalyst in Fenton-like processes, making comparison difficult to carry out. The closest work to what is proposed in this study has been published by Saputra et al. [62]. In their work, Saputra et al. synthesized a copper-modified covalent triazine framework through the ionothermal method. The XRD pattern of the synthesized catalyst exhibited characteristic peaks in accordance with a mixture of two phases of monoclinic clinoatacamite (JCPDS $\# 5 0 \mathrm { - } 1 5 5 9 )$ and atacamite (JCPDS $\# 2 5 \substack { - 0 2 6 9 }$ of copper trihydroxychlorides $\mathrm { ( C u _ { 2 } C l ( O H ) _ { 3 } ) }$ polymorphs. The catalyst with a loading of $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { L }$ was tested in combination with $2 \mathrm { m M }$ of PMS and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ in the presence of solar radiation for the removal of methylene blue dye at a concentration of $3 0 \ \mathrm { p p m }$ Significant improvement was observed compared to the process using only solar radiation.

Therefore, in the absence of references regarding the combined use of atacamite and PMS, other studies focusing on the utilization of homogeneous and heterogeneous Cu catalysts are taken as reference. For instance, Lu et al. [63]described the elimination of antibiotic-resistant bacteria through the $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ assisted peroxymonosulfate process for pool water disinfection. The authors stated that for pool waters containing a Cu ion concentration of $0 . 2 \mathrm { m g / L }$ , the addition of $1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { M }$ of PMS achieved a $4 . 7 \mathrm { - } 1 0 \mathrm { g }$ reduction in antibiotic-resistant bacteria in $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . Melo Costa-Serge et al. [64] described the activation of PMS, PS, and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ using a heterogeneous Cu-based catalyst (CuMgFe-I $\ l { 3 } ( \mathrm { O H } ) _ { 4 } ,$ to achieve simultaneous removal of antibiotics and fecal bacteria in wastewater samples. While the authors reported success in eliminating sulfamethoxazole (SMx) and Escherichia coli, they were unable to eliminate the population of Enterococcus sp.

# 3.4.Qualitative identification of radicals involved in treatments

In this section the qualitative identification of the different species involved is described in the combined system oxidant/atacamite/solar radiation. Firstly, the concentration of free chlorine in the reaction is determined. Previous studies have demonstrated that PMS and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ can react with chloride generating free chlorine in the medium, and it is important to notice that atacamite has chlorine in its composition. This is especially significant in the case of PMS, which has been reported to react with chlorine-producing $\mathrm { { H C l O } / C l _ { 2 } }$ species (Eqs. (8) and (9)) [68], responsible for bacterial inactivation.

$$
\begin{array} { l } { { C l ^ { - } + H S O _ { 5 } ^ { - } {  } H C l O + S O _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - } } } \\ { { \ } } \\ { { C l ^ { - } + H ^ { + } + H C l O {  } H _ { 2 } O + C l _ { 2 } } } \end{array}
$$

Fig. S2 (Supplementary Material) graphs the evolution of free chlorine concentration in the treatments combining oxidants $( 0 . 3 \mathrm { m M } )$ and atacamite $\mathrm { \Omega } { 5 0 } \mathrm { m g } / \mathrm { L } )$ in presence and absence of solar radiation. As it can be observed, all the treatments generated free chlorine, but there is a great difference when PMS was used, especially in presence of solar radiation, reaching a maximum concentration of $0 . 4 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ under solar radiation, and $0 . 2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ in darkness. In the case of the rest of oxidants, there are no differences between treatments with and without radiation, and the concentration of free chlorine in these treatments is in the range of $0 . 1 0 { - } 0 . 1 5 \mathrm { m g / L }$ . Other authors have proved the synergistic effect that occurs when combining $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ with chloride [69]. Thus, as in the case of PMS, free chlorine species are generated as described by Eqs. 10 and 11:

$$
\begin{array} { l } { { H _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } + C l ^ { - } + H ^ { + } {  } H C l O + H _ { 2 } O } } \\ { { \ } } \\ { { H _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } + C l ^ { - } {  } C l O ^ { - } + H _ { 2 } O } } \end{array}
$$

However, the low concentration of chlorine generated when using $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ may be related to the fact that chlorinated species can have a scavenging effect on hydroxyl radicals, as shown in Eq. 12 [70]. This fact would explain the lower generation of free chlorine species when using $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ as an oxidant and the slower inactivation kinetics of Enterococcus faecalis compared to PMS. Nevertheless, it is also important to consider that radical chlorine species can be generated, which may promote the disinfection process, as shown in Eq. 13.

$$

$$

$$

Therefore, the important differences in the generation of free chlorine of PMS against other oxidants, is the one of the main factors affecting the disinfection kinetics of water, as consequence of the disinfection power of free chlorine [72].

The presence of sulfate and hydroxyl radicals and the importance of copper in the elimination of $E .$ faecalis are studied qualitatively by using different scavengers. For this purpose, as it was shown in Section 2.2,

MeOH was used for HO' and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \cdot }$ , TBA for HO', and EDTA for Cu. This study was performed over optimized treatments in presence and absence of solar radiation $( 0 . 3 \mathrm { m M }$ of oxidant and $5 0 \mathrm { m g / L }$ of atacamite), and they are depicted in Fig. 4.

Based on the results obtained in Fig. 4, it can be inferred that, for example, in the $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } O _ { 2 } } ,$ atacamite system, the hydroxyl radical is the dominant species in the process, as could not be expected otherwise. However, there are other substances that influence it to a lesser extent, such as the free chlorine that is formed, as seen in Fig. S2, and the Cu from atacamite due to its antibacterial activity [59]. Furthermore, although they are not qualitatively determined, it is expected that other minor substances with oxidizing character, such as $\mathrm { H O _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet } }$ and $\boldsymbol { 0 } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet }$ , will also appear. The result is very similar in the case of the system using PS as an oxidant. However, in that system, as seen in Fig. 4B, the sulfate radical also appears as an oxidizing species, and moreover, with a greater impact than the hydroxyl radical. This fact was reported by Rodríguez-Chueca et al. [58] with a complete mechanism for the removal of E. coli in process based on the combination of PS/Fe(II)/solar radiation. The vast majority of the reactions on that study must also take place in the system PS/Atacamite/solar radiation, just incorporating the effect on disinfection of Cu and free chlorine species.

The presence of an additional oxidizing species compared to the previous system, and furthermore, one with a high oxidizing potential, as is the case with the sulfate radical, is one of the main reasons why generally a higher efficiency of PMS is observed compared to $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ . Finally, Fig. 4C shows the results of the qualitative analysis of the species involved in the process. It can be seen from the graph that both the hydroxyl radical and the sulfate radical play a significant role in the disinfection process. This, coupled with the intrinsic oxidizing potential of PMS, the formation of $s 0 _ { 5 } ^ { - \bullet }$ in the photoreduction of copper, and finally, the free chlorine generated in the reaction between PMS and atacamite, would imply that the level of oxidizing species in the system is the highest of the three systems studied, justifying the rate of elimination of Enterococcus faecalis compared to the others. Again, RodríguezChueca et al. [58] reported a complete mechanism for E.coli removal in the system PMS/Fe(II)/solar radiation, where the differences in some oxidizing species acting in the process with the system using PS were demonstrated. Additionally, as described by other authors, it could be considered that the oxidants are capable of penetrating cell membranes and generating intracellular Fenton-type reactions that would cause additional damage to cellular components, especially DNA [73,74].

Based on the information obtained from Figs. S2 and 4, as well as the information described by other authors in the literature, the main oxidizing species involved in the elimination process of Enterococcus faecalis are summarized schematically in Fig. 5. It should be emphasized that this is a hypothesis based on a qualitative analysis of species, and that further research is clearly needed to quantitatively determine the presence of these species and their implication in the process.

# 4. Conclusions

The present study shows for the first time the application of the natural mineral atacamite as a material with photocatalytic capability that can be enhanced in combination with different oxidants for the removal of Enterococcus faecalis in wastewater. This fact makes it a novel alternative to traditional catalysts but requires further in-depth study.

Atacamite combined with solar radiation can eliminate the total population of Enterococcus faecalis within $9 0 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ , a process that occurs more rapidly with low concentration of the mineral, as high concentrations cause the dispersion of radiation, reducing the efficiency of the system. This performance is higher than that observed individually for atacamite and solar radiation, confirming the existence of photocatalytic activity and a synergistic effect between the two.

The combination of the atacamite/solar radiation system with different oxidants such as PMS, PS, and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ shows a very positive effect that demonstrates the existence of catalytic reactions between the oxidants and the mineral, leading to the generation of various oxidizing species, predominantly $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet \bullet }$ and HO.
