# . Introduction

Plastics, a synthetic polymer, has been applied widely in the packaging, construction, transportation, electronics and healthcare industries due to its low cost, light weight, strong durability, easy processability and great chemical versatility [1,2]. However, due to the mass production, single use, long life span, and slow decomposition rate, it has created a serious crisis of white pollution for the damage to sensitive ecosystems [36]. Commonly, plastic polymers are difficult to be degraded naturally in the environment (up to 20500 years), thus becoming persistent pollutants. To date, various polymers, mainly polypropylene (PP), have been widely applied to prepare commercial plastics. Plastic production has increased 20-fold in the last 50 years, with more than 690 billion tons ending up in landfills to cause environmental pollution globally [7]. The treatment and disposal of waste plastics has attracted widespread attention from the United Nations and the Chinese Government. As waste plastics are complex, their conversions to a specific product are difficult to control, and the target product yield and selectivity are low. Traditional methods require higher temperatures and longer reaction time, resulting in inferior product properties, higher costs, waste of resources and serious secondary pollution [3,8,9]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop new, versatile and profitable ways to recycle or upgrade PP while increasing the value of its end products [1012], in particular towards the circular economy and carbon reduction [13].

Noticeably, plastics have been suggested to be converted to valuable products or other value-added products (e.g., fuels and surfactants) by various technologies. In the previous works, the reaction process is difficult to control bond breaking or oxidation of the polymer. Conventional pyrolysis facilitates the conversion of plastics by overcoming thermodynamic energy barriers from polymers to small-molecule products under extreme conditions of high temperatures and pressures, or in the presence of a catalyst that lowers the reaction potential barriers. However, random bond breaking under harsh conditions results in complex product mixtures that must undergo further separation or conversion [14]. In addition, plastic polymers linked by C-C bonds are inert, and more stringent conditions are required to achieve selective conversion of plastics, which will eventually limit large-scale applications [2,15]. Obtaining products with high economic value and high selectivity under mild conditions is thus a target of upcycling plastics [16].

Interestingly, persulfate-based advanced oxidation processes (PSAOPs) can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) with high redox potential, mainly sulfate $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , $\mathrm { E } _ { 0 } { = } 2 . 5 0 { - } 3 . 1 0 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { N H E } } )$ and hydroxyl radicals (OH, $\mathrm { E } _ { 0 } { = } 1 . 8 0 { - } 2 . 7 0 \ \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { N H E } } )$ for destroying organics [17,18]. Currently, Wang et al. pioneered the use of $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ -based AOPs for the degradation of microplastics (MPs). The mineralization of polyethylene microplastics was achieved by preparing a spiral-shaped carbon nanotube catalyst $( \mathbf { M } \mathbf { n } @ \mathbf { N } \mathbf { C } \mathbf { N } \mathbf { T } s )$ doped with nitrogen and encapsulated with manganese carbide nanoparticles for persulfate activation [18]. However, conversion of plastics into environmentally friendly and valuable products based on PS-AOPs has not been achieved yet. In a heterogeneous catalytic persulfate system, the distribution of active sites on catalyst surface can be regulated to produce target ROS. Thus, the oxidation ability of the system and the deep oxidation of the plastics will be controlled, realizing the target conversion of plastics under mild conditions.

Long-chain aliphatic hydrocarbons are the main components of conventional liquid fuels. Besides, long-chain ketones $\left( \mathsf { C } _ { 5 + } \right)$ exhibit both electrophilic and nucleophilic functions, which are applied to produce infrastructure-compatible hydrocarbon fuels (e.g., jet oil and diesel), lubricants and fuel blends, or directly as specific cleaning solvents [19, 20]. The aim of this study is to search a new path for target conversion of PP to main products of hydrocarbons and ketones in a Fenton-like system by rational design of active sites on catalyst surface and efficient activation of PMS. Wood sponge with a porous framework is a well-suited substrate for filling additional functional groups and customized active sites. Bimetals interact during catalytic oxidation, favoring electron transfer and potentially a good strategy for replacing precious metals. Three-dimensional porous carbon substrates with a large surface area and good electrical conductivity can promote the diffusion of metals and provide more loading space for metal deposition. A previous study has confirmed that the introduction of $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { g }$ can induce the transfer of Cu ions to highly electron-deficient state, and thus enhance the catalytic activity [21].

Herein we rationalized the unique laminar structure and threedimensional porous framework of wood sponge to realize the concentrated loading and dispersed distribution of $\mathrm { C u } / \mathrm { M g }$ nanoparticles through in situ growth. The CuMg co-doped carbonized wood sponge catalysts (CuMgCWS) were designed for efficient PMS activation for the first time. The activation mechanism of PMS with the carbon sponge substrate and bimetallic synergy was investigated. The conversion pathways of plastics with active species were explored. The compositional changes of the products under different influencing factors were analyzed. Also, the practical application and environmental benefits were evaluated. This study provides a new strategy for the efficient conversion of waste plastics into valuable products, which might be an attractive alternative for recycling waste plastics in the future.

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Characterizations of CuMgCWS

XRD patterns of the related catalysts showed that the graphite surface was successfully loaded with metallic Cu, CuO and MgO. In addition, no obvious monoatomic Mg crystalline phase was observed, confirming that metallic Mg was highly oxidized (Fig. S1). SEM and TEM images captured the morphology and micro-structure of the samples. The carbonized wood sponge still maintained the three-dimensional layered structure, which provided sufficient sites and a good skeleton for metal loading (Fig. 1a). The cross-sectional morphology (Fig. 1b) exhibited the irregular bumps texture and lamellar structure inside the wood sponge. Therefore, the metal sites might be the active centers for accelerating PMS activation. As depicted in Fig. 1c-e, nanoparticles loaded on the sheet layer can be clearly observed, confirming the successful aggregation of the metal nanoparticles with wood sponge as the substrate.

In addition, the lattice fringe spacing of 0.18 and $0 . 2 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ appeared in the HRTEM image of CuMgCWS600 (Fig. 1f), which matched well with the (200) and (200) planes of Cu and MgO, respectively [22,23]. Thus, $\mathrm { C u } / \mathrm { M g }$ bimetals were successfully loaded on the lamellar surface of the wood sponge. The elemental composition and distribution in CuMgCWS600 were verified by EDS mapping analysis (Fig. S2). It was obvious that C and N elements were uniformly distributed on the catalyst surface. The nanostructured metal oxides were also dispersed on the catalyst surface. Reasonably, the metal nanoparticles possessed good affinity and interfacial compatibility with the wood slice layer.

The chemical states of each component were determined by XPS spectra (Fig. S3). C, N and O-related sites were identified successfully. Additionally, the XPS spectra of high-resolution $\mathtt { C u 2 p }$ showed two pairs of peaks for $\operatorname { C u } ( 0 )$ and $\operatorname { C u } ( \operatorname { I I } )$ . The characteristic peak in $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathcal { E } } \mathbf { 1 }$ s spectrum confirmed the presence of $\mathbf { M g O }$ FTIR spectra further exhibited the peaks of O-H, C-H, $\mathrm { N - C = N }$ $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = } 0$ , C-O/C-N and M-O $( \mathbf { M } = \mathbf { C } \mathbf { u }$ ,Mgbonds (Fig. S4). The peak intensities of the $\scriptstyle \mathrm { C = } 0$ , O-H and $\mathrm { N - C = N }$ groups decreased with increasing pyrolysis temperature. # 3.2. Catalytic performance of CuMgCWS/PMS

The catalytic performance of CuMgCWS catalysts was evaluated in the PMS system for PP conversion. As shown in Fig. 1g-j, the PP conversion efficiency was used as the primary indicator to evaluate the activity of the catalysts. In PMS activation system, CuMgCWS600 improved PP conversion rate by $7 . 1 ~ \%$ and $3 . 4 ~ \%$ in $1 4 \mathrm { h }$ compared to CuMgCWS400 and CuMgCWS800. CuMgCWS600 achieved a higher PMS activation and more complete PP conversion. The low specific surface area (SSA) with fewer exposed catalytic sites might lead to the mediocre catalytic performance of CuMgCWS400. Critically, catalysts prepared at different temperatures varied in the active sites, which directly affected their activity. As the preparation temperature increased, the proportion of long-chain products (carbon number larger than 20) decreased and medium-chain products $\mathrm { ( C _ { 1 1 - 2 0 } ) }$ became abundant in the CuMgCWS/PMS system (Fig. S6). Catalysts prepared at high temperature presented a large SSA and good porous structure, which was more conducive to accelerating mass transfer in the reaction process and promoting further oxidation of the sub-polymer [24,25].

Effects of catalyst concentration, PMS dosage and reaction temperature were considered to optimize the PP conversion process. The efficiency of PP conversion by activated PMS increased with increasing catalyst concentration (Fig. S7). More active sites were provided to generate oxidative species for PP oxidation. Meanwhile, a higher concentration of catalysts might be the main reason for the decomposition of long-chain products. As the PMS dosage increased, the PP conversion rate gradually increased and then decreased (Fig. S8). The increase of PMS dosage could produce more ROS. However, excessive PMS might compete with the organics for reactive radicals within the system (Eq. 3), generating $S O _ { 5 } ^ { \bullet - }$ with a low redox potential $( \mathrm { E } _ { 0 } = 1 . 1 0 \mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { N H E } } )$ [26]. Nevertheless, the content of overall long-chain products still showed a decreasing trend as the PMS dosage was increased. The large amount of ROS within the system might be the main reason for further conversion of small molecule intermediates.

In addition, hydrothermal temperature (HT) also affected the efficiency of PP conversion effectively. With an increase of HT, PP conversion efficiency increased rapidly (Fig. S9). Higher temperatures promoted softening of the polymer and facilitated the subsequent conversion. Besides, the higher temperature also favored the attack of subpolymers by ROS in the system, which further converted and broke into shorter chains with a lower molecular weight. Non-purgeable organic carbon (NPOC) values of the reacted solutions further confirmed that CuMgCWS600/PMS systems could achieve the transformation of PP to organic compounds rather than simple mineralization (Figs. S7-9). Thus, metal-carbon catalyzed activation of PMS under hydrothermal conditions for the release of highly active ROS and PP conversion. Conversion of PP in the presence of ROS produced organic intermediates. The catalytic stability of CuMgCWS600 in activating PMS was then explored by recycling tests. After 3 cycles, CuMgCWS600 still maintained the catalytic activity with PP conversion rate of about $7 0 \%$ (Fig. S10, slightly decreased by $9 . 1 ~ \%$ ). The CuMgCWS600 catalyst showed high stability for reuse.

# 3.3.Converted products of polypropylene

The performance of the CuMgCWS600/PMS system for PP conversion was evaluated by analyzing the conversion efficiency, liquid products and polymeric solid residue. The conversion efficiency of PP increased with extended reaction time. The gradual increase of NPOC in solution also confirmed the elevated degree of PP conversion to organic products under metal-carbon catalysis and hydrothermal conditions (Fig. 2a and b). In addition, GC-MS was used to identify the liquid products after the reactions (Fig. S11).

Clearly, the proportion of hydrocarbons was high at the beginning of the reaction. The initial conversion of the macromolecular polymer was mainly occurring through the breaking of C-C bonds. The proportion of ethers and alcohols in the products was relatively low, while hydrocarbons and ketones were the main products after PP transformation (Fig. 2a). Meanwhile, at the beginning of the reaction, the chain length of products mainly showed a narrow distribution range around $\mathrm { C } _ { 2 1 }$ . With the prolongation of the reaction time, the bonds in polymerization intermediates further were broken, and the products were mainly distributed with small molecular carbons (Fig. 2c). Therefore, the fracture and oxidation degree of PP and related intermediates increased.

SEM images showed that the cracks on the smooth and flat plastic surface became more obvious with the extension of reaction time, and that the PP gradually changed from granular to dispersed fragments (Fig. S12). XPS spectra confirmed that the degree of oxidation on the PP solid surface of gradually increased as the reaction progressed (Fig. S13).

The molecular weights $( \mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { n } }$ and $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathbf { w } } \mathrm { , }$ and molecular weight distribution D $( \mathbf { M } _ { \mathbf { w } } / \mathbf { M } _ { \mathbf { n } } )$ of residual solid were further investigated by GPC. As shown in Fig. 2d and Table S2, the mixture showed a decrease in $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { n } }$ and an increase in D at the beginning of the reaction compared to untreated PP. Moreover, properties of the polymer were altered after reactions. The dispersity decreased with a deep conversion due to the cleavage of a larger fraction of the highest-molecular-mass species [1]. Overall, the residual pP extracted from the post-reaction solution was shifted towards lower molecular weights as compared to the pristine PP.

XPS and FTIR profiles of CuMgCWS600 before and after the reaction confirmed the activation of PMS by major metals (M-O, $\mathbf { M } = \mathbf { C } \mathbf { u } ,$ . The introduction of Mg in CuMgCWS600 shifted the $\mathtt { C u 2 p }$ core spectrum toward high binding energy. Higher electron deficit state Cu ions were promoted to be generated, providing higher redox potentials for PMS activation [21]. In addition, the Mg 1 s peak of CuMgCWS600 exhibited at lower binding energy (1304.1 eV) than that of $\mathsf { M g C W S 6 0 0 }$ (1305.0 eV). The results confirmed the existence of electronic interactions between Cu and $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { g }$ atoms in CuMgCWS600, revealing a new pathway of electron transfer.

XAFS measurements were used to further investigate the chemical state and coordination environment of Cu. The introduction of $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { g }$ affected the number of coordinating atoms around Cu, and the Cu-O bond became shorter, indicating a significant interaction between Cu and $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { g }$ (Table S4). # 3.6. Theoretical calculation of the catalytic PMS activation process

Specific distribution of bimetallic nanoparticles leads to changes in the electronic and geometrical structure of $\mathrm { C u } / \mathrm { M g }$ metal sites. The synergistic effect of the bimetals changes the interaction of metal sites with oxidant molecules. To further explain the role of bimetal synergy on PMS activation, we constructed the optimal adsorption conformations of PMS on MgCWS, CuCWS and CuMgCWS surfaces (Fig. 3e and S20). The O-O bonds of PMS were sideloaded onto the Cu sites of CuCWS and CuMgCWS. The side-on adsorption conformation resulted in the formation of main active species of \*OH and $S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ [17]. The adsorption energy of PMS at the Cu sites on CuMgCWS $\langle \Delta E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } = - 3 . 2 7 \ \mathrm { e V } \rangle$ was more negative than that on MgCWS ( $\mathrm { \Delta } \Delta E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } { = } { } { \cdot } 1 . 5 2 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ $\Delta E _ { \mathrm { a d s } } \mathrm { = }$ $- 2 . 6 6 \ : \mathrm { e V } )$ , indicating that CuMgCWS performed a higher chemisorption activity. The introduction of $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { g }$ sites promoted the PMS activation at Cu sites on the surface of CuMgCWS compared to CuCWS, resulting in the lengths of the O-O bonds in PMS at 1.50 and $1 . 5 4 \mathring \mathrm { A }$ on the CuCWS and CuMgCWs surface sites, respectively. Electron-deficient Cu states accepted electrons from PMS more quickly, thus inducing faster decomposition of PMS into \*OH. Thus, PMS was easier to be activated on the CuMgCWS surface.

The introduction of Mg sites, on one hand, directly modulated the charge distribution near the Cu sites on the catalyst surface. Cu-Mg dual sites of CuMgCWS exhibited high-intensity DOS in the common range of $- 3 { \mathrm { - } } 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ (Fig. 3f), indicating the synergistic effect of $\mathrm { C u - M g }$ dual sites on charge separation and migration. Besides, the stronger overlap of peaks in PDOS plots of CuMgCWS proved more mutual bonding between metals and O (Fig. 3f and S20) [31]. The metal-O channel extended the electron transport path on the catalyst surface. The results further confirmed that the presence of $\mathrm { M g } ^ { 2 + }$ favored the formation of positively charged Cu through interactions with O atoms. The charge density difference demonstrated that the $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { g }$ sites induced a redistribution effect of electrons around Cu atoms (Fig. 3e). Compared with CuCWS, CuMgCWS exhibited a more moderate d-band center (Fig. 3f), which was conducive to the optimization of the binding energy with the reaction intermediates [31,32]. In addition, the calculation of electron transferred number from the catalysts to PMS showed that CuMgCWS (0.8561 e) could provide more electrons to PMS than $\mathrm { { M g C W S } }$ (0.3894 e) and CuCWS (0.6467 e). Obviously, the d-band center of Cu 3d was shifted to the right significantly and closer to the Fermi energy level, realizing the efficient adsorption and rapid electron transfer from CuMgCWS to PMS (Fig. 3e and S20).

The Gibbs free energies of PMS activation at the surface sites of MgCWS, CuCWS and CuMgCWS were further calculated to explore the activation mechanism of PMS. The intermediate structures and the corresponding energy distributions were shown in Fig. 3g and S21. The important reaction step for PMS activation was first adsorption to the catalysts surface (I) to (II)). More negative adsorption energy on the catalyst surface sites suggested higher PMS adsorption efficiency. (II) to (III) were the dissociation processes from $\mathbf { P M S ^ { * } }$ to $S O _ { 4 } { ^ * }$ and $\mathrm { { \stackrel { \cdot } { \sim } } O H }$ , respectively. Electrons on CuMgCWS surface sites were more readily transferred to the adsorbed PMS. The dissociation energy of PMS by CuMgCWS was $0 . 2 4 \ : \mathrm { e V }$ , which was much smaller than that by $\mathrm { M g C W S }$ $( 2 . 6 0 \mathrm { e V } )$ and CuCWS $( 1 . 0 2 \mathrm { e V } )$ . The O-O bond was more easily broken at the surface sites of CuMgCWS. The $\mathrm { { \stackrel { \cdot } { \tilde { \alpha } } } O H }$ was further desorbed to generate •OH (III) to (IV): rate-limiting step). The energy of $\mathbf { \sigma } ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { O H }$ desorption from the surface of CuMgCWS $5 . 9 3 \mathrm { e V } )$ was lower than that from MgCWS (6.54 eV) and CuCWS $( 6 . 1 0 \mathrm { e V } )$ , accompanying with more •OH production. The content of $\cdot _ { \mathrm { O H } }$ mainly determined the bond breaking and oxidation depth of PP within the system to achieve the target products formation. The mechanism of PMS activation by CuMgCWS for PP conversion was shown in Fig. 4a. Under the synergistic effect of the bimetal on the catalyst surface, more M-O/M-M channels were provided and electron transfer was accelerated. The active sites on the catalyst surface adsorbed oxygen atoms of PMS through electrostatic and magnetic interactions and transferred electrons from the active sites to PMS. Thus, PMS was better activated to break O-O bond and produce active species dominated by 'OH to achieve the targeted conversion of PP.

# 3.8. Environmental benefit analysis

The aim of this study was to maximize the economic benefits by producing high-value products while processing waste plastics. The major mass balances of PP conversion products in the CuMgCWS600/ PMS system were shown in Fig. 5a. PP conversion produced ketones, paraffins, esters, and ethers mainly in the range of C5 to C26. Ketones with C5 to C17 can be used as lubricants or gasoline additives. Paraffins with C11 to C22 can be applied to produce gasoline and diesel [33]. Esters with C7 to C26 can be sold as organic solvents or purified as pharmaceuticals. C20 ethers can also be used as organic solvents.

A used milk bottle and a lunch box made mainly of PP were used to validate the practicality of the system. Commercial plastics usually contain additives that may diminish the reaction performance of the catalyst. The performances of CuMgCWS600 in activation of PMS for the conversion of milk bottles $( 5 9 . 7 \% )$ and lunch boxes $( 5 5 . 9 \% )$ were shown in Fig. 5b. Even in the presence of various additives, the main products of plastic conversion were still identified as saturated hydrocarbons and ketones, while including some oxygenated organics (esters/ alcohols and ethers). The high conversion rates and similar product compositions demonstrated the feasibility of catalytic PMS system for the conversion of commercial PP plastics.

EPR identified free radicals and persistent radicals on the surface of residual commercial plastics after the reaction. DMPO-\*OH and DMPO$S O _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ adduct signals were observed in the EPR spectra (Fig. 5c). Meanwhile, $g$ -factor value of 2.0036 confirmed the formation of carbon/oxygen centered EPFRs on reacted plastic surface (Fig. 5d) [27,28]. Activation of reactive chemicals can lead to the production of various EPFRs on the surface of plastic. EPFRs can regulate the production ratios of ROS, thus performing a function in the aging, biotoxicity, and degradation of plastics. The presence of EPFRs can allow the plastics to be more reactive and accelerate their autoxidation. On the other hand, the generated free radicals with high concentration and oxidation potential can effectively degrade microplastics and other pollutants enriched on the surface of microplastics [34].

In addition, ROS is a major cause of oxidative stress in organisms. Therefore, the formation of EPFRs on microplastics during oxidation may lead to an increase in the potential toxicity to organisms, which provides new perspectives for assessing the environmental risks. In summary, catalytic oxidation of PMS not only promoted the formation of stable EPFRs on PPs, but also increased the number of oxygencontaining groups on the residual PP surface and enlarged the surface roughness of PPs (Fig. S22), which affected the pollutant adsorption, bio-uptake pathway, and fragmentation trend of PPs. Overall, the CuMgCWS600/PMS system achieved targeted conversion of PP under optimized conditions. PP was converted to high-value chemicals dominated by hydrocarbons and ketones, supplemented by oxygenated organic compounds such as esters and ethers. (Fig. 5e). The calorific value of the products $( 5 5 . 5 \mathrm { M J } / \mathrm { k g } )$ was higher compared to the range of calorific value of plastics combustion (3743 MJ/kg). Herein, by constructing a Fenton-like system with Cu-Mg bimetallic synergistic sites, PMS was activated efficiently to produce 'OH-dominated species with strong oxidizing ability. The efficient conversion of PP under mild conditions overcame the harsh conditions required by existing plastic conversion technologies. By designing the active sites on the catalyst surface, the composition and contribution of the dominant active species in the system were regulated to obtain a better end-product composition. Directional and controllable oxidation depth avoided the irregular bond breaking under conventional pyrolysis, achieving green valorization of waste plastics.

# 4. Conclusion

Our goal was to develop a new sustainable Fenton-like system to convert polypropylene into value-added chemicals. $\mathrm { C u - M g }$ synergistic sites in the catalyst were designed to provide additional channels and accelerate interfacial electron transfer. The adsorption and activation of PMS to produce reactive species (mainly 'OH) greatly facilitated the efficient PP conversion $( \sim 8 0 ~ \% )$ to hydrocarbons and ketones under mild conditions $( 1 6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ . The conditions and products of PP conversion, as well as the possible mechanisms of the catalytic activation of PMS and plastic conversion, were explored in depth through fundamental experiments, correlation analysis and DFT calculations. The introduction of Mg-O sites in $\mathtt { C u / C }$ sponge modulated the interfacial charge distribution and increased electron transfer channels. The adsorption and activation of PMS by Cu-centered sites were then promoted. Considering that the reported catalyst surface sites can be further tailored in various ways to change the composition of active species within the system, we anticipate that this work will inspire more research on targeted conversion of waste plastics. The widespread use of plastics and traditional disposal methods have caused serious environmental issues. Plastic waste is rich in carbon resources, which can be converted into valuable chemicals to turn waste into treasure. Compared with the traditional treatment methods, the heterogeneous Fenton-like system we constructed can not only alleviate the increasingly serious white pollution problem, but also realize the circular economy of plastics. It is of great significance for the establishment of a sustainable society.
