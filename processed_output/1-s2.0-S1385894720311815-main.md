# A B S T R A C T

In this work, we synthesized highly porous reduced graphene oxide foams, presenting augmented antibacterial activity in presence/absence of peroxymonosulfate (PMS). To date, this is the first investigation of photocatalytic PMS disinfection by 3D-rGO materials. The foams were prepared through hydrothermal (3DG-W) and solvothermal method (3DG-EG), and analyzed by SEM, PXRD, FTIR, Raman spectroscopy and BET. Hydrothermally synthesized aerogels (3DG-W) were better organized and had higher specific surface area. The higher ratio of $\mathrm { I _ { D } / }$ $\mathrm { I _ { G } }$ in 3DG-W showed the greater removal of oxygen functional groups, larger density of structural defects and a smaller average size of the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ domains. Although in the dark PMS activation by 3DG and the subsequent bacterial inactivation likely proceeds via a non-radical pathway, under visible light, the bacterial inactivation rate for 3DG-W was ${ \sim } 3$ times higher than that of 3DG-EG and radical pathways are dominant. The generated germicidal reactive species were identified using appropriate scavengers and accordingly, an overview of the occurring photocatalytic and PMS activation pathways is discussed, while the mechanisms leading to bacterial inactivation are hereby detailed.

# 1. Introduction

The uncontrolled growth of the population and, in consequence, the ensuing request for commodities of all kinds is aggravating an existing challenge of modern-day societies, namely that of providing enough clean water to all. Among the solutions proposed to reduce water consumption is the replacement of freshwater needs by reclaimed sources, such as reuse of wastewater for agriculture [1]. This solution though involves eliminating chemical and biological risks. Municipal wastewater is comprised of bacteria, viruses and other less common microorganisms (eggs, helminths), including pathogenic strains in the microorganism populations [2]. For the release of bacteria to the environment, a limit is set by the World Health Organization (WHO) [3]. Therefore, state-of-the-art degradation techniques of aqueous biological and chemical contaminants are currently explored.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have been demonstrated as powerful techniques because of their ability to produce the reactive oxygen species (ROS) for the degradation of a wide range of contaminants into harmless substances without secondary pollution, mild reaction conditions and high efficiency [4,5]. The AOP technologies can be mainly classified as radiation [6], sonolysis [7], electrochemical oxidation technologies [8], Fenton-based reactions [9], ozone-based processes [6], photolysis [10] and photocatalysis [1112]. Over the last two decades, sulfate radical-based AOPs (SR-AOPs) have gained significant interest, since they present strong oxidizing ability $( \mathrm { E } _ { 0 } = 2 . 5 \mathrm { - } 3 . 1 ~ \mathrm { V } )$ , a long life-time (half-life $= 4 s \mathrm { \ i }$ ) and formation in a wide pH range; hence the SR-AOPs overcome the known restrictions of the conventional Fenton process, such as strict $\mathtt { p H }$ necessities $( \sim 3 )$ , production of large amounts of sludge and short life time $( < 1 ~ \mu s )$ of hydroxyl radicals [13]. Sulfate radicals can be generated from two major oxidants, peroxydisulfate (PDS) and peroxymonosulfate (PMS), using catalysts or applying energy, such as light, heat, and microwave radiation, essential for breaking the O-O bond [14–17]. The use of PMS in the SR-AOPs has received increasing attention thanks to its high reactivity and also to its high potential in generating sulfate radical [18]. The catalytic activity of the oxidants can be attained through the chemical activation by metallic ions (e. g. ${ \mathsf { C o } } ^ { 2 + }$ , $\mathrm { R u } ^ { 3 + }$ , $\mathrm { M n } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathsf { F e } ^ { 2 + \cdot }$ ), metal oxides (e. g. $\bf { M n } _ { 3 } \bf { O } _ { 4 } ,$ , $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 } )$ , or metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) [19]. These metal-based activators contain the metal in variable chemical states and an unoccupied orbital which can give electrons to activate peroxy bonds [19,20].

There are, however, documented concerns about the use of metals such as Co and Ni on the activation of persulfate, due to their inherent toxicity characteristics [21,22]. As a consequence, environmentally friendly metal-free carbon materials to activate PMS in SR-AOPs were developed, such as graphene [23,24], reduced graphene oxide (GO) [25] and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) [26], which have been proposed as a promising alternatives to the toxic and/or expensive metals and their oxides. Mechanistically speaking, it is now widely accepted that pristine carbo-catalysis might be originating from the “active sites” such as structural defects or functional groups [23,24]. Constructed by $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ - hybridized carbons, graphene and graphene-based materials have attracted considerable attention owing to their exceptional optical, electrical, and physicochemical properties, properties [27]. If one considers the wide band gap of GO and its controllable transformation to a semiconductor or to a graphene-like semi-metal by reduction, this pathway opens up the possibility for photocatalytic applications such as conversion of $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ [28,29] and water splitting [30,31]. Graphene absorbs photons from the visible wavelengths up to the infrared with strong intra-band transitions and ultrafast response [32]. But it were Sun et al. first discovered that chemically reduced graphene oxide (rGO) can act as an effective metal-free catalyst to activate PMS to generate reactive radicals [24]. The evolution of works in the field, on the other hand, has also received proof that the possibility of non-radical pathways initiated by carbonaceous materials may also lead to effective decontamination [33,34].

The aggregation and restacking of rGO sheets as a result of π-π stacking interactions result in the decrement of their specific surface area and catalytic active sites [35]. While by transforming 2D graphene to a three-dimensional (3D) structure, tremendously practical properties such as specific surface area, interconnected 3D macroporous and fast mass transfer, easier recycling, control and handling of the catalyst have been encountered [36]. The 3D foams are the realistic way to apply these catalysts, in a sort of flow-through filter and not as a supported surface that suffers from kinetic limitations and low contact with the water to be treated [37,38].

The catalytic activity of nitrogen-doped graphene aerogels with various GO:urea:EDA mass ratios by degradation of 2-sec-butyl-4,6-dinitrphenol (DNBP) was evaluated by Zhao et al. [5].

The aim of the present study was to synthesize 3D reduced graphene oxide via facile methods and test these materials for their capacity in photocatalytic disinfection, alone or as PMS activators. One-pot hydrothermal and solvothermal methods were compared as synthesis routes, while the effect of solvents on the reduction of the oxygencontaining functional groups of the synthesized 3D materials was assessed. Furthermore, we evaluated the photocatalytic activity of the prepared samples as semiconductors under visible light. Finally, we report the first application of the visible-light photocatalytic disinfection through PMS activation by using the synthesized 3D reduced graphene oxide as a novel photocatalyst and propose the mechanism of bacterial inactivation governing the composite process.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Chemicals and reagents

Graphene oxide (GO) powder (purity $> 9 9 \%$ , thickness: $3 . 4 \mathrm { - } 7 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , 6–10 layers) was received from Nanosay Co., Iran. Ethylene glycol (EG, $\mathrm { H O C H _ { 2 } C H _ { 2 } O H }$ : $9 9 . 8 \%$ ), Yeast Extract, potassium chloride (KCl: $9 9 \% ]$ ), sodium chloride (NaCl: $9 9 . 5 \%$ ), sodium oxalate $\mathrm { ( N a _ { 2 } C _ { 2 } O _ { 4 } }$ : $9 9 . 5 \%$ ) superoxide dismutase from bovine erythrocytes (SOD, $\geq 3 0 0 0$ units/mg protein) and peroxymonosulfate $( \mathrm { O X O N E } ^ { \mathrm { r m } }$ , $\mathrm { K H S O _ { 5 } { \cdot } 0 . 5 K H S O _ { 4 } { \cdot } 0 . 5 K _ { 2 } S O _ { 4 } }$ : $9 9 . 9 5 \%$ ) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co., USA. Tryptone was purchased from Dickinson and Co. Finally, all aqueous solutions were prepared using Ultrapure Milli-Q water (electric resistance ${ \sim } 1 8 . 2$ MΩ.cm).

# 2.2. Synthesis of the 3D reduced graphene oxide catalyst

3D reduced graphene oxide with various reduction degrees were prepared by hydrothermal and solvothermal methods. Colloidal dispersions of GO were subjected to solvothermal reduction in ethylene glycol and hydrothermal method. In each case, $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g }$ of GO was suspended in $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of Milli-Q water (3DG-W) and ethylene glycol (3DGEG) solvents by sonication for $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 1 h }$ in hydrothermal and solvothermal method, respectively. The selection of ethylene glycol as a solvent has been considered based on its reductive ability among alcohols. Sonication was done by using an ultrasonic bath (400 W, 60 min). Thereafter, each of the solutions was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and heated at ${ 1 9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } }$ under static conditions for $1 2 \mathrm { h }$ . The autoclave was then let to cool down to room temperature and a monolithic hydrogel was formed which takes the shape of the container inside the autoclave. Subsequently, the aerogels were soaked in Milli-Q water for $^ { 4 8 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ to remove any impurities and residual solvent, frozen at $- 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 6 b }$ and then freeze-dried (Alpha 1-2LDplus, Germany) in vacuum for $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ .

# 2.3. Characterization of the synthesized materials

XRD patterns of samples were recorded on a Philips X’Pert-MPD with $\mathrm { C u } \mathrm { K } \alpha$ radiation $( \lambda = 0 . 1 5 4 \mathrm { n m } )$ and time per step $\mathbf { 1 . 2 5 s }$ . Results were analyzed by X’Pert High Score software. The surface morphology of samples was characterized by means of scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Quanta 200). The specific surface area (SSA) and porosity were acquired by Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis performed with a BELSORP-mini II instrument. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed with a Bruker Tensor 27 within the range $4 0 0 { - } 4 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ with a resolution of $2 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ . The deconvolution of the FT-IR patterns was carried out with ORIGIN software considering the Gaussian function as the shape of the resolved peaks. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on a KRATOS AXIS Ultra DLD spectrometer equipped with a hemispherical electron analyzer and an Al anode (Al $\mathrm { K } \alpha = 1 4 8 6 . 6 \ \mathrm { e V } .$ ). Raman spectra of the samples were obtained by Takram P50C0R10 Raman spectrometer with an excitation wavelength of $5 3 2 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . The electrical conductivity of the samples was analyzed by the four-point probe method. Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) was carried out in a Perkin Elmer Lambda 900 UV–VIS-NIR spectrometer within the wavelength range of $3 0 0 { - } 8 0 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . The photoluminescence (PL) measurement was carried out using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer LS55) equipped with a Xenon-lamp passed through a monochrometer. Zeta potential was taken on a HORIBA SZ-100 series analyzer.

# 2.4. Photocatalytic experiments’ details

Escherichia coli, a common waterborne pathogenic microorganism, was chosen as an indicative microorganism to evaluate the photocatalytic disinfection performances of the synthesized photocatalysts. E. coli K12 wild type strain was obtained from the Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen, Braunschweig, Germany (DSMZ, No. 498). The master plate and stock solution were prepared according to a previous research reported by our laboratory [39]. In order to follow the bacterial concentration during the reaction, $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ m L } }$ of the sample was withdrawn after each pre-set time interval and then serial dilutions were made in saline solution to obtain a measurable count of colonies. The initial pH of the photocatalytic experiments was measured to be around 5.9.

The bacterial concentration of the samples (in Colony Forming Units $\mathbf { ( C F U ) } / \mathbf { m L }$ was determined by plating on a plate count agar (PCA, Merck, Germany). A $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ aliquot was dispersed onto a petri-dish containing PCA according to the standard plate count method. The plates were incubated for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ at ${ } ^ { 3 7 } \ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ in the dark. All experiments were performed at least two times in duplicate under the same reaction conditions (in two parallel tests), and minimum two dilutions were plated. The kinetics of reactions were evaluated, and first order rate constants $( \bf k _ { a p p } )$ were calculated. It should be pointed out that control tests were carried out with cells subjected to different photocatalysts in dark (no irradiation) and treated in the absence of any photocatalyst under visible light irradiation (photolysis). To verify that no re-growth of $E$ . coli occurs after the total disinfection, the treated samples were kept for $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ at $3 7 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in an incubator. Then bacterial suspension of $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ was dispersed on Petri dishes as mentioned before. In order to identify the generated species, radical-scavengers such as isopropanol ( OH· scavenger, $0 . 2 \mathrm { m M } )$ , sodium oxalate (a $\nu b h ^ { + }$ scavenger, $0 . 2 \mathrm { m M } )$ , superoxide dismutase $( O _ { 2 } ^ { \cdot - }$ scavenger, 50 units/mL) and NaCl $( S O _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ scavenger, $0 . 2 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ ) were used in the present study.

As can be seen in supplementary material (scheme 1), samples were irradiated in the cavity of a Suntest solar simulator CPS (Atlas GmbH, Hanau, Germany). The solar simulator bears a cut-off filter eliminating the light $< 3 1 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ . A second cut-off filter was manually placed in the Suntest during bacterial inactivation, with a cut-off blocking the wavelength $< 4 0 5 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ , in order to simulate visible light conditions. The light emission spectrum of the lamp and the cut-off of the visible light filter are given in the supplementary material (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2). The overall light dose from the emission of the lamp inside the reactor was $3 8 \mathrm { \ m W / c m ^ { 2 } }$ ( ${ \sim } 0 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 6 }$ photons/s). The intensity of the irradiation was measured by a coupled UV radiometer and a pyranometer (CUV3 & CM6b, Kipp & Zonen, Delft, Netherlands). The temperature in the air-cooled Suntest chamber never exceeded $3 8 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ .

# 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Catalyst characterization

SEM images of the GO sheets as precursor material are presented in Fig. 1a. The GO powder comprised of smooth nanosheets with folded shapes at the edges and the wrinkled GO sheets are loosely connected to each other [40]. The interconnected 3D porous network is shown in the SEM images of the as-synthesized samples (Fig. $^ \mathrm { ~ 1 ~ b ~ }$ and c). Sub-micrometer pores to several micrometer ones can be seen in the 3D network. It is noteworthy that the arrangement of pores in the hydrothermally synthesized aerogel (Fig. 1b) is evidently better organized.

The XRD patterns of GO and the as-prepared 3D aerogels are shown in Fig. 2. The XRD pattern of GO showed two characteristic peaks centered at 2θ of 10.7 and $4 2 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ corresponding to (0 0 1) and (1 0 0), respectively. The characteristic (0 0 2) stacking peak of graphene at $2 0 { - } 2 8 ^ { \circ }$ is observed in the pattern of the 3D aerogels (3DG-W and 3DGEG) and the typical peak of GO at about $1 1 ^ { \circ }$ disappeared which suggested that GO was reduced in the synthesized 3D aerogels. From the XRD parameters, the interlayer distances of the synthesized aerogels by hydrothermal (3DG-W) and solvothermal (3DG-EG) were estimated using Bragg’s Law, 3.81 and $4 . 1 4 \mathring { \mathrm { A } } ,$ , respectively. In comparison with the interlayer distances of GO the precursor $( 8 . 2 \dot { 8 } \mathring { \mathrm { A } } )$ , these much lower values indicated the presence of $\scriptstyle \pi - \pi$ stacking between reduced graphene oxide sheets in the aerogels and retaining little amount of oxygen-containing groups on reduced GO sheets [41].

Fig. 3 presents the FTIR spectra of GO, 3DG-W and 3DG-EG samples. The broad band between 3000 and $3 7 0 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ can be attributed to the stretching mode of $\mathrm { O - H }$ intercalated water between GO sheets, hydroxyl and eCOOH groups on the GO layers. Moreover, the peak at $1 6 2 9 ~ \mathrm { { c m } } ^ { - 1 }$ corresponds to the $\mathrm { O - H }$ deformation vibration of adsorbed water. The peak centered at $1 7 2 7 ~ \mathrm { { c m } } ^ { - 1 }$ accounts for the stretching vibration of carboxyl and conjugated carbonyl groups. The peak at $1 2 2 3 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ and a shoulder are attributed to the asymmetric stretching of epoxide $\scriptstyle ( \mathbf { C } - \mathbf { O } - \mathbf { C } )$ , and the peak at $8 7 6 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ represents the bending motion of epoxide $\scriptstyle ( \mathbf { C } - \mathbf { O } - \mathbf { C } )$ . The band at $1 0 4 4 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ is assigned to the vibration of $\subset - \cup$ or $\mathrm { C - O H }$ and the peak at $1 4 2 7 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ corresponds to the bending of $\mathrm { C - O H }$ . The spectra show the presence of bands located at $2 8 5 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and $2 9 2 4 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ corresponding to the symmetric and asymmetric vibrations of the $\mathrm { C - H }$ groups. The peak at $1 6 2 9 ~ \mathrm { { c m } } ^ { - 1 }$ corresponding to the $\scriptstyle { \mathrm { C } } = { \mathrm { C } }$ vibrations of the graphene skeleton.

The assignment of the deconvoluted peaks for the C 1s analysis of 3DG-EG and 3DG-W samples are in-line with GO components, while there is a significant reduction of oxygenated carbon signals. The evolution of the C/O atomic ratio was found to be 2.8, 5.2 and 10.1, for GO to 3DG-EG and 3DG-W, respectively. The increase of the C/O ratio shows the elimination of most of the oxygen functionalities of graphene oxide. By the same token it is also obvious that in O 1s peaks (Supplementary Fig. S3) at $5 3 2 . 6 \mathrm { e V }$ demonstrated O containing groups are incrementally broadened, weakened and decreased from GO to 3DG-EG and 3DG-W, respectively.

The relative area intensity of O-related peaks, normalized with the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ component (Fig. 6), provides a quantitative concept for investigation of functional groups. As can be clearly considered, all the O-species significantly decrease in the 3DG-W sample, while the intensity of the epoxide peak of 3DG-EG declined significantly after reduction with ethylene glycol, and the change of the intensity of hydroxyl component is not remarkable. These findings were unexpected and suggest that despite the stronger reductive ability of ethylene glycol than that of water, it seems the autoionization of super-heated water at $1 9 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 } 0 ^ { + } + \mathrm { O H } ^ { - }$ , results in the higher diffusivity and stronger removal of functional groups, as also seen elsewhere [43,51–53]. Furthermore, higher removal of the epoxide groups mediated by ethylene glycol comes from the higher reactivity of C-O-C than other functional groups due to the ring strain.

Furthermore, the degree of GO reduction was identified by the oxygen-bound carbon content, which was calculated using Eq. (2) [54]:

$$
\% O - b o u n d C = \frac { A _ { C - O H } + A _ { C - O - C } + A _ { C = O } + A _ { O - C = O } } { A _ { C 1 s } } \times 1 0 0 
$$

where $A _ { C - O H } , A _ { C - O - C } , A _ { C = O }$ and $A _ { { \cal O } - C = { \cal O } }$ are the peak areas of Obound carbon and $\mathbf { A } _ { \mathbf { C } 1 s }$ corresponds to the area under C1s peak. GO as a precursor contains $6 1 . 7 \%$ O-bound carbon, whereas as-synthesized 3DG-EG and 3DG-W resulted in different degree of reduction, $4 6 \%$ and $2 3 . 4 \%$ , respectively. This trend is also in good agreement with the results previously observed via FT-IR (Figs. 3 and 4).

Fig. 7 presents the Raman spectra of GO and the 3D aerogels prepared with the different methods used. The Raman spectrum of GO displays two characteristic bands: k-point phonons of $\mathbf { A } _ { 1 g }$ symmetry at around $1 3 5 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , the D band. The $\mathrm { E } _ { 2 g }$ phonon of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ atoms is centered at $1 5 9 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , the G band. Various defects or a breakdown of translational symmetry in the graphite lattice is ascribed via D band, while the Brillouin zone of crystalline $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ lattices in graphite derived from first-order scattering in G band, related to $\mathrm { E } _ { 2 g }$ symmetry. The disorder degree and restoration of the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ domains of the graphenebased materials are commonly estimated via calculating the intensity ratio of these bands $\mathrm { ( I _ { D } / I _ { G } ) }$ [55]. The reduction of GO by solvothermal and hydrothermal methods resulted in an increment of $\mathrm { I _ { D } } / \mathrm { I _ { G } }$ ratio from of 0.93 in GO to 0.97 and 1.23 in 3DG-EG and 3DG-W, respectively. The higher ratio of the later suggests a decrease in the average size of the graphitic domains due to the greater removal of oxygen functional group and, in consequence, a larger density of structural defects, which is in line with the FTIR and XPS results. This observation is consistent with the literature [42,56]. A decrease in the average size of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ clusters in the former stems from further reduction in 3DG-W and the formation of more new re-graphitized $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ domains as described in the previous studies [58–60]. Consequently, taking in account that surface defects are among the main contributors of “active sites” [14,32], and their presence has been linked with disinfection capacities [61], then a higher photocatalytic activity is expected.

In order to evaluate the effect of the synthesis method on the porosity and the specific surface area of the samples, the $\Nu _ { 2 }$ adsorption–desorption isotherm of the samples was investigated and presented in Fig. 8. The shape of both isotherms seems to be nearly a type IV isotherm with a hysteresis loop typically belong to a type H3 at high relative pressures according to the IUPAC classification, indicating their mesoporous structures in the size range of $2 \mathrm { - } 5 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ [62,63]. Compared with 3DG-EG, the isotherm of 3DG-W shows a larger absorption at high relative pressure $\left( \mathrm { P } / \mathrm { P } _ { 0 } \right)$ range, which indicates the higher formation mesopores [64]. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller surface area of 3DG-W is $1 2 7 . 1 \ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ , which is higher than that of the 3DG-EG foam $( 5 7 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ . This is attributed to the fact that the former has a well-defined morphology, and this result is consistent with the observations in the SEM image (Fig. 1). The corresponding pore-size distribution of the samples by Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method (inset of Fig. 8) from the adsorption branch of the isotherms exhibits a narrow peak in the mesopore range [64]. In terms of how these traits will affect their photocatalytic activity, two different effects are expected: i) The micro/nano-structure with mesopores and large specific surface area of the 3DG-W provide transport paths and more active sites in photocatalytic reactions, but ii) the size of the pore implies that the bacteria will not be able to penetrate the 3D structure. PMS has a size in the nano-region $\mathbf { \Phi } _ { \cdot } < 1 \ \mathbf { n m }$ , according to its S-O and O-O bond lengths), hence it shall fully exploit the higher specific surface area of 3D-W, while bacteria will only come in contact with the outer surface of the 3D structure.

# 3.3. Mechanistic interpretation of the photocatalytic E. coli inactivation by the 3DG materials in presence of PMS

# 3.3.1. Scavenger experiments: Involvement of reactive oxidizing or reductive species

The radical scavenging experiments were performed using different reagent in order to quench the specific reactive species, aiming to gain insight into the bacterial inactivation mechanism. The generation of species such asOH· , $\mathbf { \Gamma } _ { O _ { 2 } ^ { . - } / H O _ { 2 } ^ { . } }$ and $\nu b h ^ { + }$ play a vital role in the photocatalytic inactivation of bacteria. Here, we chose non-toxic scavengers in order not to affect the viability of bacterial cells in order to dissociate their quenching effect, and the results are presented in Fig. 13. The pseudo first-order rate constants for bacterial inactivation in the presence of as-mentioned scavengers have been displayed in Supplementary Table S1. It is worth noting that the NaCl concentration level was low enough to generate a limited amount of free chlorine in $\mathrm { P M S } / \mathrm { C l } ^ { - }$ system. As a result, the generated HOCl is negligible through bacterial inactivation.

As can be seen in Fig. 13 a and b, the addition of isopropanol and sodium chloride can greatly inhibit the photocatalytic inactivation of bacteria mediated by either photocatalyst, which indicates that the OH· and/or $s o _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ radicals played the dominant role during the catalytic oxidation, while the former is more effective. As such, a shift from nonradical to radical pathways may be involved under light. PMS as an oxidant can be applied as electron acceptor in the photocatalysis process. Sulfate and hydroxyl radicals seem to be formed with donated electrons from graphene to PMS similar to previous investigations [18,25] (see Table 2, Eqs. (2) and (3)). From Fig. 13, we can also note the incomplete bacterial inactivation after the addition of SOD, an $O _ { 2 } ^ { - } ( O _ { 2 } ^ { -- } / H O _ { 2 } ^ { \cdot } )$ quencher. This is a proof of the excitation of electrons in the lattice and the reaction with oxygen to produce superoxide radicals (and possibly after oxidation, singlet oxygen). Suffice it to mention that since the superoxide radical can act as precursor for $^ 1 o _ { 2 }$ (via hole oxidation) and the holes/superoxide effect is too low, the $^ 1 o _ { 2 }$ -mediated inactivation is low as well. It is interesting to observe that in the 3DG-W experiments in the presence of SOD doubled the necessary reaction time $( 0 . 2 7 8 \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ for 3DG-W and $0 . 1 \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ for 3DG-EG, which results in a $2 9 \%$ inhibition of the photocatalytic activity for 3DG-EG compared with $3 9 \%$ for 3DG-W); this is an indication of a more efficient semiconductor action of 3DG-W when compared to 3DG-EG, where the inhibition was lower. Although the formation of radicals, the dominance of non-radical pathways, or combination of both during carbo-catalytic PMS activation are still disputable, we could propose that under light, the dominant pathway is the light-initiated one that leads to higher radicals’ generation. Furthermore, we should note that the generated oxidative stress, membrane stress by electron exchange that account for the antibacterial effects of graphene and its derivatives are still subject to investigation [78].

It should also be mentioned that besides light absorption ability, the band energy position of the semiconductor and the redox potentials of the adsorbents must be considered for the formation of reactive species. The conduction band edge of GO-based materials, which is mainly formed by the anti-bonding $\pi ^ { * }$ orbital, regardless of the functional group contents is around $- 0 . 7 \ \mathrm { e V }$ [31,69]. Comparing the band edge energy level with the redox potential $\mathrm { o f } { \cal O } _ { 2 } / { \cal O } _ { 2 } ^ { -- } ~ = ~ - 0 . 3 3 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ vs NHE, $\pmb { O } _ { 2 } ^ { -- }$ seems to be generated initially by the excited electrons in the conduction band of 3DG aerogels. The energy level diagram for inactivation of bacteria on the 3DG-W aerogel is given in Supplementary Fig. S9. As can be seen, for the generated holes of 3DG-W, the valence band edge potential cannot oxidize water to generate hydroxyl radicals. Based on the redox potential of $\mathbf { O } _ { 2 } / H _ { 2 } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } = \mathbf { \Gamma } + 0 . 6 8 2 \ \mathrm { e V }$ vs NHE, ${ \bf { { H } } } _ { 2 } { \bf { { O } } } _ { 2 }$ seems to be generated consequently followed by the formation of $\scriptstyle o H$ radicals according to the following equations (Eqs. (4)–(6)):

$$

In the presence of 3DG-W-PMS, the bacterial inactivation for an initial loading of $2 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } \left( \mathrm { C F U / m l } \right)$ by addition of the sodium oxalate a hole $\mathbf { \nu } _ { v b h } +$ scavenger lead to a decrease of about 2-orders of magnitude, while the related inactivation curve of the 3DG-EG-PMS had no obvious change after adding sodium oxalate. It seems that holes might not take part in the inactivation of bacteria for 3DG-EG-PMS compared with 3DG-W-PMS implying the minor contribution of holes in the inactivation process.

By way of comparison, radical scavenging experiments in 3D aerogels without PMS were illustrated in Fig. S10. The accelerated bacterial inactivation rates for the 3D aerogels in the absence of peroxymonosulfate under light with respect to the dark condition demonstrate that the higher inactivation efficiency in visible light irradiation condition is rather due to the other reactive species (besides electron transfer) which is confirmed by these scavenging experiments. The results revealed that in absence of PMS, the 3DG-W-Vis photocatalytic inactivation proceeds by a mixed radical and non-radical pathway, proven by the fact that the used radical scavengers moderately inhibited bacterial inactivation. This combination of findings provides some support for the conceptual premise that the holes are playing a less important role for bacterial inactivation via hydroxyl radical production.

Consequently, the direct semiconductor action of bacteria will not be present and the 3DG will act as an initiator of radicals that will kill the bacteria. What seems to be augmenting the disinfection capacity though, is the activation of PMS by the holes, resulting $\mathrm { \ t o S O } _ { 5 } ^ { - }$ , due to the reasons already analyzed.

Hence, the higher activation capacity is attributed to the photocatalytic character, apart from the higher surface area proposed before.

# 4. Conclusions

In this work, for the first time, PMS activation mediated by a 3DrGO support was reported. Under visible light irradiation, prepared 3DG materials with an appropriate degree of oxidation and surface area were used as an effective photocatalyst and subsequently as a potent PMS activator for bacterial inactivation.

The XPS analysis and deconvolution of the FT-IR spectra estimated the degree of functionalities in the aerogels which verified the higher reduction degree of the hydrothermally synthesized aerogel (3DG-W). Furthermore, the higher conductivity of the 3DG-W over the 3D-EG material predominantly stems from the higher recovery of $\pi$ -conjugated system from GO sheets upon hydrothermal reduction. Also, a greater removal of oxygen functional groups, larger density of structural defects and a smaller average size of the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ domains in the 3DG-W was found by Raman spectroscopy. The photoluminescence intensity was seen to decrease as a result of decrement in the average size of $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ clusters in the 3DG-W. The micro/nano-structure with mesopores and large specific surface area of the 3DG-W provide transport paths and more active sites in the photocatalytic reactions.

The modifications of the material have improved the basic photocatalytic properties of reduced graphene oxide, and have led to effective E. coli inactivation under visible light irradiation; an overall mechanism has been proposed on how our synthesized materials and visible light, with the addition of PMS can reach high bacterial removal effectiveness. Although in the dark tests the predominant pathway appears to be non-radical oxidation, in the scavenging experiments under light, we demonstrated that the produced radicals (from the photocatalytic baseline in combination with PMS) were capable of bacterial inactivation, while the effective conversion of PMS showed that the surface modified 3DG materials prepared are efficient carbonaceous activators, removing the need for metal-based processes or higher temperatures for its activation. As such, the proposed material has the potential in application and further studies (up scaling, economic evaluation) are deemed necessary for its further divulgation.
