We introduce a novel all-in-one fabrication method for porous catalytic polyethersulfone (PES) membranes containing NiO nanorods and carbon nanoparticles. This method is based on the sonochemical in situ solidification of a metal salt with $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ in presence of carbon particles in a PES-containing casting-reaction solution, directly followed by membrane preparation via film casting cum phase separation. In the catalytic flow-through degradation of $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ aqueous diclofenac with persulfate as oxidant, an as-prepared NiO-carbon-PES membrane achieved a $9 9 \%$ aromatic content removal degree at a residence time of only $_ { 1 . 6 s }$ . In comparison, NiO and CuO membranes without carbon particles exhibited $< 4 0 ~ \%$ aromatic removal degrees. The high performance was attributed to a degradation-induced adsorption, with a diclofenac removal capacity of $9 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / g }$ of incorporated carbon particles. We demonstrated that diclofenac is catalytically mineralized in the membrane at incorporated NiO nanorods, followed by adsorption of remaining degradation products at nearby carbon particles. Our data further suggests that reducing NiO to Ni(0) via a membrane pretreatment shifts the catalytic persulfate activation from a radical to a non-radical pathway, and that $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ can function as sacrificial electron donor for Ni(0). This completely mitigated nickel leaching from the membrane in contact to persulfate. Moreover, even in the presence of $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ and NaCl, a Ni(0)–carbon–PES membrane consistently eliminated $\sim 9 0 ~ \%$ of the total organic carbon (TOC) from a $2 \mathrm { m g / L }$ diclofenac feed containing persulfate in single-pass flow-through mode (continuously for $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 5 h }$ , respectively for $5 0 0 ~ \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ permeate volume), with no detectable release of degradation products or any nickel leaching.

# 1. Introduction

In recent years, new research findings increased the awareness for the level of pharmaceutical contaminants in the environment and the associated effects on aquatic systems, fauna, wildlife and humans [1]. One example is diclofenac, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug that is toxic for certain bird species and is extensively used in livestock feed, which led to the near extinction of Gyps vultures in Asia in the 1990s [2]. In India, the population decline in vultures also directly impacts human health due to an increase in rabies transmitting feral dogs and rats [3–5]. Diclofenac was further shown to accumulate in water birds and fish, potentially posing a significant ecological risk [6,7]. In general, diclofenac is found in various wastewater streams and natural water systems in concentrations ranging from $\scriptstyle { \mathtt { n g } } / \mathrm { L }$ to hundreds of $\mu \textrm g / \mathrm { L }$ [8,9]. However, the removal efficiency of most traditional wastewater treatment methods is limited [10]. This highlights the need for new and effective technologies for the remediation of diclofenac from water to limit its distribution and impact on the environment.

One potential technology for this task is the removal of organic contaminants from water by adsorption processes, employing either dispersed or immobilized adsorbents such as powdered activated carbon (PAC) [11]. However, the achievable adsorption efficiencies vary significantly depending on the chemical structure of the micropollutant, and the removal of charged molecules like diclofenac is often particularly limited [12–14]. Another promising technology is the remediation of organic micropollutants by advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), which generate active radical species capable of degrading a wide range of substances [15]. A unique characteristic of AOPs is their potential for the total elimination of organic molecules via mineralization to $\mathrm { C O _ { 2 } }$ . Common oxidants employed as radical source are $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ,$ ozone, chlorine dioxide, peroxymonosulfate and persulfate (PS) [16]. Of these oxidants, PS combines a high oxidation potential of $2 . 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs. standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) with a high activity over a wide $\mathsf { p H }$ range [17,18]. Nevertheless, commercialization for water treatment is currently still lacking due to long treatment times and high costs [19]. Treatment capacities can be substantially increased using heterogeneous metal catalysts to activate the oxidant, but routinely investigated powder catalysts are difficult to reuse and offer little options for a continuous water treatment process [20–22].

A promising alternative approach is the use of metal-decorated porous membranes as catalytic flow-through reactors for oxidative water treatment [23–26]. In flow-through, the enhanced mass transport to catalyst sites can promote increased degradation rates of organic micropollutants [27,28]. However, the short residence time in porous membranes can limit the amount of molecules that are fully mineralized to $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } ,$ potentially leading to the release of toxic degradation products into the treated water [29,30]. A review of the existing literature reveals that several state-of-the-art porous polymeric and ceramic membranes exhibit high catalytic oxidation efficiencies of organic micropollutants in flow-through $( > 9 5 \% )$ ) [31–41]. In comparison, the obtained mineralization degrees at short residence times $( < 2 0 \ : s )$ are still relatively low $( 2 0 - 6 0 \%$ ). Another factor is the cost and scalability of the metal catalyst synthesis. Most research efforts focus on catalyst compositions that necessitate complex synthesis methods, such as single atom catalysts, mixed metal oxides or metal–organic frameworks [42–44]. Additionally, metal-containing porous membranes are usually fabricated by either blending pre-fabricated metal particles with a polymer-containing casting solution that is used for membrane preparation via film casting cum phase separation, or by coating a membrane substrate with the metal catalyst [24,45]. Both approaches can suffer from a low metal usage efficiency due to material loss during catalyst pre-fabrication or the use of excess metal precursor for the membrane coating step [46–48].

Recently, we developed a straightforward all-in-one fabrication for CuO nanorod-containing porous polyethersulfone (PES) membranes [49]. This integrated approach consists of the in situ synthesis of solid copper from copper acetate by a sonochemical reduction with $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 } ,$ , which was conducted directly in a PES-containing casting-reaction solution. Afterwards, the casting-reaction solution can be used for the membrane preparation via film casting cum phase separation without any workup. This led to the rapid immobilization of highly active zerovalent copper nanorods into the membrane, which fully converted to copper oxide in contact to oxygen from air. For $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ as a metal source, the relatively high redox potential of $+ 0 . 3 4 \mathrm { V }$ vs. SHE results in a high driving force for the reduction by $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ , which has a redox potential of $- 1 . 2 4 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs. SHE [50]. In contrast, most of the other transition metals investigated as catalysts in AOPs have significantly lower redox potentials, such as $\mathrm { N i ^ { 2 + } }$ with $- 0 . 2 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs. SHE, which may limit their in situ solidification with $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ . Establishing this all-in-one method as a universal platform could enhance the accessibility of compositionally diverse porous catalytic membranes compared to other state-of-the-art fabrication approaches [24,45–47].

Therefore, in this work we aimed to demonstrate a certain universal applicability of our novel all-in-one membrane preparation by employing nickel ions as metal source. Additionally, we wanted to extend our fabrication towards complex catalytic membrane compositions with potentially optimized performance, such as metal–carbon-PES membranes. Combining a metal catalyst to activate an oxidant with carbon particles as an adsorbent within a membrane may increase the total elimination of organic micropollutants during the short residence time in flow-through. To achieve these goals, we first tested nickel acetate as a metal source for the in situ reduction. We then generated copper- and nickel-carbon composites directly in the PES-containing casting-reaction solution, thus introducing components with both catalytic and adsorbent function into the membranes. We further employed the as-prepared porous metal–carbon-PES membranes in the catalytic flow-through degradation of diclofenac in aqueous solution with persulfate as oxidant. We also investigated the mechanisms of the oxidative and adsorptive diclofenac removal, the influence of the nickel oxidation state, the persulfate activation pathways, and the impact of dissolved ions on the catalytic process.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Materials

Polyethersulfone (PES) Ultrason $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ 6020P was obtained from BASF. The carbon nanoparticles (Vulcan XC72) were received from the Fuel Cell Store. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) $( > 9 9 \% )$ , N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) $( > 9 9 \% )$ ), $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ $( > 9 9 \% )$ ), $\mathrm { K _ { 2 } S _ { 2 } O _ { 8 } }$ $( > 9 9 \% )$ and diclofenac sodium salt $\left( > 9 8 \% \right)$ ) were bought from Sigma Aldrich Co. Copper(II) acetate $\left( > 9 8 \% \right)$ ), nickel(II) acetate tetrahydrate $( > 9 8 \% )$ ), $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ $( > 9 9 . 5 \% )$ ) and NaCl $( > 9 9 . 5 \%$ ) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

# 2.2. All-in-one membrane fabrication

For the copper-PES $( M . C u O )$ and nickel-PES (M_NiO) membranes, PES $( 1 5 ~ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ and either copper acetate or nickel acetate tetrahydrate $( 0 . 3 5 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ metal) were dissolved in a 70/30 mixture of NMP/DMSO, followed by shaking overnight.

For the copper-carbon-PES $( M _ { - } C u O / C )$ and nickel-carbon-PES (M_NiO/C) membranes, PES $( 1 5 ~ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ , carbon nanoparticles $( 6 ~ \mathrm { w t } \%$ , Vulcan XC72) and either copper acetate or nickel acetate tetrahydrate $( 0 . 3 5 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ metal) were dissolved in a 70/30 mixture of NMP/DMSO, followed by shaking overnight.

Afterwards, all casting-reaction solutions were high-shear mixed for 5 min using a neoLab 7–2020 vortex mixer at $3 1 5 0 \mathrm { r p m }$ . Then, $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ (1 $\mathbf { w t \% }$ , ${ \sim } 5 ~ \mathrm { m o l }$ eq. to metal) and ethanol $( 5 ~ \mathrm { w t \% } )$ were added, and the casting-reaction solutions were again high shear mixed for $2 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ . This was followed by ultrasonication in a sonication bath for 2h at $3 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , 45 kHz and 100 W (Transonic TI-H-10 from Elma). Finally, the castingreaction solutions were degassed for $2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ in a vacuum oven at $5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and 50 mbar, high shear mixed for $1 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ to redisperse any potential sediment and then used for membrane preparation.

All membranes were prepared by film casting on a glass plate with a casting speed of $1 0 \ \mathrm { m m } / s$ and a film thickness of $1 0 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ using a COATMASTER film applicator and a fixed gap doctor blade (Model 360), both from Erichson (at room temperature of ${ \sim } 2 2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and at ${ \sim } 3 0 \%$ humidity). Afterwards, the cast films were directly immersed in a deionized (DI) water precipitation bath for $1 5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ (at room temperature), leading to membrane solidification through liquid non-solvent induced phase separation (NIPS). The fresh membranes were then placed in fresh DI water containing $0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { m M }$ NaOH (pH 9) and stored for at least 24h before use.

To generate the zerovalent nickel-carbon-PES membrane $( M . N ( O ) /$ $C )$ , samples of membrane M_NiO/C were incubated in $3 0 \mathrm { m M } \mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ in DI water for 1h, followed by washing in DI water for $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ .

For the metal content, samples were dried and weighted, and then degraded in $\mathrm { H N O _ { 3 } }$ for 24h. The amount of leached metal was quantified via atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) using a Thermo Scientific AA spectrometer M Series FS95. $$

The water permeance was determined by placing water-wet membrane samples in a dead-end filtration cell $3 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ active area), which was connected to a peristaltic pump. This was followed by measuring the transmembrane pressure after $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of DI water permeation at a fixed flux of $1 0 0 \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { h }$ . $$

$$

WP: water permeance, $V ;$ volume of permeated water, A: membrane area, t: time for permeation of volume V, P: transmembrane pressure.

For the through-pore diameter analysis via gas–liquid displacement, membrane samples were first dried, then wetted with Galwick (short chain length polymerized hexafluoropropene), and thereafter placed in a capillary flow porometer from PMI (CFP-34RTG8A-X-6-LA). Afterwards, the volumetric gas flow through the wetted membrane sample was determined at step-wise increasing nitrogen gas pressures, followed by measuring the gas flow at increasing nitrogen gas pressures for the fully dewetted sample. Using Washburn’s equation, the mean throughpore diameter was calculated from the nitrogen gas pressure at which the liquid displacement resulted in a gas flow through the wetted sample equal to $5 0 \%$ of that through the fully dewetted sample:

$$

$$

d: Through-pore diameter, $\gamma \colon$ Surface tension of wetting liquid, P: Transmembrane gas pressure difference, θ: Contact angle of wetting liquid inside pores $\mathcal { V }$ for Galwick).

# 2.4. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

For the SEM and EDX analysis, membrane samples were dried from water, frozen in liquid nitrogen and then broken to access the cross section. The samples were then sputter coated with a PdAu alloy and analysis was conducted on an Apreo S LoVac system from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The SEM images were obtained at a pressure of $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ Pa, an accelerating voltage of $5 \mathrm { k V }$ and a current of 13 pA. For EDX mapping, an accelerating voltage of $3 0 \mathrm { k V }$ was used.

Before TEM analysis, membrane samples were dried from water, immobilized in a polymer resin (Caldo Fix from Struers) and then sectioned to $8 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ slices using an ultramicrotome EM UC7/FC7 from Leica. Each slice was sputter coated with carbon and the TEM images were recorded on a JEOL JEM 2200 fs operating at $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { k V }$ .

# 2.5. Catalytic flow-through degradation of diclofenac with persulfate

For all experiments, water-wet membrane samples were used. Membrane samples were placed in a dead-end filtration module $3 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ active membrane area), and a peristaltic pump provided the feed at a constant flux of $1 0 0 \mathrm { L / m ^ { 2 } h }$ . Before each experiment, membrane samples were first washed with DI water for $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ in flow-through. Afterwards, the feed was changed to the reaction solution containing a combination of diclofenac, persulfate, and salts, depending on the respective reaction conditions. Permeates were gathered after single-pass through the membranes and diclofenac removal was quantified as described in Section 2.7.

After each experiment, the membrane sample was washed with DI water for 5 min in flow-through, followed by drying and weighting. The dry weight increase of the membrane sample after the experiment was calculated as the difference to the average dry weight of three pristine samples of the respective membrane type. It should be noted that the standard deviation of the referenced average dry weight of the three pristine samples was neglected for calculating the dry weight increase of the membrane sample after the experiment.

Additionally, we determined the remaining metal content in each membrane sample after the experiment via AAS as described in Section 2.3.

# 2.6. Catalytic batch degradation of diclofenac with persulfate

For all experiments, water-wet membrane samples with a diameter of $1 4 \mathrm { m m }$ were used. Membrane samples were placed in $1 0 \mathrm { m L }$ reaction solutions containing a combination of diclofenac, persulfate, and salts, depending on the respective reaction conditions. The batch reactions were conducted for 1h under constant shaking, and the reaction was stopped by removing the membrane sample from the solution. The diclofenac removal was quantified as described in Section 2.7.

After the reaction, each membrane sample was washed in DI water for $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , followed by drying and determination of the remaining metal content in each sample via AAS as described in Section 2.3.

The characterization of the commercial carbon nanoparticles (Vulcan XC72), which were used as additive for the all-in-one membrane fabrication, can be found in the Supporting Information (SI) in Section S1. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis demonstrated that the particles are graphitized and consist of sp2 carbon. # 3.2. Membrane characterization

The basic properties of all membranes are shown in Table 1 (determined after ${ > } 2 4 \mathrm { h }$ storage of the final membranes).

The membranes were prepared by liquid non-solvent induced phase separation (NIPS), which consists of film casting from the castingreaction solution and immersion of the cast film in a water precipitation bath (see Section 2.2) [54]. All membranes were cast with an initial film thickness of $1 0 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ but exhibited final thicknesses of approximately $5 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ after solidification (Table 1). This reduction in thickness is likely due to film thinning through solvent exchange in the water bath before the polymer solidification occurs.

The amount of carbon particles added to the casting-reaction solution was $2 8 ~ \mathrm { w t } \%$ relative to the total weight of all solids $\mathrm { ( P E S + m e t a l + }$ carbon particles, see Section 2.2). Both M_CuO/C and M_NiO/C exhibit a carbon particle content between $2 6 { - } 2 8 ~ \mathrm { w t \% }$ , demonstrating a quantitative incorporation of particles into the final membranes. Similarly, the amount of metal in the casting-reaction solutions relative to the total weight of all solids was $2 . 3 \mathrm { w t } \%$ for M_CuO and M_NiO, and $1 . 6 ~ \mathrm { w t } \%$ for $M _ { - } C u O / C$ and $M N i O / C$ . The actual metal contents of the final membranes are very close to these values. This underscores that our novel allin-one fabrication method achieves a nearly perfect material usage efficiency, meaning that all added metal ions were converted to solid metal during the in situ reduction and subsequently incorporated into the membranes during the NIPS process. This represents a considerable improvement compared to other state-of-the-art fabrication approaches for catalytic membranes. Additionally, this demonstrates that even metal ions with lower redox potentials, such as $\mathrm { N i ^ { 2 + } }$ , are solidified by $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 } ,$ , highlighting a certain universal applicability of our method. We also prepared a second sheet of the M_NiO/C membrane (approximately $2 5 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ , for which we determined a nickel content of $1 . 5 8 \pm 0 . 0 4 \mathrm { w t \% }$ and a nickel loading of $3 1 \pm 2 \mu \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ . These values match those of M_NiO/C stated in Table 1, which were obtained from the first prepared sheet, underscoring the reproducibility of our all-in-one fabrication. All final membranes further exhibit relatively high porosities and water permeances, indicating an open and accessible pore network. However, we observed that the nickel-containing membranes have smaller mean through-pore diameters than the copper-containing ones (cf. Equation 3 in Section 2.3).

The M_CuO, M_NiO and M_NiO/C membranes exhibit an as-expected anisotropic morphology with a pore size gradient from small pores in the upper region (first contact to water in the precipitation bath) to larger pores in the lower region, which results from the employed NIPS process [55]. In contrast, the M_CuO/C membrane formed an isotropic structure with a sponge-like pore system. A high content of dispersed solids in the casting solution increases the viscosity (e.g. $6 \ \mathrm { w t } \%$ carbon particles) [28], which can lead to a slower solvent exchange between cast film and water bath. This can promote water distribution through the cross section before the polymer precipitation point is reached, resulting in a delayed and therefore more or less simultaneous solidification in the whole film [56]. This known phenomenon could be the reason for the isotropic pore morphology of membrane M_CuO/C. A slower polymer precipitation can also increase the extent of film thinning, which aligns with the lower thickness of M_CuO/C (cf. Table 1). However, both $M _ { - } C u O / C$ and $M _ { - } N i O / C$ were fabricated identically, with the only difference that for M_NiO/C a hydrate-containing acetate was used as metal source (see Section 2.1 and 2.2). Water as additive in the casting solution facilitates a faster precipitation of the polymer in the water contacting region of the cast film [57]. This can promote the formation of an anisotropic pore structure and may explain the morphology of M_NiO/C. Similarly, the exemplary SEM images of the top membrane surfaces depict a seemingly denser top layer for membrane M_NiO compared to M_CuO (see SI, Section S4), which also aligns with the lower mean through-pore diameter of M_NiO (cf. Table 1). This supports our hypothesis that the hydrate content of the nickel salt leads to a faster polymer precipitation in contact to the water bath. Furthermore, small particles can be observed on the inner pore surface of $M _ { - } C u O / C$ and M_NiO/C (Fig. 2, right side), which illustrates the presentation of the incorporated carbon particles. Additionally, the EDX mapping demonstrates the successful uptake of solid metal into the final membranes, and a high dispersion through the whole cross section (Fig. 2, inset in lower left corner of each SEM image).

For M_NiO/C, we also investigated the nickel-carbon presentation via TEM and EDX (Fig. 3). For this, a membrane sample was immobilized in a polymer resin and sectioned to obtain slices with ${ 8 0 } \mathrm { n m }$ thickness (see Section 2.4). This means that the images in Fig. 3 depict the incorporation of nickel and carbon within the pore system of the as-prepared M_NiO/C membrane. However, this analysis is only a snapshot of the pore structure and may not be entirely representative of the whole membrane.

The exemplary TEM image in Fig. 3a shows small carbon nanoparticle clusters (dark grey) that are dispersed within the porous membrane matrix (light grey is the pore space and grey the polymer matrix). The carbon clusters are approximately $1 0 0 { - } 2 0 0 \mathrm { n m }$ in size, and individual carbon nanoparticles have diameters of about $5 0 \mathrm { n m }$ (see SI, Section S1 and S5). DF-STEM analysis revealed that the in situ reduction led to the formation of nickel nanorods (white), incorporated at the surface of a pore pathway (Fig. 3b, viewed along a narrowing pore pathway). This is also illustrated by the EDX mapping of Ni and S, showing the presentation of nickel at the boundary of the matrix polymer PES (PES is the only component that contains S).

The exemplary HR-TEM image of M_NiO/C in Fig. 3c further highlights the surface-accessible embedment of nickel nanorods directly in contact to the pore space. The image shows a nanorod with a high aspect ratio of 25 (approximately $4 0 \ \mathrm { n m }$ in length and $1 . 5 \ \mathrm { n m }$ in thickness). Additionally, we manually analyzed the size distribution of 20 exemplary nickel nanorods in $M _ { - } N i O / C$ using TEM images of larger regions within the membrane (see SI, Section S6). This yielded an average length of $4 1 \pm 1 0$ and average aspect ratio of $1 8 \pm 5$ . In comparison, for M_CuO/C we observed copper nanorods with an average length of ${ 1 0 9 \pm }$ 22 and average aspect ratio of ${ \bf 1 1 \pm 3 }$ . This aligns with the higher redox potential of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { 2 + } }$ compared to $\mathrm { N i ^ { 2 + } }$ , indicating that a higher driving force for the reduction by $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ results in a faster nanorod growth rate (cf. Section 2.2).

The analysis of larger regions inside the M_NiO/C membrane also revealed that most nickel nanorods are located either on or near carbon particles (see SI, Section S5 and S6). This suggests a certain level of direct nickel-carbon interaction, possibly promoted by carboxyl groups on the surface of the carbon particles (see SI, Section S1). However, the HR-TEM image of $M _ { - } N i O / C$ shows nickel nanorods in close proximity to carbon but without direct contact ${ \sim } 1 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ distance) (Fig. 3c). This indicates that only a limited amount of nickel is directly deposited onto carbon during the metal reduction in the casting-reaction solution (cf. Fig. 1). It also means that the observed proximity within the membrane may partially result from the rapid co-precipitation of the matrix polymer with dispersed nickel nanorods and carbon particles during membrane preparation. In contrast, the $M _ { - } C u O / C$ membrane exhibits a higher density of copper nanorods in certain regions, which, consequently, also led to areas with less metal near the carbon particles compared to M_NiO/C (see SI, Section S6). This could be explained by the slower polymer solidification during the fabrication of $M _ { - } C u O / C$ (cf. Fig. 2), which may extend the time available for copper nanorods to agglomerate.

In general, the formation of nanorods can likely be ascribed to the forces applied by the directional propagation of sound waves in the casting-reaction solution during the sonochemical in situ solidification of $\mathrm { N i ^ { 2 + } }$ and ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ with $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ (cf. Fig. 1 and Section 2.2). This demonstrates a certain universal applicability of our all-in-one fabrication method for synthesizing nanorods from metal ions, even when performed in the presence of additional carbon nanoparticles (a similar nickel nanorod formation to that in M_NiO/C was also observed for the M_NiO membrane without carbon particles, see Section S7 in the SI).

# 3.3. Evaluation in the catalytic flow-through degradation of diclofenac

To investigate the influence of the membrane composition on the catalytic performance, we employed all membranes in the flow-through degradation of diclofenac (DF) at $\tt p H 7$ using persulfate (PS) as oxidant (Fig. 4a). The aim of our investigation was to improve the total elimination of DF during flow-through. As indicator for the total DF elimination, we determined the aromatic content removal $( \mathrm { U V } _ { 2 7 0 \mathrm { n m } } ,$ see Section 2.7) for each permeate (all permeates were collected for 30 min).

All membranes exhibited a low DF adsorption capacity, which resulted in an initial aromatic removal degree of only ${ \sim } 1 0 \%$ $3 0 \mathrm { m i n } \AA$ ) in absence of PS and a saturation yielding a removal of less than $2 \%$ (120 min) at the end of the adsorption phase (Fig. 4b, without PS, $0 { \mathrm { - } } 1 2 0 \operatorname { m i n } ,$ . Interestingly, incorporating carbon particles into the $M _ { - } C u O / C$ and M_NiO/C membranes did not significantly increase the removal without PS, indicating that virtually no adsorption of DF onto the carbon particles occurred. In the presence of PS (120–270 min), the degree of aromatic removal significantly increased for all membranes, illustrating that the in situ synthesized copper and nickel species catalyze the PSfacilitated DF oxidation. Here, it is important to note that we did not observe any DF degradation by PS alone, and the aromatic content in the feed did not change during the investigated time frame.

M_CuO and M_NiO exhibited catalytic aromatic removal efficiencies between $2 0 { - } 4 0 \%$ (Fig. 4b). In contrast, $M _ { - } C u O / C$ and M_NiO/C achieved a near-complete aromatic content removal of ${ > } 9 7 \%$ in the first permeate after PS addition ( $\mathrm { 1 5 0 ~ m i n }$ ). Surprisingly, we further observed a sudden drop of removal, i.e. breakthrough of aromatic compounds, for these carbon-containing membranes. This behavior is typical for adsorption processes in flow-through, which are characterized by a marked increase in the adsorbate concentration in the permeate after the adsorption capacity is reached [58]. This signals a degradation-induced adsorption mechanism for the membranes $M _ { - } C u O / C$ and M_NiO/C. Without the oxidant PS, little DF was removed by adsorption (Fig. 4b, $0 { \mathrm { - } } 1 2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , which suggests that only certain catalytically formed oxidation products can effectively bind to the membranes. The membranes without carbon particles (M_CuO and M_NiO) did not exhibit this behaviour, which illustrates that the degradation-induced adsorption occurred selectively at the carbon particles. Additionally, we monitored the effective permeance of the membranes during the flow-through experiment (cf. Section 2.3). The highest loss occurred for the M_NiO/C membrane, but it was only about $2 0 \%$ after $2 5 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ compared to the pure water permeance (cf. Table 1). This decrease might be attributed to slight membrane compaction or an altered pore surface hydrophilicity caused by adsorbed degradation products. In any case, it implies that the membrane integrity was not significantly impacted by the oxidative conditions. We could also demonstrate that adsorbed aromatic products can be detached from samples of $M _ { - } C u O / C$ and M_NiO/C after a degradation experiment by washing them with aqueous NaOH in flow-through, but no release occurred in contact to DI water (see SI, Section S8).

# 3.4. Batch investigation of the catalytic mechanism

For M_NiO/C, which achieved the best overall DF elimination performance (cf. Fig. 4), we further investigated the catalytic mechanism of the DF degradation and the PS activation (Fig. 5). To elucidate the influence of the nickel oxidation state, we converted samples of the $M _ { - } N i O / C$ membrane into the $M _ { - } N i ( O ) / C$ state by reduction in aqueous $\mathrm { N a B H _ { 4 } }$ before use (see Section 2.2). Additionally, we tested the impact of $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ , $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - } ,$ and Cl- , three of the most common water constituents, on the DF elimination. All experiments were conducted in a batch setup by immersing membrane samples in the reaction solution (see Section 2.6). To quantify the catalytic performance, we determined the aromatic removal $\left( \mathrm { U V } _ { 2 7 0 \mathrm { n m } } \right)$ , TOC removal, nickel loss from the membrane samples, and colored product formation $\left( \mathsf { A } _ { 4 5 0 \mathrm { n m } } \right)$ , all after $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ of reaction time (see Section 2.7).

We observed that the degree of aromatic and TOC removal closely matched for each individual reaction (Fig. 5a). This aligns well with the proposed DF elimination by degradation-induced adsorption (cf. Fig. 4). Additionally, this may suggest that the degradation of aromatic residues is the final reaction step in the mineralization to $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ . For the M_NiO/C membrane, the TOC removal performance drastically declined from $6 0 \%$ to approximately $1 5 \%$ in the presence of ${ \mathrm { O H } } ^ { - } \left( { \mathrm { p H } } 1 0 \right)$ . However, this was accompanied by a significant release of colored degradation products $\left( { \mathrm { A } } _ { 4 5 0 \mathrm { n m } } \right)$ into the solution. This indicates that the oxidation of DF by catalytically generated radicals was not significantly inhibited (Fig. 5c, 1). Instead, the adsorption of degradation products onto the carbon particles (2) may be suppressed in the presence of $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ (3). Surprisingly, the addition of Cl- to $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ partially recovered the aromatic and TOC removal efficiency to $3 0 \%$ , while simultaneously reducing the release of products. One explanation could be that Cl- , which is a known sulfate radical scavenger [59], reacts with $\cdot S \dot { \mathrm { O _ { 4 } ^ { - } } }$ to form ⋅Cl. This may lead to the formation of hydrophobic chlorinated degradation products with a higher adsorption affinity to carbon in the presence of $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ (4) [60]. We also demonstrated that $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ and Cl- have the same effect on the aromatic removal performance of $M _ { - } N i O / C$ under flow-through conditions (see SI, Section S10). It should be noted that the exact nature of the active chlorine species formed in the presence of PS is still debated (potential reactive candidates include ⋅Cl, $\mathbf { \mathrm { C l } } _ { 2 } ^ { - }$ , $\mathrm { C l } _ { 2 }$ , ⋅HOCl- , ⋅ $\mathrm { C l O } _ { 2 }$ , and $\mathrm { C l O } { \bar { 3 } } )$ , but the generation of ⋅Cl is usually assumed to be the first step [61].

The presence of $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ (pH 8) drastically reduced both the TOC removal performance and the product formation for M_NiO/C, indicating that the catalytic oxidation of DF was suppressed (Fig. 5a). Other investigations have shown that $\mathrm { \cdot H C O _ { 3 } }$ exhibits reaction rates with most organic molecules that are approximately 1–5 orders of magnitude lower than those of $\cdot S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ and ⋅Cl [62]. This suggests that $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ scavenges sulfate radicals (5), reducing their concentration and, therefore, the degradation rate of DF. Moreover, the addition of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ to $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ also partially recovered the TOC removal. This supports the proposed mechanism in which Cl- reacts with $\cdot S \dot { \mathrm { O _ { 4 } ^ { - } } }$ to form ⋅Cl, thereby reducing the formation of the less reactive $\cdot \mathrm { H C O _ { 3 } }$ through competition [62]. We further observed a similar amount of nickel leaching $( \sim 5 0 \% )$ from membrane M_NiO/C for all reactions with PS (all except “Adsorption”), indicating that variations in catalyst stability did not contribute to the performance differences. In contrast, the DF removal efficiency of the $M _ { - } N i ( O ) / C$ membrane was not significantly impacted by the presence of $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ and Cl- , which suggests that zerovalent nickel does not release ${ \cdot } S 0 _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ from PS under these conditions (Fig. 5a). Additionally, the PSfacilitated metal leaching was completely mitigated for M_Ni(0)/C, but only when in presence of $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ .

In contrast, the $M _ { - } N i ( O ) / C$ membrane did not release any sulfate from PS, but significant PS cleavage occurred in the presence of $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ and Cl- . Overall, these observations align with a non-radical pathway in which zerovalent nickel activates surface-bound PS, enabling the oxidation of DF by a direct electron transfer (II). Consequently, PS cleavage should not occur without an additional reaction partner, but sulfate can be released by reaction of PS with a suitable electron donor such as $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ or Cl- (III). This could also explain the high stability of zerovalent nickel in the presence of both PS and $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ . Nickel leaching likely occurs only after Ni(0) is oxidized by PS to soluble highvalent species. When in contact with $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ , the electron transfer from $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ to PS may be preferred over that from $\mathrm { N i } ( 0 )$ .

# 3.5. Continuous DF elimination via catalytic degradation-induced adsorption

We further investigated the continuous TOC removal performance of the $M _ { - } N i O / C$ and $M _ { - } N i ( O ) / C$ membranes in the PS-facilitated flowthrough oxidation of different DF concentrations in presence of $\mathbf { 3 } \mathrm { m M }$ $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ and 2 mM NaCl (Fig. 6). Similar to the experiments in Fig. 5, we observed matching aromatic and TOC removal degrees for the flowthrough conditions investigated here (see Section S11 in the SI for the aromatic removal).

Surprisingly, at $2 0 \mathrm { \ m g / L }$ DF, both the M_NiO/C and $M _ { - } N i ( O ) / C$ membranes achieved an almost complete TOC removal for the first permeate and around $8 0 \%$ for the second permeate (Fig. 6a). This contrasts with their significantly different catalytic performances in the batch experiments (cf. Fig. 5). This could indicate an overall higher DF oxidation rate in flow-through due to enhanced mass transport, resulting in a catalytic activity that is not limiting for the DF removal by degradation-induced adsorption. Specifically, the TOC removal observed for M_NiO/C in the batch experiment corresponds to a total elimination rate of $1 . 3 ~ \mathrm { m } \mathrm { g }$ DF per mg Ni per hour (cf. Fig. 5a, $\cdot _ { + \mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - } }$ $+ \mathbf { C l } ^ { - _ { 4 4 } } )$ , while the TOC removal for the first permeate in the flow-through degradation yields a rate of $5 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m g / m g / h }$ . Furthermore, in all flow–through experiments a rapid increase in the cumulative amount of totally eliminated DF occurred until approximately $1 . 7 5 \mathrm { \textrm { g } } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ was reached (Fig. 6a, right). This illustrates that this degradation-adsorption capacity was not influenced by the nickel oxidation state or the DF concentration. This capacity, however, was obtained in the presence of $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ and NaCl, and is significantly lower than the $4 . 7 5 \ : \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ that we observed for the $M N i O / C$ membrane in the flow-through experiment without dissolved salts (cf. Fig. 4). This can likely be ascribed to an inhibited interaction between degradation products and carbon particles in presence of additional ions. Surprisingly, in the batch experiments, we only observed indications of a radical scavenging effect of $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ and not an obvious influence on the degradation-induced adsorption (cf. Fig. 5). The TOC removal degrees in the batch experiments correspond to a cumulative total DF elimination of less than $\scriptstyle { 1 \ g / \mathbf { m } ^ { 2 } }$ , meaning that the degradation-adsorption capacity was not reached and, therefore, was not a limiting factor.

# 3.6. Comparison with other state-of-the-art catalytic membranes

To further highlight the exceptional performance of our all-in-one fabricated membranes, we compared our results to these of other stateof-the-art catalytic membranes from literature (Fig. 7) [31–41]. For this comparison, we only considered studies that employed metal-containing membranes in a catalytic flow-through degradation of organic micropollutants in water in the presence of an oxidant (for a detailed overview, see Section S12 in the SI). Another prerequisite was that either the organic carbon content, the chemical oxygen demand, or the aromatic content (UV absorbance between $2 5 0 { - } 2 7 0 \mathrm { n m }$ ) of treated permeates was analysed and reported in the study. We then calculated the total elimination degree from the highest reported decrease in any of these values after the oxidative flow-through degradation.

Additionally, to quantify the activity of the catalytic species, we calculated the turnover frequency (TOF) as follows:

TOF $=$ amount eliminated organic C [mg C] / amount catalyst in membrane [mg] / time [h] (6)

Moreover, we calculated the reaction rate as follows:

Reaction rate $=$ elimination $[ \% ] ^ { * }$ organic C concentration [mg/L C] / residence time [s] (7)

Comparing the total elimination performance of all catalytic membranes to the residence time in each membrane revealed an apparent upper boundary for the mineralization degree that can be obtained at a given residence time (Fig. 7a, logarithmic scale for residence time).

For mineralization to occur, multiple consecutive oxidative degradation steps are needed, which explains that the residence time in the membrane is the limiting factor for this process. Surprisingly, not one of the 11 literature examples significantly surpassed the apparent upper boundary, even though the data includes experiments with a wide range of organic micropollutant concentrations for similar residence times (see SI, Section S12). Similarly, for M_Ni(0)/C, we observed a TOC removal degree of approximately $1 0 \%$ at $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ DF and $^ { 1 3 \% }$ at $1 0 ~ \mathrm { m g / L }$ DF after the degradation-adsorption capacity was saturated (in presence of $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ and NaCl, cf. Fig. 6). This phenomenon could be the result of the interaction equilibrium of degradation products with the catalyst in the membrane. To achieve full mineralization, degradation products released from one catalyst site may need to interact with another site, allowing the subsequent oxidation step to occur before the molecule passes the membrane. The driving force for this interaction with active sites is the locally available amount of formed degradation product, which should be larger when the pollutant concentration in the feed is higher. In other words, this could mean that the absolute mineralization rate increases or decreases in line with the pollutant concentration, resulting in a partially concentration-independent mineralization degree.

However, both our carbon particle-containing membranes $( M _ { - } C u O / C$ and M_NiO/C, data from Fig. 4) surpassed this apparent upper boundary, fully eliminating ${ > } 9 7 \%$ of DF in presence of PS in flow-through at a residence time of only ${ \sim } 1 . 5 s$ in the membranes. This performance can likely be ascribed to the synergistic combination of a partial mineralization of DF and adsorption of the remaining degradation products at the incorporated carbon particles. In contrast, M_CuO and M_NiO exhibited a total elimination performance exactly at the upper boundary, indicating that mineralization of DF was limited by the residence time. Additionally, even in the flow-through degradation of DF in presence of $\mathrm { N a H C O _ { 3 } }$ and NaCl (data from Fig. 6), the nickel-carbon-PES membrane achieved a combination of TOF and reaction rate that significantly surpassed the performance of all the other state-of-the-art catalytic membranes (red in Fig. 7b, double logarithmic scale). After the degradation-adsorption capacity was reached, the remaining TOF and reaction rate were still considerably higher than these of most membranes from literature (light red in Fig. 7b). Moreover, in the flowthrough removal of bisphenol A using the nickel-carbon-PES membrane, we observed minimal removal through degradation-induced adsorption (Section S9 in the SI, without salts). Nevertheless, the lowest catalytic elimination degree we observed for bisphenol A $( \sim 3 0 \%$ , at the end of the experiment; blue in Fig. 7b) corresponds to a TOF that is an order of magnitude higher than the next highest TOF value of the other state-ofthe-art membranes. This highlights a generally outstanding catalytic activity of the incorporated nickel species, which can be ascribed to our all-in-one fabrication, resulting in the formation of nanorods with a high aspect ratio (cf. Fig. 3) and the dispersion of immobilized nickel throughout the cross-section of the membrane (cf. Fig. 2).

# 4. Conclusion

We present a novel all-in-one fabrication of compositionally diverse metal-decorated porous catalytic polyethersulfone (PES) membranes. This method is based on the sonochemical in situ solidification of a metal salt with $\mathrm { N a B H } _ { 4 }$ in a PES-based casting-reaction solution, directly followed by film casting cum phase separation. We showed that metal nanorods can be obtained through the in situ reduction of metal ions with both high $( \mathsf { C u } ^ { 2 + } )$ and low $( \mathrm { N i } ^ { 2 + } )$ reduction potentials. We further demonstrated the possibility to incorporate additional functional carbon nanoparticles into the catalytic membranes by simply blending them with the casting-reaction solution.

We found that the combination of nickel as catalytic metal species and carbon particles as an adsorbent in the membrane enabled a highly efficient total elimination of diclofenac (DF) during the catalytic flowthrough oxidation with persulfate (PS). A comprehensive comparison with the existing literature revealed that our all-in-one fabricated catalytic membranes could significantly outperform other state-of-the-art metal-decorated membranes by utilizing a degradation-induced adsorption. We illustrated that small degradation products adsorb onto the incorporated carbon particles while the rest of each DF molecule is catalytically mineralized. Our data further suggests that NiO activates PS through a redox-mediated radical pathway, which switched to a non-radical pathway after a reductive pretreatment to Ni(0). Additionally, the stability of Ni(0) in the presence of PS is significantly enhanced when also in contact to $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - } ,$ , which was not the case for NiO. This indicates that $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ acts as a sacrificial electron donor towards PS, mitigating the oxidation and accompanied leaching of nickel.

However, despite this outstanding performance, it is important to note that the membranes presented here would not yet be suitable for long-term application. While we observed a stable performance of $M _ { - } N i$ $( O ) / C$ within a time frame of hours, the redox potential of persulfate $^ { + 2 }$ V vs. SHE) will inevitably lead to the oxidation of Ni(0) to $\mathrm { N i ^ { 2 + } }$ over time $( - 0 . 2 6 \ : \mathrm { V }$ vs. SHE). Nevertheless, this study presents a novel fabrication method and a promising application approach. Based on these new insights, the next step towards an applicable wastewater treatment could be the fabrication of zerovalent noble metal–carbon-PES membranes. A potential metal candidate would be Au, with a redox potential of $+ 1 . 8 3$ V vs. SHE. This may prevent metal oxidation by PS during a long-term catalytic flow-through water treatment, which could potentially also enable a regeneration of the degradation-adsorption capacity after saturation.
