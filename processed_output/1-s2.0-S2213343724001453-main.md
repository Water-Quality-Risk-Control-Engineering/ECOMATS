# . Introduction

The activation of inorganic peroxides such as peroxymonosulfate (PMS) [16], peroxydisulfate (PDS) [7,8], or hydrogen peroxide $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } \right)$ by carbonaceous materials to degrade organic pollutants in water is known as carbocatalysis [9]. Recently carbocatalysis using wood sawdust (an abundant solid waste of the forestry industry that can cause serious health and environmental issues due to inadequate disposals [1012]) as a carbonaceous material precursor has been used for the production of degrading species (e.g., free radicals) [8,13]. In addition, the effect of modifications of carbonaceous materials with transition metals such as Fe and Mn has been reported. For example, Huang et al., 2020 synthesized an Mn-doped magnetic biochar from bamboo waste to activate PDS [14]. According to characterization, such a modified material presented oxygen functional groups, defects in the carbonaceous structure, and metal oxides, which are the relevant sites for the activation of PDS. Moreover, they found that hydroxyl (HO•) and sulfate $( \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - } )$ radicals participated in the degradation of the target pollutant (tetracycline) [14]. Furthermore, Zheng et al., 2021 prepared a carbonaceous material from sawdust doped with Fe and N that was evaluated for PMS activation toward tetracycline removal, showing that the superoxide anion radical $\left( \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - } \right)$ and singlet oxygen $( ^ { 1 } 0 _ { 2 } )$ played the most important role in the removal of the pollutant. Moreover, $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 0 } / \mathrm { F e } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C } ,$ N-based moieties were the dominant active sites for PMS activation [15].

In carbocatalysis, aspects such as pollutant, oxidant, and catalyst concentrations, the effect of pH, matrix effects, and reuse of catalyst have been widely tested. Also, adsorption works using materials prepared from sawdust are very common [16]. However, to our best knowledge, the use of carbonaceous materials synthesized from sawdust wood and modified with manganese for the activation of PMS has not been evaluated yet. Furthermore, the previous works about carbocatalysis using metals-modified materials have considered the ability of the material to activate PMS, but the role of the inorganic peroxide as an activating agent of the carbonaceous material (which leads to the material itself degrading pollutants) has not been investigated.

On the other hand, the combination of carbocatalysis with ultrasound waves is gaining the attention of the scientific community because this can significantly increase the production of species useful for degrading target pollutants in water [1721]. The interaction of ultrasound waves with a liquid medium induces the formation, pulsation, and implosion of bubbles (i.e., acoustic cavitation). The implosion leads to the cleavage of water and oxygen molecules, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as HO• [22]. Therefore, upon water sonication, HO• attacks pollutants at the solution-bubble interface (typically for hydrophobic non-volatile compounds) or the solution bulk (for very hydrophilic compounds). Additionally, volatile compounds can be pyrolyzed inside the bubbles [23,24]. Besides, the turbulence generated by ultrasound waves increases mass transfer [25], and decreases the particle size of solids [26], and ultrasound waves can also induce the breakdown of peroxide bonds (as presented in PMS), forming extra radical species [27]. It is important to remark that neither the combination of metal-modified carbonaceous material with low or high-frequency ultrasound waves (to promote synergistic effects in the removal of pollutants), nor the application of carbocatalysis to real water have been informed in previous investigations.

In this work, a carbonaceous material (SW-Mn), prepared from sawdust and modified with manganese carbonate, was tested as a carbocatalyst for activation of PMS to eliminate a relevant contaminant of emerging concern (the pharmaceutical ciprofloxacin, CIP, see Text S1 in Supporting information). Initially, the synthesized SW-Mn was characterized using Brunauer-Emmett-Teller surface area analyses (BET), Fourier-transformed infrared spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Then, the activating capability of SW-Mn toward PMS was assessed and compared with the non-modified carbonaceous material (SW), showing that SW-Mn had a superior catalytic action for the elimination of the target pollutant. Afterward, the degradation of CIP by the carbocatalytic system (using SW-Mn) in combination with ultrasonic waves at low ( $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ , which mainly provides mechanical effects) and high-frequency $( 3 7 5 \mathrm { k H z }$ , which mainly induces chemical effects) was assessed, evidencing higher synergistic degradation for the combination with US at $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ For the combined system (i.e., the sonocarbocatalytic treatment), the effects of catalyst dose, $\mathrm { p H }$ ,matrix effects (using irrigation crop water), and reuse were tested, identifying the parameters that strongly influenced the performance of the degrading process. Moreover, the ROS involved in the sonocarbocatalysis and the activation routes were determined using specific scavengers for $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ , $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , HO•, carrying out electrochemical and XPS analyses. It was found that the non-radical pathways predominated in the CIP degradation (i.e., singlet oxygen from PMS activation plus oxidation by $\mathrm { M n ^ { 4 + } }$ coming from the interaction of SW-Mn with PMS). The primary transformations of the model pollutant were established, depicting that $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ attacked the piperazine ring of CIP (which is a moiety determinant for the biological activity of the pollutant). Finally, the extent of the sonocarbocatalytic treatment was determined by measuring changes in chemical oxygen demand (COD), antimicrobial activity (AA), and phytotoxicity. It was evidenced that this sonocarbocatalytic process effectively oxidized CIP (as supported by a decrease in COD), and diminished the AA and toxicity, indicating positive environmental effects of CIP degradation by a system based on a carbonaceous material prepared from industrial wastes.

# 3.1. Characterization of the synthesized SW-Mn

Firstly, the carbonaceous material modified with manganese (SWMn) was compared to their corresponding precursors (i.e., the raw sawdust (RW) and carbonaceous one (SW)), in terms of the surface area. As shown in Table S2 and Fig. S1-S3, $\pmb { S } _ { \mathrm { B E T } }$ values were 4.0, 10, and $1 0 4 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ the carbonization (formation of SW) and the modification of SW with $\bf { M n C O _ { 3 } }$ (production of SW-Mn) led to an increase in the $S _ { \mathrm { B E T } }$ regarding the raw material (RW).

Carbonization of the sawdust wastes increases the surface area because the high temperatures in this preparation method favor the release of volatile compounds influencing the formation of pores [2830]. The further increase in the surface area with the incorporation of manganese can be associated with an accelerating function of $\bf { M n C O _ { 3 } }$ in the formation of pores [31]. Also, it is important to mention that oxygen atoms (e.g., alcohol and carboxylate groups) present in SW, can promote metallic oxides incorporation [32,33]. Thereby, SW impregnated with $\bf { M n C O _ { 3 } }$ developed a high surface area after the pyrolysis. Additionally, the general stability sequence of high-spin (usually having octahedral metals) complexes for the replacement of water by other ligands, according to the Irving-Williams series, favors the Mn (II) incorporation [34]. Likewise, the fast diffusion of metal ions from the solution and the decrease in the distance of the metal and ligands can be favored by the structure of the highly porous carbon during pyrolysis.

After evidencing the relevant role of carbonization and Mnmodification on the surface area of the carbonaceous material, Raman, FT-IR, and SEM analyses for both SW and SW-Mn were carried out. The FT-IR for SW (Fig. S4) showed a broad peak around $3 2 7 7 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , associated with the presence of OH groups (belonging to alcohols, phenols, and carboxylic acids) [35]. The vibrations at $1 2 0 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 5 8 4 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ were assigned to C-O and $\mathrm { c } { = } \mathrm { c }$ (Fig. S4); which are typical signals for alcohols, carboxylic acids, and alkenes [35]. In turn, Fig. 1a shows the FT-IR spectra for SW-Mn. The weak peaks around $1 5 7 0 \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 0 5 6 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ were related to vibrations of $\mathrm { - C = C }$ -C-O, and Si-OH functional groups [36,37]. Two peaks, observed in $4 8 1 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ ,and $6 0 4 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (which are not observed for SW, Fig. S4), corresponded to $\mathtt { M n - O }$ vibrations [3840], indicating that manganese was effectively incorporated in the carbonaceous structure.

To understand the origin of the synergy, some characteristics of the carbonaceous materials must be considered. The FT-IR analysis for SW (Fig. S4) shows the presence of oxygenated functional moieties such as ketone, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups provided sW some properties as adsorbent [37]and carbocatalyst [59,60]. For instance, ketone moieties are electron-rich reactive sites able to activate PMS [61]. In turn, SW-Mn has a ten-fold higher surface area than SW (Table S2), which offers more sites available for CIP adsorption. Also, it is proposed that the incorporation of manganese species provides SW-Mn more catalytical points for PMS activation. According to the characterization of SW-Mn (Fig. 1a and Table 1), manganese (II) is embedded in the carbonaceous material making it favorable to interact with PMS [53]. Details about the activation mechanism (which is linked to the synergistic effects) are deeply discussed in Section 3.3.

Because of the synergistic degradation of CIP using SW-Mn and PMS (Fig. 2a), the influence of operational parameters such as the SW-Mn dose $( 0 . 0 { - } 0 . 5 \ \mathbf { g } \ \mathbf { L } ^ { - 1 } )$ on the carbocatalytic system was evaluated. The evolutions of both CIP (Fig. 2b) and PMS (Fig. 2c) were followed. In the absence of the catalyst (i.e., $0 . 0 \ \mathrm { g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ of SW-Mn) very small elimination of CIP and consumption of PMS was found. This indicates that under the tested conditions, the contribution to the degradation of CIP by direct oxidation with PMS is negligible. However, the PMS concentration significantly decreased, and the CIP degradation was improved as the SW-Mn dose increased (Figs. 2b and 2c). The increment in the carbocatalyst amount promotes an increase in PMS consumption because a higher SW-Mn concentration brings more useful sites to active PMS. Consequently, more degradation of CIP is observed (Fig. 2b). Our results agree with the information reported by Lui et al., 2023, where the carbocatalytic elimination of CIP, using porous Mn/N co-doped biochar, is also enhanced by increasing the catalyst concentration [62].

# 3.2.2. Sonocarbocatalysis

As a strategy to improve the performance of the carbocatalytic system (i.e., $\mathbf { S W - M n } + \mathbf { P M S } )$ , the addition of ultrasound waves to the reaction system (to generate sonocarbocatalysis) was evaluated. It is wellknown that ultrasound frequency has an important role in the elimination of contaminants [63]. Hence, the influence of the ultrasound frequency on the performance of the sonocarbocatalytic process was determined. Fig. 3a compares CIP removal after $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of treatment by ultrasound alone and sonocarbocatalysis at 40 or $3 7 5 \mathrm { k H z }$ The synergy index for the combination at both frequencies was also calculated (Fig. 3a).

At high-frequency ultrasound (i.e., $3 7 5 \mathrm { k H z } )$ , $3 4 . 9 \%$ of CIP was eliminated by the action of ultrasound alone (US), contrasting with the non-elimination of the antibiotic at the low-frequency tested (i.e., $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ ). According to the literature, in the sonochemical systems, at low-frequency ultrasound $( < 1 0 0 \ \mathrm { k H z } )$ , the physical effects (e.g., stirring and shock waves) are predominant. On the contrary, at high-frequency ultrasound $( > 2 0 0 \ \mathrm { k H z } )$ the chemical effects (e.g., formation of radicals by water molecules cleavage, Eq. 2) are preponderant [64]. Therefore, the degradation of the CIP by ultrasound alone at $3 7 5 \mathrm { k H z }$ is favored by the attacks of the sonogenerated radicals. As CIP is a hydrophilic compound $( \mathrm { L o g } ~ \mathrm { K o w } = 0 . 2 7 )$ , its sonodegradation should take place at the solution bulk [65]. In contrast, a direct sonodegradation by the ultrasound system at $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ was not observed (Fig. 3a), this is due to the low capacity of this system to generate radicals from the water cleavage (Eq. 2).

In fact, the control system PMS + US (Fig. S8) eliminated ${ \sim } 2 8 \%$ and $5 5 \%$ of CIP at low frequency, and high frequency, respectively. At low frequencies, the cavitation bubble radius is in the range of ${ \sim } 1 0 { \mathrm { - } } 1 0 0 \ \mu \mathrm { m }$ [69]. The SW-Mn catalyst has $1 6 4 \mu \mathrm { m }$ of average size (which was obtained from the Image J analysis [70]). Thus, this material is mainly placed far away from the cavitation bubbles, showing low interference. Furthermore, at $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ , there are strong mechanical effects of these ultrasonic waves that favor the mass transfer and disaggregation of particles [20,21,71], which enhance the interaction of SW-Mn with PMS, thus leading to the synergistic effect $( S = 1 . 6 5 $ $\pm \ : 0 . 0 7 4 )$ .

At high frequencies, the particles may induce a higher attenuation of the ultrasound wave field (bubbles radius $< 1 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ , [24]). Additionally, the physical effects are much weaker at high-frequency than at low-frequency ultrasound [72]. Thereby, at $3 7 5 \mathrm { k H z }$ , it is found a quasi-antagonistic effect $( \mathbf { S } { = } 0 . 9 3 \pm 0 . 0 1 0 )$ for the sonocarbocatalytic system. Considering such aspects, the sonocarbocatalytic system at $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ was selected to develop the subsequent steps of the research.

# 3.2.3.Understanding theorigi fynerg

To understand the origin of the synergy in the sonocarbocatalytic system using $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ , the different subsystems (i.e., PMS, US, SW-Mn, PMS $^ +$ US, SW-Mn $^ +$ US, and $\mathbf { S W - M n } \ + \ \mathbf { P M S } )$ were considered and compared (Fig. 3c). Also, the PMS consumption in these subsystems was followed (Fig. 3d). As discussed in the previous sections, US $( 4 0 \mathrm { k H z } )$ or PMS alone did not achieve a significant degradation of CIP.

The adsorption of the pollutant on SW-Mn achieved ${ \sim } 4 6 \%$ after $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ (Fig. 3c). It must be mentioned that according to the point of zero charges (PZC) of SW-Mn (which is 7.5, see Text S1.3 and Fig. S9) and the experimental pH (5.8), the material has positive surface charges. Meanwhile, at pH 5.8, CIP $( \mathrm { p K } _ { \mathrm { a 1 } } = 5 . 9 0 \pm 0 . 1 5 $ and $\mathrm { p K } _ { \mathrm { a 2 } } = 8 . 8 8 \pm 0 . 1 1$ is protonated at the piperazine ring (i.e., this moiety is positively charged), while ${ \sim } 5 0 \%$ of the carboxylic groups are deprotonated (negatively charged) (Fig. S10). Then, the adsorption process can take place by electrostatic interaction between the negative carboxylic groups on CIP and the positive charges of the SW-Mn surface. Also, hydrogen bonding through the carboxylate group on CIP, and silanol and alcohol groups of SW-Mn can occur [7375]. Additionally, $\pi { - } \pi$ interactions between the $\mathsf { s p } ^ { 2 }$ carbons in the aromatic ring of SW-Mn and the benzene ring of CIP are also possible [76].

In the case of carbocatalysis (i.e., $S W \mathbf { \bar { - M } n } + \mathbf { P M S } )$ , the interaction of the carbonaceous material with PMS led to a high decomposition of the inorganic peroxide (as indicated by the low residual concentration of PMS $( \sim 0 . 2 \mathrm { m M } )$ for this system in Fig. 3d). This suggests that the combination of PMS with SW-Mn promotes the generation of degrading ROS. Consequently, the carbocatalytic degradation of the antibiotic is much higher than those obtained from the single adsorption or direct oxidation by PMS alone (Fig. 3c); thus, denoting the synergy of the combination of PMS with SW-Mn.

On the other hand, the combination of US with PMS improved the

CIP removal (even increased the PMS consumption) concerning the individual US and PMS systems (Figs. 3c and 3d). This can be associated with both an enhancement of contact between PMS and CIP (by the shaking forces of the US) and/or homolysis of PMS by US to produce radicals (Eq. 3). Meanwhile, the ultrasound did not influence the adsorption of the antibiotic on the material. In fact, the adsorption of the pollutant on SW-Mn without and with ultrasound was very similar ${ \sim } 4 6 \%$ after $3 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ in both cases, Fig. 3c).

The ultrasound waves can induce other physical effects on the carbonaceous material. To unravel such effects, the particle size before and after the sonocarbocatalytic process (i.e., $\mathsf { \widehat W \mathrm { - } M n } + \mathsf { P M S + U S } )$ , was studied through DLS (Fig. S11). Interestingly, a decrease in particle size was observed after the treatment, indicating that ultrasound waves at $4 0 \mathrm { k H z }$ disaggregate the particles or break the solid into smaller particles [20,21,71]. The disaggregation or breaking of the solid may increase the number of available catalytic sites to activate PMS toward the formation of degrading species. This is supported by a rise in the consumption of PMS, as evidenced in the sonocarbocatalysis process regarding the carbocatalysis system (Fig. 3d). Thereby, the combination of carbocatalysis and ultrasound process has a positive and synergistic effect on the removal of the target pollutant.

# 3.3. Identification of ROS and reaction mechanism during the sonocarbocatalytic treatment

# 3.3.1. Role of reactive oxygen species

A study, using scavengers to elucidate the participation of different ROS (such as hydroxyl radical, sulfate radical, superoxide radical anion, and singlet oxygen), was carried out. Table S4 presents some properties of the quenching agents used for the identification of the role of ROS in the elimination of the CIP by sonocarbocatalysis. Methanol (MeOH), and tert-butanol (TBA) were utilized as a scavenger for $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ , and HO'; while $p$ -benzoquinone (BQ), and sodium aside $\mathrm { ( N a N _ { 3 } ) }$ were the quenchers of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and $\mathrm { \bar { \Delta } _ { 1 } } _ { \mathrm { O _ { 2 } } }$ ,respectively.

Fig. 4a shows the CIP elimination in the presence of diverse scavengers. When BQ was added to the sonocarbocatalytic system CIP degradation was similar to the obtained in the scavenger absence, indicating that $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ^ { \bullet - }$ did not participate significantly in the removal of the pollutant. MeOH inhibited CIP removal slightly, suggesting low participation of $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ and HO• in the process (Eq. 3). TBA did not inhibit CIP elimination, and considering that TBA has higher selectivity toward HO• $( \mathrm { k _ { H O } } { \bullet } = 6 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 } \mathrm { \bf ~ M } ^ { - 1 } s ^ { - 1 } )$ compared to $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ $( \mathrm { k } _ { \mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { * - } } = 8 . \overset { \cdot } { 4 } \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } ~ \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ $s ^ { - 1 } ) [ 7 8 ]$ , this suggests that $\mathrm { S O } _ { 4 } ^ { \bullet - }$ ,evn to a lowe extent, participates in CIP degradation. Meanwhile, in the presence of $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ , the pollutant elimination was considerably decreased, and despite sodium azide can also directly react with PMS [79,80], this result suggests the participation of singlet oxygen. Also, we must mention that in a recent study from our team using SW-Mn and PMS, it was evidenced the generation of singlet oxygen using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), as a complementary analysis to scavenging tests with $\mathrm { N a N } _ { 3 }$ [81]. Furthermore, only one degradation by-product was predominantly detected (Fig. S13a) and its chromatographic area decreased when adding sodium azide (Fig. S13b). These results also indicate that $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ played the main degrading role in the sonocarbocatalytic treatment.

Previous studies have reported that the defects in carbonaceous materials can contribute to the generation of $^ 1 \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ In fact, it has been indicated that the decrease in the $\mathrm { I _ { D } / I _ { G } }$ ratio after treatment is evidence of $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ participation [82,83]. From the results of Fig. 4a, it is also worth noting that even if $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ plays an important role during CIP degradation ( ${ \sim } 2 5 \%$ of reduction of CIP degradation is achieved in the presence of sodium azide) and sulfate radicals also contribute to a lower extent $5 \%$ of CIP reduction in presence of tert-butanol); other oxidative species (e.g., $\scriptstyle = \mathbf { M } \mathbf { n } ^ { 4 + }$ could also participate in the process. Interestingly, the sonocarbocatalytic system induces very low leaching of Mn into the solution $( 0 . 3 6 1 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ , $0 . 0 7 \%$ of Mn), suggesting that homogenous catalysis can be neglected in this process.

The deconvolution of orbital $ { \mathrm { M n } } 2  { \mathrm { p } } ^ { 3 / 2 }$ indicated the presence of MnO and $\mathbf { M n O } _ { 2 }$ in SW-Mn after treatment (Fig. 4b). # 3.4. The extent of the sonocarbocatalytic treatment

3.4.1. Reuse cycles, antimicrobial activity evolution, and phytotoxicity of treated solution

After understanding the activation mechanisms involved in the sonocarbocatalytic process, the reuse of the SW-Mn was evaluated. Reusability is an important property owing to this can provide information about the deactivation or poisoning of the catalyst [96]. The SW-Mn material was tested during three cycles of use (Text S1.4), comparing the adsorption (a classical system for carbonaceous materials) and the sonocarbocatalysis processes proposed herein (Fig. 7). As seen, the adsorption process efficiency drastically diminished after the second and third reuse cycles (only $2 0 . 7 \%$ and $1 2 . 1 \%$ of CIP removal, respectively, Fig. 7a). This can be associated to a covering/blockage of the adsorption sites by the pollutant after use [61], which is evidenced by the decrease in $S _ { \mathrm { B E T } }$ (Fig. S17) from $1 0 4 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } \ : \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ (before use) to $2 9 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ $\mathbf { g } ^ { - 1 }$ (after third reuse).

In sonocarbocatalysis, the elimination efficiency was also decreased in the reuse of SW-MN. In fact, for the second and third reuse cycles, the CIP eliminations by sonocarbocatalysis were $5 4 . 1 \%$ and $6 0 . 4 \%$ , respectively. Moreover, PMS consumption decreased during the reuse cycles too (Fig. S18). Despite this, these results highlighted that the sonocarbocatalysis efficiency is maintained at ${ \sim } 6 0 \%$ even after the third use of SW-Mn, contrasting to the observed for the adsorption process.

In the case of adsorption, the sites are occupied by CIP, thus limiting the adsorption of other molecules of the pollutant after each reuse cycle.

It is important to take into account that the pH also influences the surface charge of the catalyst. As SW-Mn has a PZC value of 7.5, at the acidic pH, a lower CIP adsorption is expected by electrostatic repulsion (due to the contaminant is protonated on the piperazine ring, and the SW-Mn is charged positively). Similarly, when the $\mathrm { p H } > \mathrm { P Z C }$ (e.g., at pH 9.0) the catalytic material surface is negatively charged and the anionic form of CIP predominates, also disfavoring the CIP adsorption. Therefore, the improvement of CIP degradation and PMS consumption observed in Fig. 8b at pH values of 3.0 and 9.0 would be associated with catalytic action more than adsorption on the SW-Mn material. These results suggest that the carbocatalytic process can be applied using different initial pH values, achieving high degradations of CIP.

On the other hand, the performance of the sonocarbocatalytic process for the degradation of CIP in a real complex matrix was assessed. When the irrigation crop water sample was characterized, it was intrinsically found $0 . 1 4 ~ \mu \mathrm { g } ~ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ of CIP (see Text S1.5); thus, to make it comparable to the conditions in distilled water, the irrigation crop water was spiked with CIP to achieve a total CIP concentration of $0 . 0 3 0 6 \mathrm { m M } )$ . It can be mentioned that the real matrix contained organic matter (measured as TOC) and several ions such as bicarbonate $\mathrm { ( H C O _ { 3 } ^ { - } }$ , alkalinity) and chloride ions $\left( \mathbf { C l } ^ { - } \right)$ (Table 2). Fig. 8c compares the degradation of CIP in distilled water and the complex matrix.

The CIP removal rate strongly increased $( 1 0 0 \%$ degradation after only $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of treatment) in the complex matrix when compared to the elimination in distilled water. The accelerating effect can be explained by the presence of chloride ions in the real matrix (Table 2). Chloride ions, in systems involving PMS, accelerate the elimination of organic contaminants [105]. The elimination improvement can be ascribed to the formación of HOCl $( \mathrm { E } ^ { \circ } = 1 . 4 8 \ : \mathrm { V }$ , Eq. 9), which can react directly with CIP [106,107]. Furthermore, HOCl can produce $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ from both, its self-decomposition (Eq. 10) and its reaction with PMS (Eq. 11) [105]. Furthermore, it must be considered that other anions such as ${ \mathrm { H C O } } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ and $\mathrm { H P O } _ { 4 } ^ { 2 - }$ , which can be present in the irrigation crop water are able to promote the activation of PMS or produce peroxoanions (such as $\mathrm { H C O } _ { 4 } ^ { - }$ and $\mathrm { H P O } _ { 5 } ^ { 2 - }$ , which are also oxidizing species ([84,108]), thus leading to a faster degradation of CIP. Indeed, the positive effects of bicarbonate anion and phosphate mono-acid anion by direct interaction with PMS have been previously reported in the literature [108]. Then, the extra formation of degrading species (i.e., hypochlorous acid, singlet oxygen, or peroxoanions) can also enhance the elimination of CIP in the irrigation crop water. The competition of other matrix components with CIP for the degrading species (such as hypochlorous acid, singlet oxygen, or peroxoanions) is low, owing to their high selectivity [109,110]. Besides, it is suggested for future works to perform in experiments using chloride, bicarbonate, or phosphate anions individually, to understand deeply the role of such matrix species.

After the sonocarbocatalytic treatment, the COD removal was $2 0 . 4 \%$ . The removal percentage for COD was not similar to that obtained in distilled water (Fig. S12), because the irrigation crop water contains other organic matter than CIP, and the other water components could be less prone to oxidation than CIP via sonocarbocatalysis, thus, contributing to a higher remaining amount of COD after the treatment of the complex water.

# 4. Conclusions

Sawdust was valorized to prepare SW-Mn, which effectively degraded ciprofloxacin in water through sonocarbocatalysis. A dual role was observed. The SW-Mn material activated peroxymonosulfate to $^ 1 0 _ { 2 }$ and PMS modified the material making Mn capable of oxidizing CIP. The coupling with low-frequency ultrasound $( 4 0 \mathrm { k H z } )$ synergistically eliminated CIP. The transformation occurred via piperazine ring opening, and this was linked to the decrease of antimicrobial activity and phytotoxicity, indicating that the resultant solution from sonocarbocatalytic treatment produced less problematic water than the initial one. Finally, the sonocarbocatalysis led to a better degradation of CIP in actual irrigation crop water than in distilled water.
